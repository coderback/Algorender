<Section step="hook" title="Running a matrix backwards">

You know where a matrix sends a vector: $A$ takes $x$ to $Ax$. Very often the question comes the other way round. You
know where something landed, $b$, and want to know where it started: find the $x$ with $Ax = b$. That is the same as
solving a set of equations, such as

$$
\begin{aligned}
4x_1 + 2x_2 &= 8 \\
2x_1 + 3x_2 + x_3 &= 7 \\
-2x_1 + x_2 + 3x_3 &= -3
\end{aligned}
$$

Written with a matrix, the numbers in front of the unknowns go into $A$, the unknowns into $x = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$, and the
right-hand sides into $b = (8, 7, -3)$:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Row 1 of $A$ dotted with $x$ is $4x_1 + 2x_2 + 0x_3$, the left side of the first equation, and each row works the same
way. So $Ax = b$ says all three equations at once.

Problems like this are everywhere: balancing a budget, mixing paints, and, at the end of this stage, finding the line
that best fits some data. This lesson answers three questions. When can a matrix be run backwards at all? How do you
undo a $2 \times 2$ matrix? And how do you solve equations of any size, step by step?

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="determinant det A">For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, $ad - bc$. Its size is how much $A$ scales every area; a minus sign means $A$ flips the plane over.</Term>
  <Term name="identity I">The matrix with 1s down its diagonal and 0s elsewhere. It changes nothing: $Ix = x$.</Term>
  <Term name="inverse A⁻¹">The matrix that undoes $A$, read “A inverse”: $A^{-1}A = I$. Not every matrix has one.</Term>
  <Term name="augmented matrix [A | b]">The equations written as a grid: each row holds one equation's numbers, with $b$ as an extra last column.</Term>
  <Term name="row operation">Swapping two rows, or subtracting a multiple of one row from another. It changes the equations but not their solution.</Term>
  <Term name="pivot">The entry used to clear the numbers below it, one per column, on the diagonal.</Term>
  <Term name="back substitution">Solving the last equation first, then putting its answer into the one above, and so on up.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="The determinant: what A does to area">

In the lesson on matrices moving space, the unit square turned into a parallelogram, and $\det A = ad - bc$ said how its
area changed. Here it is again, because it decides whether $A$ can be undone.

<Explore slug="inverse-matrix" settings={{ preset: 'area' }} input caption="A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. Watch the unit square become a parallelogram, and the shaded half square with it. Drag either tip." />

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\det A = 2 \times 2 - 1 \times 1 = 3
$$

The square of area 1 becomes a parallelogram of area 3, and the shaded triangle, half the square, goes from 0.5 to
1.5. Every shape's area is multiplied by the same number. Swap the columns, $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$,
and $\det A = -3$: the areas still triple, and the minus sign says the plane has been turned over.

<KeyIdea>

**$|\det A|$ is the factor $A$ scales every area by; a negative sign means the plane is flipped.** When $\det A = 0$, the
plane is squashed flat.

</KeyIdea>

Now squash it. Below, $A$ sends $î$ to $u = (2, 1)$ and $ĵ$ to half of $u$, $(1, 0.5)$: both columns lie on one line.

<Explore slug="inverse-matrix" settings={{ preset: 'squash' }} input caption="Columns (2, 1) and (1, 0.5) on one line. Watch where P, Q and S land, and whether anything lands on w = (0, 1)." />

The whole plane falls onto that line. Three different points, $P = (0.5, 1)$, $Q = (1, 0)$ and $S = (1.5, -1)$, all land
on the same spot, $(2, 1)$. To undo $A$, one matrix would have to send $(2, 1)$ back to $P$, $Q$ and $S$ all at once,
but a matrix sends each point to only one place. And a point off the line, such as $w = (0, 1)$, is never reached, so $Ax = w$ has no solution at all.

>

A matrix with $\det A = 0$ doesn't just make the arithmetic awkward. It throws information away: whole lines of points
land on one spot, and a whole region of the plane is never reached. So $Ax = b$ has either no solution or infinitely
many, never exactly one, and no inverse exists. Only $\det A \neq 0$ guarantees one answer for every $b$.

</Misconception>

When $\det A \neq 0$, nothing is squashed, and the move can be undone. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix there is a recipe: swap
$a$ and $d$, put a minus sign on $b$ and $c$, and divide everything by the determinant.

$$
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}
$$

<Explore slug="inverse-matrix" settings={{ preset: 'undo' }} input caption="A = [[2, 1], [2, 2]]. Work out det A, build A⁻¹ from the recipe, then watch î and ĵ after A, then A⁻¹." />

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $\det A = 2 \times 2 - 1 \times 2 = 2$, so

$$
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -0.5 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Check it with dot products. $A$ sends $î$ to its first column, $(2, 2)$. Each part of $A^{-1}(2, 2)$ is a row of
$A^{-1}$ dotted with $(2, 2)$: row 1 gives $1 \times 2 + (-0.5) \times 2 = 1$ and row 2 gives
$(-1) \times 2 + 1 \times 2 = 0$. So $A^{-1}$ sends $(2, 2)$ back to $(1, 0)$, and the same works for $ĵ$:
$A^{-1}A = I$. Areas undo too: $\det A^{-1} = 1 / \det A = 0.5$. That is the other reason $\det A = 0$ has no inverse:
there is no $1/0$.

>

With ordinary numbers, undoing “times 4” is “times 1/4”, so it is natural to flip each entry of a matrix. But every entry
of $A^{-1}$ depends on all four entries of $A$, because undoing a move of the whole plane has to untangle how $î$ and
$ĵ$ were mixed together. Check any guess by multiplying: $A^{-1}A$ must come out as $I$. Flip the entries of
$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ to get $\begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and multiply: the top-left
cell is $0.5 \times 2 + 1 \times 1 = 2$, not 1, so that is not the inverse.

</Misconception>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: solve three equations by elimination">

The recipe for $A^{-1}$ only works for $2 \times 2$ matrices. For any size there is a method you may have met at school
for two equations: subtract a multiple of one equation from another to get rid of an unknown. Written as a grid, the
three equations from the start are the augmented matrix

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
4 & 2 & 0 & 8 \\
2 & 3 & 1 & 7 \\
-2 & 1 & 3 & -3
\end{array}\right]
$$

**Elimination** works column by column. Each column's pivot sits on the diagonal (in column 1 here, the 4), and every
entry below it is cleared by subtracting a multiple of the pivot's row:

$$
\text{multiple} = \frac{\text{entry to clear}}{\text{pivot}}
$$

For example, with a pivot of 3 and a 6 below it, the multiple is $6 / 3 = 2$: subtract 2 times the pivot row, written
$R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$. The multiple can be negative: a $-6$ under a pivot of 3 gives $-2$, and subtracting
$-2R_1$ is the same as adding $2R_1$. The whole row changes, $b$ included, and the equations still have the same
solution. Once every entry under every pivot is 0, the last row has a single unknown,
and **back substitution** solves from the bottom up. Predict each multiple, then each unknown.

<PredictChallenge slug="inverse-matrix" settings={{ preset: 'eliminate' }} goal={5} />

The multiples were 0.5, −0.5 and 1 (subtracting $-0.5R_1$ means adding $0.5R_1$), and the answer is $x = (1, 2, -1)$. Check it against the original equations: each
row of $A$ dotted with $x$ gives back $b = (8, 7, -3)$.

**The determinant, from the pivots.** For a $3 \times 3$ matrix, $\det A$ is the factor it scales volumes by, as
$ad - bc$ was for areas. Subtracting a multiple of one row from another doesn't change it: it slides space along one
direction, like the shear in the lesson on matrices moving space, which keeps area (and volume) the same. Once
elimination has made a staircase of 0s, the determinant is just the product of the diagonal, the pivots:
$4 \times 2 \times 2 = 16$.

You have seen this already for $2 \times 2$. Eliminating $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ subtracts
$c/a$ times row 1 from row 2, leaving pivots $a$ and $d - bc/a$, and their product is $a(d - bc/a) = ad - bc$. For the
matrix you undid above, $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, the pivots are 2 and 1, whose product is 2,
the same as $\det A$.

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="When a pivot is 0, swap">

Elimination divides by each pivot, so a 0 on the diagonal is a problem. Here the first column clears without trouble,
but then a 0 lands in the pivot spot of column 2.

<Explore slug="inverse-matrix" settings={{ preset: 'pivot' }} caption="After two row operations, a 0 lands on the diagonal in column 2. Watch which row comes to the rescue." />

The fix is to swap in a row from below that has a non-zero entry in that column: swapping two equations doesn't
change their solution. The usual rule, called **partial pivoting**, always swaps up the row whose entry is biggest in
size, ignoring its sign, even when the pivot isn't 0. Computers keep only so many digits, so small rounding errors creep
into every division, and dividing by a big number keeps them small. Each swap flips the sign of the determinant, just as
swapping $î$ and $ĵ$ turns the plane over: here the pivots are 1, 1 and 1 with one swap, so $\det A = -1$.

      trap: 0,
      why: 'det = 0 × 1 − 2 × 1 = −2, not 0. Swap the rows and solve: x = (1, 2).',
    },
  ]}
>

A 0 in the pivot spot only says this row is in the wrong place. If any row below has a non-zero entry in that column,
swap it up and carry on. Trouble only comes when the whole column, from the pivot spot down, is 0: then there is no
pivot, $\det A = 0$, and there is no single solution.

</Misconception>

That last case looks like this. Here $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, whose second column is twice
its first, and $b = (3, 5)$.

<Explore slug="inverse-matrix" settings={{ preset: 'singular' }} caption="A’s second column is twice its first. Watch what elimination leaves in the last row." />

After one swap and one row operation the last row reads $0x_1 + 0x_2 = 0.5$: impossible, so there is no solution. Had
it read $0 = 0$, there would have been infinitely many.

Now write it yourself. `solve(A, b)` copies $A$ and $b$ into an augmented table, eliminates column by column, swapping
up the row whose entry is biggest in size each time, then back-substitutes. Code counts rows from 0, so $R_1$ is row 0
there. As it runs, every swap and row operation shows on the grid. You will reuse this `solve` at the end of the stage:
it is part 2 of the first project, least squares from scratch, which fits lines to data.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- $\det A = ad - bc$ for a $2 \times 2$ matrix: $|\det A|$ scales areas, a minus sign flips the plane.
- $\det A = 0$: the plane is squashed, there is no inverse, and $Ax = b$ has no solution or infinitely many.
- $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$, with $A^{-1}A = I$ and $\det A^{-1} = 1 / \det A$.
- Elimination: clear each column under its pivot with row operations (multiple = entry ÷ pivot), swapping up the row
  whose entry is biggest in size, then back-substitute from the last row up.
- $\det A$ scales volumes for a $3 \times 3$ matrix; it is the product of the pivots, with a minus sign for each swap.
- Next: **span, basis and rank**, which say exactly which $b$ a matrix can reach.

</Section>
