<Section step="hook" title="A grid of numbers that moves the whole plane">

A $2 \times 2$ matrix is four numbers. It is easy to learn the rule for multiplying it by a vector and never see what it
*does*. Here is what it does: it picks up the entire plane and moves it, stretching, turning, shearing or flipping it,
while keeping every grid line straight.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to look at a matrix like $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and
picture its effect before doing any arithmetic.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="î and ĵ">The basis vectors $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$: one step right, one step up. Every vector is built from them.</Term>
  <Term name="v = x·î + y·ĵ">A vector's coordinates are a recipe: $x$ steps along î, then $y$ steps along ĵ.</Term>
  <Term name="columns">The first column of a matrix is where î lands; the second is where ĵ lands.</Term>
  <Term name="det A">The determinant, $ad - bc$: how much the matrix scales areas, with a minus sign if it flips the plane over.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Watch a matrix move the grid">

<Explore slug="linear-transformations" settings={{ preset: 'stretch-shear' }} caption="First î lands on the first column, then ĵ on the second, then the whole grid follows. Step with → and read each line." />

Because every vector is $x$ steps along î plus $y$ steps along ĵ, it must land on $x$ steps along *where î went* plus
$y$ steps along *where ĵ went*:

$$
A\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = x \begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix}
$$

<KeyIdea>

**Read a matrix by its columns.** They are the images of î and ĵ, and those two arrows decide where everything else
goes. Grid lines stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced, and the origin stays put: that is what *linear* means.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Drag the columns yourself">

Drag the tips of î and ĵ (or focus one and use the arrow keys) and watch the grid, $Av$ and $\det A$ follow. Try these:

- Put ĵ on top of î's line. The plane collapses onto a line and $\det A = 0$.
- Swap the two arrows' places. The grid flips over and the determinant turns negative.
- Make a shear: leave î at $(1, 0)$ and move ĵ to $(1, 1)$. The square leans, but its area stays 1.

<Explore slug="linear-transformations" settings={{ preset: 'shear', step: '8' }} input />

<Misconception title="The rows tell you where î goes">

It is tempting to read the first *row* $(a, b)$ as the image of î. It isn't: $A\hat{\imath} = (a, c)$, the first
*column*. Rows are what you dot with $v$ when you compute by hand; columns are what the matrix does to space.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A matrix moves the whole plane; its **columns** are where î and ĵ land.
- $A(x\hat{\imath} + y\hat{\jmath}) = x(A\hat{\imath}) + y(A\hat{\jmath})$: knowing two arrows tells you everything.
- $\det A = ad - bc$ scales area; negative flips the plane, zero squashes it and can't be undone.
- Next: **eigenvectors**, the special directions a matrix only stretches.

</Section>
