<Section step="hook" title="Twenty questions, then many small fixes">

A doctor's checklist, a bank's loan rules, the game of twenty questions: each asks one simple question, then picks the
next question based on the answer. A **decision tree** learns such a checklist from data, and anyone can read the
result.

One tree is easy to understand but easy to fool. Add up hundreds of tiny trees, each fixing the mistakes of the ones
before, and you get **gradient boosting**, the method that wins most competitions on tabular data.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="split">A yes/no question about one feature, like $x \lt 0.4$. It cuts a region of the plane into two boxes.</Term>
  <Term name="Gini impurity">$1 - \sum_c p_c^2$ for the class shares $p_c$ in a region: 0 when it is pure, ½ for an even two-class mix.</Term>
  <Term name="leaf">A region that isn't split any further. It predicts its majority class.</Term>
  <Term name="residual">In boosting, what the model still gets wrong: $y - F(x)$.</Term>
  <Term name="stump">A tree with a single split: a step, with one value on each side.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Grow a tree on XOR">

<Explore slug="decision-tree" settings={{ preset: 'xor', n: 2 }} caption="Each region tries every question on x and on y and keeps the one whose halves are purest. The tree is drawn beside the plane." />

The tree is grown greedily. For a region it tries every threshold on each axis, scores each split by the Gini impurity
of the two halves (weighted by their sizes), and keeps the lowest. A single question can't separate XOR, since every
cut leaves both classes on both sides, but two levels of questions can: this tree gets 95% of the points right at depth 2.

Deeper isn't always better, though:

<Explore slug="decision-tree" settings={{ preset: 'circles', n: 5 }} caption="A round boundary built from boxes. At depth 5 the tree is perfect on its training points, with leaves that hold only one or two of them." />

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Boosting: fit what is left">

<Explore slug="gradient-boosting" settings={{ preset: 'step' }} caption="Each round fits a stump to the residuals (the bars) and adds a shrunk copy of it to the model F." />

Boosting starts from a flat guess, the mean of $y$. Each round it looks at the residuals, fits one stump to them, and
adds $\eta$ times that stump to the model:

$$
F \leftarrow F + \eta \cdot \text{stump}
$$

Each stump is weak, just one step, but together they build a staircase that follows the curve. Because each one is
fitted to the current errors, the training error can only fall.

<KeyIdea>

**Trees split greedily by purity; boosting adds trees that fix each other's mistakes.** A single deep tree memorises
its data. Many shallow trees, each nudging the model a little ($\eta \lt 1$) towards what is still wrong, generalise far
better. Fitting residuals is gradient descent, with whole functions as the steps.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

A tree deep enough to give every point its own leaf is always 100% right on the points it was grown on, and that says
nothing about new points. Judge a model on data it hasn't seen (a validation set), and limit depth, leaf size or the
number of boosting rounds to stop it memorising.

</Misconception>

  title="Boosting averages many independent trees"

      trap: 0,
      why: 'Each stump is fitted to the residual y − F(x) = 8 − 5 = 3, what the model still gets wrong, not to y itself.',

      q: 'How does gradient boosting combine its stumps?',
      choices: ['It averages stumps trained independently on random samples of the data', 'It keeps the single best stump', 'It adds them up, each scaled by η and fitted to what the earlier ones got wrong', 'It retrains one stump again and again on y'],
      answer: 2,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Averaging independent trees is bagging (random forests). Boosting builds its trees in sequence, each correcting the ones before, and adds them.',
    },
  ]}
>

Averaging trees that were each grown on their own sample is *bagging*, the idea behind random forests. Boosting is
different: each tree is fitted to the residuals the earlier trees left, so the trees depend on each other, and they are
added up (each scaled by $\eta$), not averaged.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A decision tree asks one question about one feature at a time; each split is the one with the lowest weighted Gini.
- Deep trees fit their training data perfectly and overfit; limit depth and judge on new data.
- Gradient boosting adds shrunk stumps fitted to the residuals, so the training error only falls.
- Small learning rates with more rounds are the usual recipe.

</Section>
