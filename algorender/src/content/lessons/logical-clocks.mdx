<Section step="hook" title="Which happened first?">

Two servers log events. Server A writes `balance = 50` at 10:00:00.120, and server B writes `balance = 80` at
10:00:00.115. Which write came last, and so wins?

The timestamps say A's. But each timestamp comes from that machine's own clock, and clocks drift. Even with NTP
keeping them in step, two machines can disagree by milliseconds across a data centre and by far more across the
internet, and a clock can jump backwards when it is corrected. If B's clock runs 10 ms slow, B's write really came
second. Physical time can't tell you.

In 1978 Leslie Lamport pointed out that you often don't need the real time at all. What matters is **cause and
effect**: could one event have influenced the other? That can be tracked with plain counters, called logical clocks.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="process">One machine or thread with its own sequence of events. Each gets a horizontal line on the diagram, with time running left to right.</Term>
  <Term name="event">Something a process does: a local step, sending a message, or receiving one. Drawn as a dot, named by process and position (b2 is B's second event).</Term>
  <Term name="happened before (→)">e → f if e comes before f on the same process, or e is the sending of a message that f receives, or there is a chain of these from e to f.</Term>
  <Term name="concurrent">Neither e → f nor f → e: no chain of messages links them, so neither could have affected the other, whatever the wall clocks say.</Term>
  <Term name="Lamport clock">One counter per process. Tick it before every event; a message carries the sender's count, and a receiver first jumps to the larger of its own count and the message's.</Term>
  <Term name="vector clock">One counter for every process, kept by every process. It records how many events of each process are in an event's past.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Stamping events with Lamport clocks">

Three processes, two messages: A tells B something, then B tells C. Step through and watch each event get its stamp.
Local events and sends tick the clock by one. A receive first takes the larger of the local clock and the stamp the
message carries, then ticks.

<Explore slug="logical-clocks" settings={{ preset: 'chain' }} caption="b2 receives m1, stamped 2, while B's own clock reads 1: B jumps to max(1, 2) + 1 = 3. Then C, at 1, receives m2 stamped 4 and jumps to 5." />

<KeyIdea>

**If e happened before f, then L(e) < L(f).** Along a process, every event ticks the clock up. Across a message, the
receiver jumps past the stamp it carries. So the counter grows along every chain of cause and effect: this is
Lamport's **clock condition**.

</KeyIdea>

The rule only works in one direction. Lamport clocks never put an effect before its cause, but a smaller timestamp
does not mean an event came first.

<Explore slug="logical-clocks" settings={{ preset: 'misleads' }} caption="a1 gets 1 and c2 gets 4, yet no chain of messages links them: they are concurrent. a3 and c1 both get 3, and the total order breaks the tie by process name." />

Sorting by (timestamp, process name) gives a **total order** that never contradicts →. Every process can work it
out from the stamps alone, so they all agree on one order of events. Lamport used it to build mutual exclusion
without a central lock server, and systems use it to agree on an order of writes. Between concurrent events, though,
the order it picks is arbitrary.

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Vector clocks: spotting concurrency">

A Lamport timestamp is one number, so it can't tell "happened before" from "concurrent": the pair (1, 4) above looks
just like a real cause and effect. **Vector clocks** (Fidge and Mattern, independently, in 1988) fix this by keeping
one counter per process:

- **A local event or a send:** add one to your own entry. A message carries the sender's whole vector.
- **A receive:** take the larger of each entry, yours or the message's, then add one to your own entry.

Entry $i$ of an event's vector counts the events of process $i$ in its past, including itself if it is on process $i$.
To compare, $V(e) < V(f)$ means every entry of $V(e)$ is $\le$ the matching entry of $V(f)$, and at least one is
smaller. Then **e → f exactly when V(e) < V(f)**, and two events are concurrent exactly when neither vector is smaller.

<Explore slug="logical-clocks" settings={{ preset: 'chain-vector' }} caption="b2 merges [0,1,0] with m1's [2,0,0] to [2,1,0], then ticks B's entry: [2,2,0]." />

<Explore slug="logical-clocks" settings={{ preset: 'misleads-vector' }} input caption="a1 is [1,0,0] and c2 is [0,2,2]: each is bigger somewhere, so they are concurrent. Type your own, e.g. vector; A: send m1, local; B: local, recv m1; C: local." />

The price is size: a vector has one entry per process, so it grows with the cluster. Amazon's Dynamo stored vector
clocks with each value so that, after a network split, it could tell an update that replaced another from two
concurrent updates that conflict and must both be kept. Riak's **version vectors** do the same job, with one entry
per replica rather than per client.

Lamport clocks promise only one direction: **if e → f then L(e) < L(f)**. The converse fails. In the second example,
a1 is stamped 1 and c2 is stamped 4, but no chain of messages links them, so they are concurrent. A smaller stamp tells
you only that the later-stamped event did not happen before the other. To know whether e → f, you need vector
clocks, or to follow the messages.

</Misconception>

A receive is an event of its own, so after taking the elementwise maximum, the process **ticks its own entry**. Skip
the tick and the receive's vector equals the merge of two earlier events. Then the next receive can look identical to
it, and the count of the process's own events is wrong.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Machines' clocks drift, so wall-clock times can't order events across machines. **Happened-before** (→) is what counts: same-process order, send before receive, and chains of these.
- **Lamport clocks:** tick before every event; on a receive, $L \leftarrow \max(L, t_m) + 1$. Then **e → f ⇒ L(e) < L(f)**, but **not the converse**.
- Ties broken by process give a **total order** everyone agrees on, consistent with →. It is used for mutual exclusion and for ordering writes.
- **Vector clocks:** one entry per process; tick your own entry, merge with an elementwise max on a receive (then tick). **e → f ⇔ V(e) < V(f)**, and incomparable vectors mean **concurrent**.
- Concurrent means **causally unrelated**, not simultaneous. Vector clocks and version vectors (Dynamo, Riak) use this to spot conflicting writes, at the cost of one entry per process or replica.

</Section>
