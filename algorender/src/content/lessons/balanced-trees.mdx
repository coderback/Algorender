<Section step="hook" title="Sorted keys, short tree">

The [binary search trees lesson](/learn/binary-search-trees) ended on its worst case: insert keys in sorted order and
each one goes right of the last, so the tree becomes a chain and a search walks past every key. Real data often arrives
sorted, so a plain search tree can't promise O(log n).

A **self-balancing tree** keeps that promise by repairing its shape as keys arrive. The **AVL tree**, published by
Adelson-Velsky and Landis in 1962, watches how much taller one side of each node is than the other. When an insert makes
one side two levels taller, a **rotation** lifts that side up: a few links change, the keys stay in order, and the seven
sorted keys that made a chain of 7 levels come out 3 levels tall.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="height">The number of levels in a subtree, as in the last lesson: a leaf has height 1 and an empty subtree 0. Each AVL node stores its own.</Term>
  <Term name="balance factor">A node's left height minus its right height: +1 means its left side is one level taller, −1 its right side, 0 level. (Some books subtract the other way round.)</Term>
  <Term name="AVL rule">On top of the search-tree rule, every node's balance factor is −1, 0 or +1. A node at +2 or −2 must be fixed.</Term>
  <Term name="rotation">Lifting one child of a node into the node's place and moving the node down to the other side. A few links change; the keys stay in order.</Term>
  <Term name="the four cases">Named by the path from the node at ±2 down its taller side: left-left and right-right are straight, left-right and right-left are bent.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Lift the taller side">

These are the seven keys that made a chain in the plain tree, inserted in the same order. To the right of each node are
its balance factor, above, and its height, below (h1, h2 …).

<Explore slug="avl-tree" settings={{ start: 'sorted' }} caption="10 to 70, inserted in sorted order: 3 levels, 40 at the root, and every balance factor 0. Search for 70: it is compared with 40, 60 and 70, three comparisons where the chain took seven." />

The repairs happen one insert at a time. In the smallest case, 30 has a left child, 20, and nothing on its right, so it
already leans left by one.

<Explore slug="avl-tree" settings={{ start: 'leaning' }} caption="Insert 10: it goes left at 30 and at 20, and becomes 20's left child. On the way back up, 20's height becomes 2 and its balance factor +1, which is allowed; 30's height becomes 3 and its balance factor +2, which is not. One right rotation lifts 20 into 30's place, with 10 and 30 as its children." />

Insert walks down as in a plain search tree. The new part is the walk back up the same path: each node recomputes its
height as 1 + its taller child's height, then its balance factor. Nodes off the path gained nothing, so they can't have
changed.

At 30 the path went left and left again, so 20 moved up and 30 moved down to its right. The keys still read 10, 20, 30
from left to right, and the subtree is 2 levels tall again, as before 10 arrived, so nothing above it changes.

<KeyIdea>

**An AVL tree keeps the two subtree heights of every node within 1 of each other. After an insert it walks back up the
path, updating heights, and lifts the taller side of the first node at ±2 with one or two rotations, which change a few
links and keep the keys in order.** The height stays O(log n), so search, insert and delete are O(log n) whatever order
the keys arrive in.

</KeyIdea>

That path was straight. If it bends, left and then right, one rotation is not enough.

<Explore slug="avl-tree" settings={{ start: 'leaning' }} caption="Insert 25: left at 30, then right at 20. 30 is at +2 again, but its left child 20 now leans right (−1). So 20 is rotated left first, which lifts 25 above it and straightens the path; then 30 is rotated right, and 25 takes 30's place with 20 and 30 as its children. Two rotations." />

So there are four cases. **Left-left** takes one right rotation and **right-right** one left rotation. **Left-right**
takes a left rotation at the child, then a right rotation at the node, and **right-left** is its mirror. In the
pseudo-code: if the node leans left (`bf > 1`) and its left child leans right (`bf(left) < 0`), straighten the child
first.

</Section>

Two links change inside the subtree, and the caller stores the returned node where 30 hung (here `root`): three links,
however big the subtrees below, so a rotation is O(1). The last line prints `10 < 20 < 30`: the order survives. Besides
the two nodes that trade places, only the lifted child's inner subtree changes parent, and its keys all lie between
those two. An AVL node also stores its height, so recompute the lowered node's, then the lifted node's. A left rotation
is the mirror image.

A rotation is local: it rewires a node, its lifted child and the link from above, and recomputes two heights. The
subtrees below come along whole, so it is O(1). An insert walks one path down and back up, O(log n) nodes, with at most
one rotation, single or double, because afterwards the subtree is as tall as it was before the insert.

</Misconception>

Why does a bent case need two rotations? Think back to the insert of 25.

>

A rotation moves the lifted child's inner subtree across to the other side. In a straight case the extra level is on
the outside and goes up with the child. In a bent case it is in the inner subtree, so it just changes sides: rotate 30
right while 20 leans right, and 20 ends up on top at −2, the same problem mirrored. So rotate the child first, which
lifts the middle key of the three, then the node, which lifts that key to the top.

</Misconception>

Delete works the same way: remove the key as in a plain search tree, then walk back up, updating heights and rotating
wherever a node reaches ±2.

<Explore slug="avl-tree" settings={{ start: 'balanced' }} caption="Delete 20, then 40, then 30. After the first two, every balance factor is still within ±1. The third leaves 50 with nothing on its left and two levels on its right: −2. Its right child, 70, is level (0), so one left rotation lifts 70 to the root, and 60, the key between 50 and 70, moves across to become 50's right child." />

One rotation was enough here, but after a delete the rotated subtree can end up a level shorter than before, which can
tip the node above it, and so on: at worst one rotation per level, still O(log n). The walk checks every node on the
path, because the rule covers every node.

<Misconception
  id="root-only-balance"
  title="A tree is balanced if its root is"
  probes={[

      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'The rule holds at every node. The left child’s subtrees differ by two levels, so it is at +2 and must be rotated, however level the root is.',
    },
    {
      q: 'After an insert, which nodes can an AVL tree find out of balance?',
      choices: ['Only the root', 'Any node on the path above the new leaf, up to the root', 'Only the new leaf’s parent'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Every node on the path gained a key below it, so any of them from the leaf’s grandparent up to the root can reach ±2 (the parent holds at most one key on each side). The walk back up checks each one.',
    },
  ]}
>

The AVL rule is about **every** node: the two subtree heights of each one differ by at most 1. A level root says
nothing about the nodes below it. A tree that kept only its root level could hang a chain of n/2 keys on each side, and
its height would be O(n). Only the rule at every node bounds the height, so insert and delete check each node on the
path back up, and code that checks a tree must look at every node.

</Misconception>

Within 1 is enough: the fewest keys an AVL tree of a given height can hold grow like the Fibonacci numbers, so its
height stays below about $1.44 \log_2 n$. A million keys take at most 28 levels; no binary tree fits them in fewer than
20, and the sorted chain took a million. **Red-black trees**, behind Java's TreeMap and the usual C++ std::map, balance
more loosely (height up to about $2 \log_2 n$) but need at most three rotations per insert or delete: cheaper updates,
slightly slower lookups.

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- An AVL tree is a binary search tree whose every node has a balance factor (left height minus right height) of −1, 0
  or +1.
- Insert and delete work as in a plain search tree, then walk back up updating heights and rotating at any node at ±2.
  Straight cases need one rotation; bent cases rotate the child first.
- A rotation rewires three links and keeps the keys in order: O(1). An insert needs at most one single or double
  rotation; a delete may need one per level.
- The height stays below about 1.44 log₂ n, so search, insert and delete are O(log n) in the worst case, whatever the
  order of the keys.
- Next: [B-trees](/learn/b-trees), which pack many keys into each node (try the [B-tree visualiser](/visualisers/b-tree)), and
  [storage engines](/learn/storage-engines), where a variant, the B+ tree, holds database indexes.
  Practise with [Check a Binary Tree Is Balanced](/practice/balanced-binary-tree) and
  [Depth of a Binary Tree](/practice/maximum-depth-of-binary-tree), or open the [AVL tree visualiser](/visualisers/avl-tree).
