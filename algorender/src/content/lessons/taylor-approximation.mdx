<Section step="hook" title="How does a calculator know eˣ?">

Press $e^x$ on a calculator and the answer appears at once, yet the chip inside can only add, subtract, multiply and
divide. Somewhere the curve has been swapped for something built from those four. The trick: close to a point, a
smooth curve looks almost exactly like a straight line, and even more like a parabola, and both need only adding and
multiplying.

Training a model leans on the same trick. A loss is a complicated curve, but an optimiser (the code that adjusts the
weights) standing at one setting of a weight only knows what is nearby: the height, the slope and how the ground
bends. Every step it takes is a bet on a simple shape that copies some of those. This lesson measures how good the line and the parabola are, how fast they get better
as you look closer, and where they let you down.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="flat line T₀">$T_0(x) = f(a)$: the simplest guess near the point $P = (a, f(a))$. It keeps only the height.</Term>
  <Term name="tangent T₁">$T_1(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$: the line through $P$ with the curve's slope there, as in the derivatives lesson.</Term>
  <Term name="second derivative f″">The derivative of $f'$, said “f double-prime”: the slope of the slope. It says how fast the slope changes, which is how much the curve bends. Positive means it bends up, like a bowl; negative, down.</Term>
  <Term name="parabola T₂">The tangent plus a squared term that copies the bend at $a$ as well. $T_0$, $T_1$ and $T_2$ are called **Taylor approximations**.</Term>
  <Term name="gap d and miss">Look at $x = a + d$, a gap $d$ from $a$. A guess's **miss** there is how far it is from the curve, up or down.</Term>
  <Term name="Newton's method">A way to find where a curve crosses zero: replace the curve by its tangent at a guess, move to where the tangent crosses zero, and repeat.</Term>
  <Term name="step size η">Gradient descent moves a weight $w$ to $w - \eta \cdot L'(w)$, where $L$ is the loss and $\eta$ (“eta”) is a small number you choose (you met it in [derivatives](/learn/derivatives-gradients)).</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="A line, then a parabola">

$P$ sits on $f(x) = e^x$ at $a = 0$, so $P = (0, 1)$. Since $e^x$ is its own derivative, the slope there is $f'(0) = 1$.
How well can simple shapes copy the curve near $P$?

<Explore slug="taylor" settings={{ preset: 'fits' }} caption="eˣ near P: a flat line, the tangent, the slopes either side of P, the parabola, then how much each one misses as the gap halves. Drag P along the curve." />

Judge each guess at $x = 0.5$, a gap $d = 0.5$ to the right of $P$, where $e^{0.5} \approx 1.6487$:

- The flat line $T_0(x) = 1$ keeps only the height. It misses the curve by 0.6487.
- The tangent $T_1(x) = 1 + x$ copies the slope too. It says 1.5 and misses by only 0.1487.

The tangent still misses, because the curve's slope keeps changing: it is 0.607 at $x = -0.5$, 1 at $P$ and 1.649
at $x = 0.5$. How fast the slope changes is how much the curve bends, and you measure it the usual way, with a
derivative: the derivative of $f'$ is the **second derivative** $f''$. Here $f'(x) = e^x$, whose derivative is $e^x$
again, so $f''(0) = 1$. It is positive: the slope grows, and the curve bends up away from its tangent.

A straight line can't bend, but a parabola can. Add a squared term to the tangent:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_2(x) = {} & f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) \\
& + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(a)(x - a)^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Why the $\tfrac{1}{2}$? The slope of $\tfrac{1}{2} c\,(x - a)^2$ is $c\,(x - a)$ (expand:
$\tfrac{1}{2} c\,(x^2 - 2ax + a^2)$ has slope $\tfrac{1}{2} c\,(2x - 2a) = c\,(x - a)$). The term and its slope are
both 0 at $a$, so the tangent's height and slope are kept. The slope of $c\,(x - a)$ is $c$, so the term's bend is
exactly $c$. With $c = f''(a)$, the parabola bends just as the curve does at $P$. For $e^x$ at 0 that is
$T_2(x) = 1 + x + \tfrac{1}{2}x^2$. At $x = 0.5$ the new term adds $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1 \times 0.5^2 = 0.125$, so the
parabola says 1.625 and misses by just 0.0237.

Now move closer. Here are the misses at $d$ = 0.5, 0.25 and 0.125:

- flat line: 0.6487, then 0.284, then 0.1331 (each about 2 times smaller: ÷2.3, then ÷2.1)
- tangent: 0.1487, then 0.034, then 0.0081 (about 4 times: ÷4.4, then ÷4.2)
- parabola: 0.0237, then 0.0028, then 0.00034 (about 8 times: ÷8.5, then ÷8.3)

Halving $d$ halves $d$, quarters $d^2$ and divides $d^3$ by 8. The misses shrink like $d$, $d^2$ and $d^3$, because
each guess misses by roughly the first thing it leaves out. The flat line leaves out the slope term $f'(a)\,d$, which
at $d = 0.5$ is 0.5, against its real miss of 0.6487. The tangent leaves out the bend term
$\tfrac{1}{2} f''(a)\,d^2$, which at $d = 0.5$ is 0.125, against its real miss of 0.1487. The parabola leaves out
something smaller still, which shrinks like $d^3$. A calculator does the same with a few more terms on a tiny gap, so
the miss falls below its last digit.

<KeyIdea>

**Near a point, a smooth curve is almost its tangent, and closer still its parabola:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(a + d) \approx{} & f(a) + f'(a)\,d \\
& + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(a)\,d^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Each term copies one more thing about $f$ at $a$ (height, slope, bend), and makes the miss shrink one power of $d$
faster: halve $d$ and the misses fall about 2, 4 and 8 times.

</KeyIdea>

Only the flat line's miss halves when the gap halves, because only it leaves out a term that grows like $d$. The
tangent already has the slope right, so what it misses is the bend, which grows like $d^2$: half the gap, a quarter of
the miss. That is why a tangent is such a good guess close up. Walk half as far from $a$ and it is four times as
accurate, and the parabola eight times.

</Misconception>

The rules about $d$, $d^2$ and $d^3$ are promises about small gaps only. Go to $x = -2$, far from $P$, where $e^{-2}$ is

1.135: worse than the flat line. The parabola says 1 and misses by 0.865 again. A Taylor approximation is a local copy.
To estimate the curve far away, take a point $a$ close to where you need it.

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: Newton's method finds √2">

$\sqrt{2}$ is where $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ crosses zero. Finding where a curve crosses zero is hard, but finding where a
straight line does is easy. So at a guess $x$, swap the curve for its tangent and move to where the tangent crosses
zero. At a point $t$ the tangent's height is $f(x) + f'(x)(t - x)$. It is 0 at the new guess, so
$f(x) + f'(x)(x_{\text{new}} - x) = 0$, which gives:

$$
x_{\text{new}} = x - \frac{f(x)}{f'(x)}
$$

For $x^2 - 2$ the slope is $f'(x) = 2x$, so each step is $x - (x^2 - 2)/(2x)$. Then step again from the new guess, and
stop once a step moves $x$ by less than $10^{-12}$, a millionth of a millionth. A guess's **correct decimal places** are
the digits after the point that agree with $\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\ldots$: 1.4139 agrees as far as 1.41, so it has 2.
Start from $x_0 = 3$. Predict where the first tangent lands, the next guess, how the number of correct decimal places
grows, and what happens if you forget the 2 in $f'(x) = 2x$.

<PredictChallenge slug="taylor" settings={{ preset: 'newton' }} goal={6} />

At 3, $f(3) = 7$ and the slope is $f'(3) = 6$, so $x_1 = 3 - 7/6 = 1.833$. That stops short of $\sqrt{2}$: the curve
bends up, so its tangent runs below it and reaches zero first. The next guesses, cut off one digit after they stop
agreeing with $\sqrt{2}$, are $x_2 = 1.46\ldots$, $x_3 = 1.4149\ldots$, $x_4 = 1.4142137\ldots$ and
$x_5 = 1.4142135623731\ldots$. The correct decimal places went
0, 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, 15, and the run stopped after 6 steps.

Far away progress is slow, but once close, each step roughly doubles the correct digits. The reason is the tangent's
$d^2$ miss from the last section. If a guess is off by $d$, the tangent misses the curve at $\sqrt{2}$ by about
$\tfrac{1}{2} f''\,d^2 = d^2$ (here $f'' = 2$), and dividing by the slope $2x$ turns that height into a distance along
the axis. So the new guess is off by about $d^2 \div 2x$. Here $x_3$ is off by 0.000785, and
$0.000785^2 \div 2.83 \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-7}$, which is how far off $x_4$ is. Squaring a small number doubles its
zeros after the point.

Forget the 2 and the slope is $x$, a line too shallow: $x - (x^2 - 2)/x$ is just $2/x$. From 3 that goes to 0.667,
then back to 3, and it bounces between the two forever. If a Newton loop never settles, check the derivative first.

can throw a guess far off, and a tangent with slope 0 never crosses zero at all. Newton's method is fast once it is
close, not from anywhere.

<Section step="do" title="Square roots in code">

Now write it. `newton_sqrt(a)` starts from the guess $x = a$, steps to $x - (x^2 - a)/(2x)$ again and again, keeps
every guess in `steps`, and stops once a step is at most $10^{-12}$ times $x$ (times $x$, so the test scales with the
size of the answer). One example is $\sqrt{1{,}000{,}000}$, starting from a guess of 1,000,000, a thousand times too
big. The first nine steps only roughly halve it, down to about 2121: when $x^2$ is much bigger than $a$,
$x - (x^2 - a)/2x$ is about $x - x/2$. Far from the root the tangent is a poor copy of the curve. Once close, the
digits start doubling, and the run is done after 15 steps.

<Section step="see" title="The parabola behind a training step">

Back to training. This loss is lowest at $w = 2$:

$$
L(w) = (w - 2)^2 + \tfrac{1}{4}(w - 2)^4
$$

A sideways shift doesn't change slopes, so $(w - 2)^n$ has slope $n(w - 2)^{n-1}$, just as $w^n$ has $nw^{n-1}$.
So its slope is $L'(w) = 2(w - 2) + (w - 2)^3$ and its bend is $L''(w) = 2 + 3(w - 2)^2$. An optimiser standing at
$w_0 = 4$ finds $L'(4) = 12$ and $L''(4) = 14$. Where should it step?

<Explore slug="taylor" settings={{ preset: 'step' }} caption="A loss lowest at w = 2, starting from w₀ = 4: the tangent, the gradient step, the Newton step, then three more steps of each. Drag w₀ to start elsewhere." />

The tangent only says which way: uphill to the right, so go left. Gradient descent with $\eta = 0.1$ moves to
$w_1 = 4 - 0.1 \times 12 = 2.8$. That step is the lowest point of a parabola. Take the parabola with $L$'s height and
slope at $w_0$ and a bend of $1/\eta = 10$, as a function of the step $d$:

$$
q(d) = L(w_0) + L'(w_0)\,d + \frac{d^2}{2\eta}
$$

Its slope is $L'(w_0) + d/\eta$, which is 0 at $d = -\eta\,L'(w_0)$: exactly the gradient step. So gradient descent
quietly assumes the loss bends by $1/\eta$. A small $\eta$ assumes a sharp bend, and takes a small step.

The Taylor parabola uses the real bend, $\tfrac{1}{2} L''(w_0)\,d^2$ in place of $d^2/2\eta$. Its slope
$L'(w_0) + L''(w_0)\,d$ is 0 at $d = -L'(w_0)/L''(w_0)$, which gives the **Newton step**
$w_1 = 4 - 12/14 = 3.143$. It is Newton's method again, aimed at the zero of the slope $L'$.

Which lands closer to 2? Here the gradient step, 0.8 away against 1.143. At 4 the loss bends hard ($L'' = 14$) but
much less on the way down (only 2 at the bottom), so the parabola fitted at 4 turns up too soon. Closer in, the
parabola fits better and better. After 2, 3 and 4 steps, gradient descent is 0.589, 0.451 and 0.351 from the minimum,
losing only about a quarter of the distance each step; Newton is 0.504, 0.093 and 0.0008. A Newton step is only as
good as the parabola, and each one costs more, since $L''$ has to be worked out as well as $L'$.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- The tangent $f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$ copies the height and slope at $a$; adding $\tfrac{1}{2} f''(a)(x - a)^2$ copies the bend too. $f''$ is the slope of the slope.
- Near $a$ the flat line, tangent and parabola miss by amounts that shrink like $d$, $d^2$ and $d^3$: halve the gap and they fall about 2, 4 and 8 times. Far from $a$ there is no such promise.
- Newton's method repeats $x \leftarrow x - f(x)/f'(x)$. Once close, each step about doubles the correct digits; from a poor start it can overshoot, and a wrong derivative can stop it settling.
- A gradient step is the bottom of a parabola with bend $1/\eta$; the Newton step $w - L'(w)/L''(w)$ is the bottom of the Taylor parabola. Which lands closer depends on how well that parabola fits.

</Section>
