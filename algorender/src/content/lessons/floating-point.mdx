<Section step="hook" title="Why 0.1 + 0.2 isn’t 0.3">

Type `0.1 + 0.2` into Python or a browser console and you get `0.30000000000000004`. Nothing is broken: computers
store real numbers in a fixed number of bits, so most decimals can only be stored approximately, and the tiny errors
show up when you add them.

Once you can read the bits, the surprises become predictable: which numbers are exact, how big the error can be, and why
you should never compare two floats with `==`.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="sign bit">Bit 31: 0 for positive, 1 for negative.</Term>
  <Term name="exponent">Bits 30–23, an integer $e$ from 0 to 255. The power of two is $e - 127$ (the bias makes negative powers possible).</Term>
  <Term name="fraction">Bits 22–0: the binary digits after the point. A hidden 1 sits in front, so a float32 has 24 significant bits.</Term>
  <Term name="gap (ulp)">The distance to the next float. Nothing in between can be stored, so a number is rounded to the nearest float.</Term>
</Terms>

A float32 is scientific notation in base 2:

$$
x = (-1)^s \times 1.f \times 2^{\,e - 127}
$$

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Take 0.1 apart">

<Explore slug="floating-point" settings={{ preset: 'tenth' }} caption="Read the sign, then the exponent, then the fraction, and compare what is stored with what you asked for." />

In binary, $0.1$ is $0.000110011001100\ldots$, repeating forever, just as $1/3$ is $0.333\ldots$ in decimal. A fraction
has a finite binary form only if its denominator is a power of two, and $10 = 2 \times 5$ isn't. So the 23 fraction bits
cut the pattern off, and the stored value is the nearest float: $0.100000001490116\ldots$

Larger numbers have larger gaps. Above $2^{24}$ the gap is 2, so even a whole number can fall between floats:

<Explore slug="floating-point" settings={{ preset: 'big-int' }} caption="16 777 217 = 2²⁴ + 1 has no float32 of its own: its neighbours are 16 777 216 and 16 777 218." />

<KeyIdea>

**Floats are spaced evenly in relative terms, not absolute ones.** The gap next to $x$ is about $2^{-23} \approx 1.2
\times 10^{-7}$ times $x$: tiny near 0, enormous near $10^{30}$. That is why a float32 keeps about 7 significant decimal
digits wherever the number sits, and a float64 (52 fraction bits) about 16.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Try the edges">

Type your own numbers into the box, or pick an example:

- **0.1 + 0.2:** the sum is done in float64 first, giving $0.30000000000000004$, and then rounded to float32. That
  float64 error is far smaller than a float32 gap, so it lands on the very same float32 as $0.3$. In float64 the two
  differ by one gap, which is the famous result. Whether two computed values compare equal depends on the precision and
  the rounding, not on the maths.
- **1e-40:** too small for a normal float. The exponent bits are all 0, the hidden 1 disappears, and precision fades
  gradually (a *subnormal*).
- **Infinity, NaN and −0:** special patterns with an all-ones or all-zeros exponent.

Printing rounds to a handful of digits, so two different floats can print identically, and two calculations of the
"same" value can differ in the last bit. Compare with a tolerance instead: $|a - b| \le \varepsilon \cdot
\max(|a|, |b|)$, with $\varepsilon$ a little above the precision you need. NaN is the extreme case: it isn't even equal
to itself.

</Misconception>

about $10^{-7}$; above $2^{24}$ even whole numbers start to be skipped. Precision is *relative*, about 7 significant
digits, not a fixed number of decimal places.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A float32 is a sign, a power of two ($e - 127$) and 24 significant bits (one of them hidden).
- Most decimals, like 0.1, repeat forever in binary and are rounded to the nearest float.
- The gap between floats grows with the number: about 7 significant digits in float32, 16 in float64.
- Never compare floats with `==`; use a tolerance. Subnormals, ±0, ∞ and NaN cover the edges.

</Section>
