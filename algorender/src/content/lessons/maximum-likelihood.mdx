<Section step="hook" title="Which coin explains the flips?">

A coin is flipped 10 times and lands heads 7 times. What is your best guess for its chance of heads? Almost everyone
says 0.7, and maximum likelihood agrees: 0.7 is the best single guess, though ten flips can't show it is certainly the
true value. But "the share of heads" is a rule for coins only. What is the rule behind it, the one that also says how
to fit a bell curve to measurements, a line to points, or a model to a million photos?

It is called **maximum likelihood**: of all the settings a model could have, pick the one that makes the data you
actually saw as probable as possible. This lesson builds it from the coin up. On the way you will see why programs
add logs instead of multiplying chances, and why machine learning trains models on two particular losses, **squared
error** and **cross-entropy**: both are maximum likelihood in disguise.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="model and parameter">A model is a rule that gives data a chance; its parameter is the setting you can change. For a coin, the model is "each flip is heads with chance $p$, on its own", and $p$ is the parameter.</Term>
  <Term name="likelihood L(p)">The chance (for measurements, the density) the model gives the data you saw, worked out for each value of the parameter: a function of $p$, with the data held fixed.</Term>
  <Term name="maximum-likelihood estimate">The parameter value where $L$ is biggest: the setting that makes the data most probable. Written $\hat{p}$ ("p hat").</Term>
  <Term name="log-likelihood ln L">The natural log of the likelihood, from [exponents and logarithms](/learn/exponents-logs). A product of chances becomes a sum of logs.</Term>
  <Term name="density">For a measurement that can be any number, a model gives a height on a curve instead of a chance: a chance per unit of width. The chance of landing in a narrow strip is about the height times the strip's width.</Term>
  <Term name="normal bell">The density $f(x) = e^{-(x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} / (\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$, with centre $\mu$ ("mu") and spread $\sigma$ ("sigma"). It is tallest at $\mu$ and falls away on both sides.</Term>
  <Term name="noise">A random nudge up or down, different for every point, that moves data off the line a model predicts.</Term>
  <Term name="residual">How far a point sits above or below a line: $r = y - \text{line}(x)$.</Term>
  <Term name="cross-entropy">For yes/no data, minus the average log of the chance the model gives each outcome. Smaller is better.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="The likelihood of 7 heads in 10 flips">

Suppose the coin's chance of heads is $p$. The flips are independent, so, as with "and" in
[chance and events](/learn/probability-basics), the chance of the whole run is the chance of each flip multiplied
together: $p$ for each of the 7 heads and $1 - p$ for each of the 3 tails.

$$
L(p) = p^7 \, (1 - p)^3
$$

That is the **likelihood**: the chance of exactly these flips, worked out for each guess $p$. Step through it, and
drag the guess $p$ along the bottom:

<Explore slug="maximum-likelihood" settings={{ preset: 'coin' }} caption="Ten flips with 7 heads. The likelihood L(p) for each chance of heads p, then the same curve after taking logs, the slopes, and the cross-entropy. Drag your guess p." />

If $p = 0.5$, every flip has chance 0.5, so $L(0.5) = 0.5^{10} = 0.000977$. If $p = 0.7$,
$L(0.7) = 0.7^7 \times 0.3^3 = 0.00222$, more than twice as likely. The curve peaks at $p = 0.7$, the share of heads:
no other chance of heads makes these flips as likely. That is the **maximum-likelihood estimate**, $\hat{p} = 7/10$.
The everyday answer now has a reason behind it.

Why the chance of the run itself, and not of "7 heads somewhere in 10"? That chance, as in
[counting](/learn/counting-combinatorics), is $C(10, 7) = 120$ times bigger, because 120 orders of the flips have 7
heads: 0.117 at $p = 0.5$. But the 120 is the same for every $p$. It stretches the whole curve upwards and leaves its
peak where it is, so either way the best $p$ is 0.7.

Notice what $L$ is not. Each $L(p)$ is a chance of the *data*, once $p$ is fixed. It is not the chance that $p$ is the
coin's true value. Add up the heights at $p = 0.1, 0.2, \ldots, 0.9$ and you get 0.00758; for a single flip that
landed heads, $L(p) = p$, and the same nine heights add up to 4.5. The heights can add up to anything, because they
are not chances of different outcomes: a likelihood compares guesses, it doesn't share out a total of 1 among them.

**The product underflows.** Ten flips give $L = 0.00222$, already small. Now take 3,500 heads in 5,000 flips, the same
share. Every extra flip multiplies $L$ by a number below 1, and $0.7^{3500} \times 0.3^{1500}$ has more than 1,300 zeros
after the decimal point. The smallest number a float can hold has about 320, so a computer multiplying it out gets
exactly 0. Every guess $p$ then scores 0, and nothing can be compared.

The cure is the rule from the exponents lesson, $\ln(a \times b) = \ln a + \ln b$. The log of a product is the sum of
the logs, so each head adds $\ln p$ and each tail adds $\ln(1 - p)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln L(p) &= 7 \ln p + 3 \ln(1 - p)
\end{aligned}
$$

For the 5,000 flips that is $3500 \ln 0.7 + 1500 \ln 0.3 = -3054.3$: an ordinary number, which a float holds easily.
For the 10 flips, $\ln L(0.5) = -6.931$ and $\ln L(0.7) = -6.109$. Every log is negative, because every chance is
below 1.

Taking logs doesn't move the peak. $\ln$ only ever grows as its input grows, so the bigger of two likelihoods always
has the bigger log, and the $p$ with the biggest $L$ is the $p$ with the biggest $\ln L$. The log curve is a different
shape, but it peaks at 0.7 too.

At a peak a curve levels off, so its slope is 0, as in [derivatives and gradients](/learn/derivatives-gradients).
Measured with a small gap either side, the slope of $\ln L$ is 8 at $p = 0.5$, uphill towards 0.7, and 0 at
$p = 0.7$. Training a model is a search for the place where that slope is 0.

Last, divide $-\ln L$ by the number of flips:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\tfrac{1}{10}\ln L(p) &= -\big(0.7 \ln p \\
&\qquad + 0.3 \ln(1 - p)\big)
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the **cross-entropy** between the data's shares (0.7 heads, 0.3 tails) and the model's chances ($p$ and
$1 - p$). It is 0.611 at $p = 0.7$ and 0.693 at $p = 0.5$. Making the likelihood as big as possible is the same as
making the cross-entropy as small as possible. A classifier that answers yes or no, such as "is this email spam?",
gives each email its own chance of spam rather than one $p$ for all, but its loss is the same thing: minus the average
log of the chance it gave each email's true answer, and training makes that as small as possible.

<KeyIdea>

**The likelihood $L(\theta)$ is the chance (or, for measurements, the density) of the data you saw, for each setting
$\theta$ ("theta") of the model. The maximum-likelihood estimate is the setting that makes it biggest.** Work with
$\ln L$, a sum of logs that never underflows: it peaks at the same place, where its slope is 0.

</KeyIdea>

$L(p)$ is a chance of the **data**, worked out as if $p$ were the true value. Read across the $p$'s, it ranks the

$p$'s, so they don't add up to 1, and $L(0.7) = 0.00222$ doesn't mean "the chance that $p$ is 0.7 is 0.00222".

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: 1 head in 4 flips">

Now a coin that landed heads once in 4 flips, with your guess starting at $p = 0.4$. Predict each step before it
appears: the chance of these flips at your guess, the best $p$, whether the heights add up to 1, what happens to the
product for 5,000 flips, where the log curve peaks, its value and slope at your guess, and the cross-entropy at the
best $p$.

<PredictChallenge slug="maximum-likelihood" settings={{ preset: 'coin-few' }} goal={5} />

With $p = 0.4$ the four flips have chance $0.4 \times 0.6^3 = 0.0864$, and the curve $L(p) = p(1 - p)^3$ peaks at
$\hat{p} = 1/4 = 0.25$, where $L = 0.105$. The nine heights add up to 0.492, not 1. With 5,000 flips (1,250 heads),
the product underflows to 0 again, while the log is fine.

The log curve peaks at 0.25 as well. At your guess, $\ln L(0.4) = \ln 0.4 + 3 \ln 0.6 = -2.449$: one $\ln p$ for the
head and three $\ln(1 - p)$ for the tails. Its measured slope there is $-2.5$: downhill, because the peak is to the
left. Divided by the 4 flips, $-\ln L(0.25)$ gives a cross-entropy of 0.562.

With only 4 flips the curve is wide: the likelihood at $p = 0.1$ and at $p = 0.45$ is still about 70% of the peak's.
Fewer data means a flatter likelihood, and less certainty about $p$.

>

$\ln$ squashes big values and stretches small ones, so the log curve has a different shape. But it never changes
which of two numbers is bigger. So the parameter that wins on $L$ wins on $\ln L$, and the peak stays put: 0.7 for 7
heads in 10, 0.25 for 1 head in 4. That is why models can be trained on the log-likelihood with nothing lost.

</Misconception>

<Section step="see" title="A normal bell over measurements">

Measurements such as heights or times can be any number, so no single value has a chance of its own. Instead a model
gives each one a **density**, the height of a curve over it: a chance per unit of width, so the chance of landing in
a narrow strip is about the height times the strip's width. For measurements the likelihood multiplies densities
instead of chances. The strip widths are the same whatever the model's settings, so leaving them out doesn't move the
peak. A density is not a chance, though: a narrow curve can be taller than 1.

The commonest curve is the **normal bell**,

$$
f(x) = \frac{e^{-(x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
$$

with centre $\mu$ and spread $\sigma$. The power of $e$ is 0 at $x = \mu$ and more and more negative away from it, so
the bell is tallest at $\mu$. Dividing by $\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}$ makes the whole area under the bell 1, so a wider bell is
lower. Here are 8 measurements: 2.5, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5, 5.5, 7 and 10. Each one's density is a bar up to the bell, and
the likelihood is the bars multiplied together. Drag $\mu$ and $\mu + \sigma$ along the bottom:

<Explore slug="maximum-likelihood" settings={{ preset: 'gaussian' }} input caption="Eight measurements under a normal bell with centre μ = 4 and spread σ = 1.5: each one's density as a bar, then ln L as μ slides and as σ changes, and the best bell. Drag μ and μ + σ." />

With $\mu = 4$ and $\sigma = 1.5$, the bar at 4 is the tallest, 0.266, and the bar at 10 is only 0.0000892. Their
product is about $3 \times 10^{-10}$, so add logs again. The log of a density is the log of $1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$
plus the log of the power of $e$. For the first, $\ln a + \ln(1/a) = \ln(a \times 1/a) = \ln 1 = 0$, so
$\ln(1/a) = -\ln a$. For the second, $\ln(e^{b}) = b$. So

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln f(x) &= -\ln\big(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\big) \\
&\qquad - \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Added over the 8 measurements, $\ln L = -21.929$ for this bell.

**The best centre.** Only the squared gaps $(x - \mu)^2$ depend on $\mu$, and they come with a minus sign. So the best
$\mu$ is the one that makes $\sum (x_i - \mu)^2$ smallest. Expand one gap as $\mu^2 - 2x\mu + x^2$; by the power rule
its slope against $\mu$ is $2\mu - 2x$. Added over all $n$ measurements, the slope is $2n\mu - 2\sum x_i$, which is 0
when $\mu = \sum x_i / n$, the plain average. So the best $\mu$ is the **mean**, 5.25. It is not the median, 4.75,
nor halfway between the smallest and largest, 6.25. The squares weigh the far measurement, 10, heavily, and the mean
answers by moving towards it. With $\sigma$ kept at 1.5, $\ln L$ is $-19.151$ at the mean and $-19.595$ at the median.

**The best spread.** Put $\mu$ at the mean. The squared gaps from 5.25 add up to 38.5. Divide by $n = 8$, which gives
4.8125, then take the square root:

$$
\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = 2.194
$$

The visualiser's chart of $\ln L$ against $\sigma$ peaks exactly there. It is a familiar number: give each of the 8
measurements chance 1/8, and $\hat{\sigma}$ is the standard deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}$ from
[random variables](/learn/random-variables). Too small a $\sigma$ makes the bar at 10 almost 0, and its log hugely
negative; too big a $\sigma$ flattens every bar.

The *sample* standard deviation (the $s_{n-1}$ key on a calculator, or STDEV in a spreadsheet) divides by $n - 1$
instead and gives 2.345. It is a little bigger on purpose: measurements sit a little closer to their own mean than to
the bell's true centre, and dividing by $n - 1$ makes up for that on average. Maximum likelihood divides by $n$. The
best bell, $\mu = 5.25$ and $\sigma = 2.194$, has $\ln L = -17.636$, and no other normal bell does better.

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Squared error is maximum likelihood">

Points along a line work the same way. Suppose each $y$ is a line's height plus **noise**: a random nudge drawn from
a normal bell with centre 0 and a fixed spread $\sigma$. A point's residual $r$ is then its nudge, so its density is
the bell's height at $r$. Here are 6 points and a line through them, with each residual dashed; drag the line's ends:

<Explore slug="maximum-likelihood" settings={{ preset: 'squared-error' }} input caption="Six points and a line, with each point's residual dashed. Σ r² and ln L as the line moves up or down, then with wider noise, and the least-squares line. Drag the line's two ends." />

Each point's log density is $-\ln(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}) - r^2/(2\sigma^2)$, so with $n$ points

$$
\ln L = -n \ln\big(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\big) - \frac{\sum r_i^2}{2\sigma^2}
$$

The first part is the same for every line. So a line has a bigger likelihood exactly when its squared residuals add
up to less. For the line drawn, they add up to 2.929. With $\sigma = 1$, $\ln(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}) = 0.919$, so
$\ln L = -6 \times 0.919 - 2.929/2 = -6.978$.

Slide the line up or down and $\sum r^2$ is a bowl, lowest when the line moves up by 0.33, the average residual;
$\ln L$ is the same bowl turned upside down and halved, so its peak is right above. That is the best-$\mu$ result
again: moving the line up by $d$ fits a centre to the residuals, and the best centre is their mean. With wider noise,
$\sigma = 2$, the curve is flatter but peaks at the same place.

Let the slope move too, and the best line is the one with the smallest $\sum r^2$, called the **least-squares** line:
here with slope 0.914 and mean squared error (the average of the squared residuals) 0.367, whatever $\sigma$ is.
Under normal noise, minimising mean squared error *is* maximum likelihood.

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- The likelihood $L(\theta)$ is the chance (for measurements, the density) of the observed data under the model with parameter $\theta$, read as a function of $\theta$. Maximum likelihood picks the $\theta$ that makes it biggest.
- $L$ is a chance of the data, not of $\theta$: across values of $\theta$ it need not add up to 1.
- For $h$ heads in $n$ flips, $L(p) = p^h(1 - p)^{n - h}$ peaks at $\hat{p} = h/n$, the share of heads.
- Products of many chances underflow to 0, so use $\ln L$, a sum of logs. $\ln$ keeps the order of numbers, so the peak doesn't move, and at the peak the slope of $\ln L$ is 0.
- For a normal bell, the best $\mu$ is the mean and the best $\sigma$ is $\sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 / n}$, dividing by $n$, not the sample standard deviation's $n - 1$.
- Under normal noise, $\ln L = \text{constant} - \sum r_i^2/(2\sigma^2)$: maximising the likelihood is minimising squared error, whatever $\sigma$ is.
- For yes/no data, $-\ln L$ divided by the number of outcomes is the cross-entropy: minimising it is maximum likelihood too.

</Section>
