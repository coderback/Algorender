<Section step="hook" title="A line that says how sure it is">

Is this email spam? Will this customer cancel? A yes/no answer isn't enough: you want to know *how likely*, so you can
act differently at 51% and at 99%.

Logistic regression draws a straight line through the data and turns "which side, and how far" into a probability. It
is also a single neuron with a sigmoid, the building block that neural networks stack by the thousand.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="score">$z = w_1 x + w_2 y + b$: positive on one side of a line, negative on the other, larger further away.</Term>
  <Term name="sigmoid">$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$ squashes any score into a probability between 0 and 1, with $\sigma(0) = \tfrac12$.</Term>
  <Term name="decision boundary">Where $z = 0$, so $p = \tfrac12$: always a straight line.</Term>
  <Term name="cross-entropy loss">The average of $-\ln p$ of the true class: small when the model is confidently right, huge when it is confidently wrong.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Watch the line swing into place">

<Explore slug="logistic-regression" settings={{ preset: 'linear' }} caption="The shading is p(class 1) at every spot; the line is where p = ½. Each step moves the weights against the gradient of the loss." />

At the start every weight is 0, so $p = \tfrac12$ everywhere and the loss is $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$: a coin flip. The
gradient is remarkably simple:

$$
\nabla_{w} \text{loss} = \text{mean of } (p - \text{label}) \cdot x
$$

Each point pushes the weights by its own error $p - \text{label}$. A point the model already gets right with confidence
barely pushes; a point on the wrong side pushes hard. As the classes separate, the weights grow and the shading sharpens
from a gentle blur into a crisp edge.

<KeyIdea>

**Logistic regression is a straight line plus a confidence.** The boundary $p = \tfrac12$ is always the line $w \cdot x + b = 0$,
and $p$ depends only on the distance from it. Training minimises cross-entropy, the loss from the
[entropy lesson](/learn/entropy): the average surprise of the true labels under the model's probabilities.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Make it fail">

Pick the datasets one after another:

- **Overlapping:** no line separates every point, so the model hedges with $p$ near ½ in the middle.
- **XOR:** opposite corners share a class. The loss barely moves from $\ln 2$, and accuracy stays far from 100%.
- **Circles:** one class surrounds the other; any straight line cuts through both.

<Explore slug="logistic-regression" settings={{ preset: 'xor' }} input />

The last two aren't a training problem: more steps won't help, because no straight line fits. Curved boundaries need
new features, or hidden layers, which is where [neural networks](/learn/neural-networks) come in.

<Misconception title="Logistic regression predicts a number, like linear regression">

Despite the name, it is a classifier. The "regression" is on the score $z$, the log-odds $\ln\frac{p}{1-p}$, which is
a linear function of the inputs; the output you use is a probability, and the prediction is whichever side of ½ it
falls on.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- $p = \sigma(w \cdot x + b)$: a linear score squashed into a probability.
- The boundary $p = \tfrac12$ is always a straight line; confidence grows with distance from it.
- The loss is cross-entropy; its gradient is the mean of (p − label) times the input.
- Curved boundaries are out of reach without new features or hidden layers.

</Section>
