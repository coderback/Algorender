<Section step="hook" title="Hundreds of programs, a handful of cores">

Your laptop is running hundreds of threads right now: the browser, a music player, background updates, this page. It has
perhaps eight cores. Each core can run only one thread at a time, so something has to decide, many times a second, who
gets it next. That something is the **scheduler**.

The choice matters. Make a short job wait behind a long one and it feels sluggish. Hand out turns too eagerly and the core
spends its time switching instead of working. Every policy here is a different answer to one question: *who should run
now?*

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="process and burst">A job that needs the CPU. Its **burst** is how many units of CPU time it needs before it is done (or stops to wait for something else).</Term>
  <Term name="ready queue">The processes that have arrived and are waiting for the CPU. The scheduler picks from here.</Term>
  <Term name="preemption">Taking the CPU away from a running process before it finishes, because the rule says someone else should run.</Term>
  <Term name="quantum">Round robin’s turn length: a process runs for at most this long, then goes to the back of the queue.</Term>
  <Term name="waiting, turnaround, response">**Turnaround** is finish − arrival. **Waiting** is the part of that spent in the queue: turnaround − burst. **Response** is how long until it first runs.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Watch a convoy form">

First come, first served is the obvious rule: run jobs in the order they arrive, each to the end. Watch what happens when
the first job is a long one. On the Gantt chart, each row is a process: its pale bars are time spent waiting, its solid
bars are time on the CPU.

<Explore slug="cpu-scheduling" settings={{ preset: 'fcfs' }} caption="P1 needs 8 units and arrives first. P2, P3 and P4 need 6 units between them, yet each waits about 8. That queue behind one slow job is the convoy effect." />

<KeyIdea>

**Every policy does the same work; they differ in who waits.** The CPU never idles while a process is ready, so all of
them finish everything at the same moment. What changes is the order, and with it each process’s waiting time. Running
the short jobs first gives the lowest average wait, because a short job delays everyone behind it only a little.

</KeyIdea>

Now the same four jobs under **shortest remaining time first** (SRTF): whenever a job arrives with less work left than the
running one, it takes over the CPU.

<Explore slug="cpu-scheduling" settings={{ preset: 'srtf' }} caption="P2 arrives at t = 1 needing 2, while P1 still needs 7: P1 is preempted. The average wait drops from 5.75 under FCFS to 2." />

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Take turns: round robin">

Shortest-first is unfair to long jobs, and it needs to know the future. **Round robin** needs neither: processes take
turns of at most one quantum, and a process whose turn is up goes to the back of the queue. Try the same three jobs with a
quantum of 1 and of 4, and compare the context switches and when each job first runs.

<Explore slug="cpu-scheduling" settings={{ preset: 'rr-1' }} caption="Quantum 1: every job starts within a tick or two, but the CPU switches 11 times." />

<Explore slug="cpu-scheduling" settings={{ preset: 'rr-4' }} input caption="Quantum 4: far fewer switches, but P3 waits longer for its first turn. Type your own jobs, e.g. rr 2: 0-5, 0-3, 1-4, or try fcfs, sjf, srtf or priority." />

A quantum longer than every burst means no job is ever cut short: round robin becomes FCFS. The art is a quantum long
enough that switching is a small cost, short enough that nobody waits long for a turn.

  title="Round robin is always the fairest, best choice"

>

Round robin shares the CPU in turns, which keeps **response** times short: nobody waits long to start. But its average
**turnaround** can be the worst of all: with several similar jobs, they all finish near the end. And every turn costs a
context switch, time spent saving one process and loading another instead of working. Fair to everyone is not the same as
best for everyone.

</Misconception>

  id="sjf-knows"
  title="An operating system can simply run shortest job first"

      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'SJF and SRTF rank jobs by their burst lengths, and those aren’t known in advance. A real scheduler has to predict them from how the process behaved before.',
    },
    {
      q: 'A scheduler predicts each job’s next burst as the average of its last two. A job’s last bursts were 2 and 4, and its next one will really be 10. What length does the scheduler work with?',
      choices: ['10', '4', '3', '2'],
      answer: 2,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'It predicts (2 + 4) / 2 = 3, so it may run this job ahead of one that is really shorter. Shortest-first in practice is only as good as its predictions.',

SJF and SRTF need to know how long each burst will be, and an operating system can’t see the future. Real schedulers
**estimate** the next burst from the previous ones (often a weighted average that favours recent bursts), and an estimate
can be wrong. Shortest-first is the ideal the estimates aim at, and the benchmark other policies are measured against.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A scheduler picks which ready process runs, whenever the CPU frees up or (if it preempts) a better candidate arrives.
- **Turnaround** = finish − arrival, **waiting** = turnaround − burst, **response** = first run − arrival.
- **FCFS** is simple but forms convoys behind long jobs. **SJF/SRTF** minimise average waiting but need predicted burst
  lengths. **Round robin** keeps response times short at the cost of context switches; with a huge quantum it becomes
  FCFS. **Priority** can starve low priorities unless waiting jobs age upwards.
- Heaps meet schedulers here: SRTF and priority scheduling keep the ready queue in a **priority queue** keyed by remaining
  time or priority, so picking the next process costs $O(\log n)$ instead of a scan.

</Section>
