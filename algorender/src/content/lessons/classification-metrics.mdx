<Section step="hook" title="99% accurate, and useless">

A disease affects 1 person in 100. Someone builds a model that reads each patient's test results and says "healthy"
to everyone. It is right about 99 people in every 100: it is 99% accurate. It has also never found a single sick
person.

Accuracy, the share of answers that are right, sounds like the obvious way to judge a classifier, and when the two
classes are about equally common it is a fair start. When one class is rare it can mislead badly. It also hides a
choice every classifier makes: how sure must it be before it says "yes"? This lesson counts a classifier's answers
four ways instead of one, moves its threshold to see what each move trades, draws the ROC curve that sums up every
threshold at once, and then makes the positives rare to see which measures notice.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

A classifier such as [logistic regression](/learn/logistic-regression) doesn't answer yes or no. It gives each example
a **score** between 0 and 1, its probability that the example is positive, and something has to turn that score into
an answer.

<Terms>
  <Term name="positive and negative">The two classes. "Positive" is the one you are looking for (spam, a disease, fraud), not a good outcome.</Term>
  <Term name="threshold t">The cut-off: flag an example as positive when its score is $t$ or more.</Term>
  <Term name="TP, FP, FN, TN">The four kinds of answer. A true positive is flagged and really positive; a false positive is flagged but really negative (a false alarm); a false negative is not flagged but really positive (a miss); a true negative is not flagged and really negative.</Term>
  <Term name="confusion matrix">The four counts in a 2 × 2 table: one row for each truth, one column for each answer.</Term>
  <Term name="recall">$\frac{TP}{TP + FN}$: of the real positives, the share flagged. Also called the true positive rate.</Term>
  <Term name="false positive rate">$\frac{FP}{FP + TN}$: of the real negatives, the share flagged.</Term>
  <Term name="precision">$\frac{TP}{TP + FP}$: of the flagged examples, the share really positive.</Term>
  <Term name="accuracy">$\frac{TP + TN}{n}$: of all $n$ answers, the share that are right.</Term>
  <Term name="ROC curve and AUC">Recall plotted against the false positive rate, one point for every threshold, and the area under that curve.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Four kinds of answer">

A classifier has scored 80 emails: 40 real spam emails (the positives, drawn above the axis) and 40 real emails (the
negatives, below it). The scores overlap, as real scores do: some spam looks innocent, and some real email looks like
spam. Step on to the threshold, then drag its line and watch the counts.

<Explore slug="roc-curve" settings={{ preset: 'threshold', seed: 7 }} caption="Scores in bins of 0.05, positives above the axis and negatives below. The threshold line flags everything to its right; the confusion matrix and the rates follow it." />

At $t = 0.5$ everything right of the line is flagged. Above the axis, 36 spam emails are flagged (true positives) and 4
slip through (false negatives). Below it, 9 real emails are flagged (false positives) and 31 are left alone (true
negatives). That is 67 of the 80 answers right, an accuracy of 0.838. As a confusion matrix:

- Really spam: TP 36, FN 4.
- Really not spam: FP 9, TN 31.

Each measure is a share, a count divided by a total. What matters is which total.

- **Recall** divides by the real positives, the top row: $36/40 = 0.9$. The filter catches 90% of the spam.
- **The false positive rate** divides by the real negatives, the bottom row: $9/40 = 0.225$. It wrongly flags 22.5% of
  the real emails.
- **Precision** divides by the flagged emails, the first column: $36/45 = 0.8$. Of the emails in the spam folder, 80%
  are spam.

Recall and precision have the same 36 true positives on top, but they answer different questions. Recall asks: of the
spam, how much did we catch? Precision asks: of what we caught, how much was spam? A doctor choosing a screening test
cares about recall, because it says how many of the sick people the test will find. A patient who has just tested
positive cares about precision, because it says how likely they are to be sick given that result. That second question
is exactly the one [Bayes' rule](/learn/bayes) answers.

>

That is recall. Both have TP on top, but recall divides by the real positives, TP + FN, and precision divides by the
flagged examples, TP + FP. Here they are 0.9 and 0.8, but they can be far apart, and as the threshold moves they
usually move in opposite directions.

</Misconception>

Now raise the threshold to 0.7. The filter turns cautious: recall falls to 0.45 (18 of 40) and the false positive rate
to 0.025 (1 of 40). Raising the threshold never flags anything new. It only unflags the scores between the old
threshold and the new one, so TP and FP can only shrink or stay the same, and recall and the false positive rate can
never rise. That is the trade: a higher threshold means fewer false alarms and more misses, a lower one the reverse. At
$t = 0$ everything is flagged: recall is 1, the false positive rate is 1, and precision is just the share of spam among
all 80 emails, 0.5.

Precision usually rises with the threshold, because the flags that remain are the most confident ones: at 0.7 it is
$18/19 = 0.947$. But not always. Its top and its bottom both shrink as the threshold rises, and when the flags removed
were more often right than the flags kept, precision falls. In this run, raising the threshold from 0.48 to 0.49 unflags 3
spam emails that scored exactly 0.48, and no real ones, so precision drops from 0.796 to 0.783. The widget's precision
chart marks the drop.

>

Raising the threshold only guarantees that recall and the false positive rate don't rise. Precision is a share of the
flagged examples, and if the flags removed were more often right than the flags kept, it falls, as it did from 0.48
to 0.49 above.

</Misconception>

Which threshold is right? None in general: it depends on what each mistake costs. A missed tumour costs far more than
a second scan, so a screening test sits low and accepts many false alarms. A spam filter that hides a job offer does
more harm than one that lets a spam through, so it sits high. A logistic regression model has a natural-looking cut at
0.5, where it thinks positive and negative are equally likely, but that is only a default: it treats a miss and a false
alarm as equally bad.

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: the ROC curve">

Rather than pick one threshold, look at all of them at once. Sweep the threshold from 1 down to 0, and at each
threshold plot one point: its false positive rate across, its recall up. The points join into the **ROC curve**, a
name from radar engineering ("receiver operating characteristic"). The challenge uses a fresh set of 80 scores. Predict
where the curve starts, which way the point can move, where it lands after one step, the area under the dashed
diagonal of a classifier that guesses, and what share of (positive, negative) pairs the scores put in order.

<PredictChallenge slug="roc-curve" settings={{ preset: 'roc', seed: 1 }} goal={4} />

The curve starts at (0, 0), where nothing is flagged, and ends at (1, 1), where everything is. As the threshold falls
it can only move up, when a positive gets flagged, or right, when a negative does. At $t = 0.5$ it had reached
$(0.125, 0.875)$: recall 0.875 for a false positive rate of 0.125. A good classifier climbs steeply at first, finding
positives before it flags many negatives, so its curve hugs the top-left corner. A classifier that flags a random
share of everything finds, on average, that share of each class, so its points lie on the diagonal, with half the
unit square, 0.5, under it.

The area under the ROC curve, the **AUC**, is 0.938 here. It is worked out as a strip of area for each piece of the
curve, but it has a meaning of its own. Pair every positive with every negative: 40 × 40 = 1,600 pairs. In 1,495 of
them the positive scores higher, and 12 are ties. Counting a tie as half a pair,

$$
\frac{1495 + 12/2}{1600} = \frac{1501}{1600} = 0.938
$$

which is the AUC exactly. Why? As the sweep passes a positive, the curve rises by $1/40$, at a false positive rate equal
to the share of negatives scoring above that positive. The strip of area under that rise, out to the right edge, is
$1/40$ tall and as wide as the share of negatives the positive beats. A positive that ties with a negative makes the
curve step up and across at once, along a diagonal, so its strip loses a triangle: half of that pair. One strip per
positive adds up every pair won, ties as half.

<KeyIdea>

**AUC is the chance that a random positive scores above a random negative**, ties counting half. It judges how well
the scores rank the examples, over every threshold at once: 0.5 is guessing, and 1 is a perfect ranking, every positive
above every negative.

</KeyIdea>

AUC never picks a threshold. It counts how often the scores put a positive above a negative, which is about the
order of the scores, not about the answers at any one cut. Here the AUC is 0.938, but no threshold gets more than 70
of the 80 answers right (87.5%).

</Misconception>

<Section step="do" title="Count a confusion matrix">

Counting the four kinds of answer is a loop with one decision per example: is it flagged, and is it really positive?
Take seven made-up scores, 0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.5, 0.5, 0.3 and 0.1, whose labels are 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0 (1 for a
positive), with the threshold at 0.5. The scores 0.9, 0.8 and the first 0.5 are flagged positives: TP = 3. The 0.7
and the second 0.5 are flagged negatives: FP = 2. The 0.3 is a missed positive, FN = 1, and the 0.1 an unflagged
negative, TN = 1. Both 0.5s are flagged, because the rule is a score of $t$ or more.

<CodeExercise exercise="confusion" />

The AUC needs no loop over thresholds at all. Counting pairs directly compares every positive with every negative,
and 600 of each make 360,000 pairs. Sorting the scores once is faster: walk up them from the lowest, keeping count of
the negatives passed so far, and each positive beats all of those at once. With positives scoring 0.8 and 0.4 and
negatives 0.4 and 0.2, the 0.8 beats both negatives and the 0.4 beats one and ties one, so the AUC is
$(3 + 0.5)/4 = 0.875$. Write it in [the area under the ROC curve, by sorting](/code/auc).

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="When positives are rare">

Back to the 80 emails, and now make the positives rare. Keep the 40 positives, and count each negative 99 times, itself
and 98 copies scoring just as it does: 40 positives among 4,000 examples, 1 in 100, as with fraud, a rare disease or a
faulty part on a production line.

<Explore slug="roc-curve" settings={{ preset: 'imbalance', seed: 7 }} caption="One classifier on half positives, then on 1 positive in 100: the confusion matrix, the rates, and the ROC and precision–recall curves for both." />

A lazy model that flags nothing is right about all 3,960 negatives: 99% accurate, with a recall of 0. The real
classifier at $t = 0.5$ still has recall 0.9 and a false positive rate of 0.225. Each of those is a share of one class,
and copies keep every within-class share exactly as it was, so the mix can't move them. That is why copies: new
negatives drawn from the same classifier would wobble these shares, and the ROC curve, a little, but leave them the
same on average. But its 9 false positives have become 891, and its accuracy has fallen to 77.6%, below the
lazy model's. And precision collapses, from 0.8 to 0.039: of the 927 examples it flags, only 36 are positive. A small
false positive rate, applied to a crowd of negatives, outnumbers all the true finds. That is the rare-disease puzzle
from Bayes' rule again.

The ROC curve doesn't notice. Every point on it is a pair of within-class shares, which the copies keep exactly, so
the curve, and these first 80 emails' AUC of 0.914, are exactly the same at either mix. That is its strength, since it describes the scores whatever the mix, and its
blind spot. The **precision–recall curve**, precision plotted against recall at every threshold, does notice: at 1 in
100 it sits far below the balanced one. At $t = 0.7$, precision falls from 0.947 to 0.154 (18 positives among 117
flags). Precision reaches 1 only above the highest negative score, 0.7, where no negative is flagged at all, and there
recall is at most 0.375.

When both matter, one number that is low whenever either is low helps: the **F1 score**,
$F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P + R}$ for precision $P$ and recall $R$. At $t = 0.5$ it is 0.847 with half the examples positive,
and 0.074 at 1 in 100.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A threshold turns scores into answers of four kinds: true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives, counted in a confusion matrix.
- Recall is TP over the real positives; the false positive rate is FP over the real negatives; precision is TP over everything flagged. Recall and precision answer different questions.
- Raising the threshold never raises recall or the false positive rate; precision usually rises, but it can fall. Choose the threshold from what each mistake costs, not 0.5 by habit.
- The ROC curve plots recall against the false positive rate at every threshold, from (0, 0) to (1, 1). Its area, the AUC, is the chance that a random positive outscores a random negative: 0.5 is guessing, 1 is perfect.
- When positives are rare, accuracy and the ROC curve can look fine while most flags are false. Check precision and recall, or the precision–recall curve.

</Section>
