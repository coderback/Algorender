<Section step="hook" title="How fast, right now?">

A car's speedometer reads 50 km/h. Speed is distance over time, yet the needle shows a speed at a single instant,
when no time passes. What can that number mean?

Machine learning asks the same question. A model's loss depends on millions of weights, and training asks of each:
if this weight grew a tiny bit, how fast would the loss change, and which way? For one input the answer is a
**derivative**; for all of them at once, the **gradient**. Both come from one move: measure a slope across a small gap,
then let the gap shrink.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="secant slope">The slope of the line through two points of a curve, $P = (a, f(a))$ and $Q = (a + h, f(a + h))$: rise over run.</Term>
  <Term name="derivative f′(a)">What the secant slopes settle on as $h$ shrinks to 0 from both sides: the curve's slope at the single point $a$.</Term>
  <Term name="tangent line">The line through $P$ with slope $f'(a)$: the closest straight fit to the curve near $P$.</Term>
  <Term name="partial derivative ∂f/∂x">The slope as $x$ changes while every other input is held fixed.</Term>
  <Term name="gradient ∇f">All the partial derivatives stacked into a vector. It points the way $f$ rises fastest.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Shrink the gap">

$P$ sits on $f(x) = x^2$ at $a = 1$. A slope needs two points, so take a second point $Q$ a gap $h$ further along,
measure the slope between them, and shrink $h$.

<Explore slug="derivative" settings={{ preset: 'square' }} caption="As h shrinks from 2 to 0.01, the secant slope goes 4, 3, 2.5, 2.25, 2.1, 2.01, and from the left, h = −0.01 gives 1.99. Both sides close in on 2, the slope of the tangent. Then the slope is measured at ten points from x = −2.7 to 2.7: the graph of f′(x) = 2x is a straight line." />

For $x^2$, algebra shows why:

$$
\frac{(a + h)^2 - a^2}{h} = \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = 2a + h
$$

At $a = 1$ that is $2 + h$: every secant misses the tangent's slope by exactly $h$. As $h$ shrinks to 0 from either
side, $2a + h$ settles on $2a$, so the derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$. In general,

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}
$$

and the limit must be the same from both sides. That is the needle's number: average speeds over shorter and shorter
intervals, settling on one value.

Done at every point, this makes $f'$ a function of its own, the Slope f′(x) graph above. Its **sign** says which way $f$ is
going: negative left of 0, where $x^2$ falls, positive right of it. It is **0** where $f$ levels off, at peaks and
valleys. Its **size** says how sensitive $f$ is: at $x = 2.7$, $f' = 5.4$, so a small step in $x$ moves $f$ about 5.4
times as far; near 0, $f$ hardly moves.

<KeyIdea>

**The derivative $f'(a)$ is the slope the secants settle on as the gap $h$ shrinks to 0 from both sides: the slope of
the tangent line at $a$.** Near $a$ the tangent is the best straight-line guess, so
$f(a + h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,h$. Every method that follows a gradient relies on that guess.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Slopes in code, and slopes on a map">

A computer can't let $h$ reach 0, so it stops at a small $h$ and divides. This program measures the **forward
difference**, the secant slope with $Q$ to the right of $P$, for $x^2$ at $a = 3$ with $h$ = 0.5, 0.25 and 0.125.

  answer={2}
  why="For x² the secant slope is 2a + h, here 6 + h: 6.5, then 6.25, then 6.125. Each halving of h halves the gap to the true slope, 6, because the forward difference’s error is proportional to h."
/>

Points on both sides of $a$ do better. The **central difference**, $\frac{f(a + h) - f(a - h)}{2h}$, averages the
forward and backward slopes. On $x^2$ those are $2a + h$ and $2a - h$, so the errors cancel exactly; in general what is
left is proportional to $h^2$. For $e^x$ at 0 (slope 1) with $h = 0.01$, forward gives 1.0050 and central 1.0000167.

So why not take $h = 10^{-20}$? Because floats have gaps (see [floating point](/learn/floating-point)). For $x^2$ at 3
in float64, $h = 10^{-8}$ gives 5.99999996, $h = 10^{-12}$ gives 6.0005, and $h = 10^{-16}$ gives 0: floats near 3 are
about $4 \times 10^{-16}$ apart, so $3 + 10^{-16}$ rounds back to 3. **Gradient checking** tests
[backpropagation](/learn/backpropagation) this way: nudge each weight about $10^{-5}$ either way and compare the
central difference of the loss with the backward pass's answer.

In exact arithmetic a smaller $h$ is closer; floats are not exact. As $h$ shrinks, $f(a + h)$ and $f(a)$
share more leading digits, so their difference keeps fewer correct ones, and dividing by $h$ magnifies the rest. The
straight-line error shrinks with $h$ while the rounding error grows like $1/h$, so the best $h$ in float64 is about
$10^{-8}$ for a forward difference and $10^{-5}$ for a central one.

</Misconception>

Nobody works out every derivative from the limit. A few rules, each proved once from it, cover this lesson's functions:

- **Power rule:** $x^n$ gives $n x^{n-1}$, so $x^3$ gives $3x^2$. A constant gives 0.
- **Constant multiple and sum:** $(c f)' = c f'$ and $(f + g)' = f' + g'$, so $x^3/3 - x$ gives $x^2 - 1$.
- **Two special functions:** $e^x$ is its own derivative, and $\sin x$ gives $\cos x$ (in radians).

<Explore slug="derivative" settings={{ preset: 'exp' }} input caption="eˣ at a = 0.5. The secant slopes fall from about 5.27 at h = 2 to 1.657 at h = 0.01 (1.641 from the left) and settle on 1.6487: e^0.5, the height of P. f′ comes out as eˣ again. Try another in the box, such as sine at 2." />

The rules assume the curve is smooth where you use them.

Unbroken is not the same as smooth. At a corner the curve changes direction all at once, so the secants from each side
settle on different slopes and no tangent fits: $|x|$ has slope −1 left of 0 and +1 right of it, and ReLU,
$\max(0, x)$, has slopes 0 and 1. Training code picks a value at that one point (usually 0), since an input almost
never lands exactly on it.

</Misconception>

A loss depends on many weights at once. For $f(x, y)$, the **partial derivative** $\partial f/\partial x$ is the
ordinary derivative in $x$ with $y$ held fixed like a number, and $\partial f/\partial y$ the other way round.
In the narrow valley $f(x, y) = x^2/20 + 5y^2$ they are $x/10$ and $10y$. Stacked, they make the gradient
$\nabla f = (x/10, 10y)$, which at $(-3.5, 1.5)$ is $(-0.35, 15)$.

Walk in the direction of a unit vector $u$, and the slope underfoot is the dot product $\nabla f \cdot u$.
That is largest when $u$ points along $\nabla f$, so **the gradient points the way $f$ rises fastest, and its length is
that steepest slope**: about 15 at $(-3.5, 1.5)$, where walking along $+x$ gives just −0.35. At right angles to
$\nabla f$ the slope is 0, which is along the contour line: the gradient crosses contour lines at right angles.

<Explore slug="gradient-descent" settings={{ preset: 'timid' }} caption="The valley x²/20 + 5y² from (−3.5, 1.5), with small steps (η = 0.02). Each arrow, −η·∇f, is at right angles to the contour line where it starts. The first gradient is (−0.35, 15), so the path heads almost straight across the valley, not towards the minimum at (0, 0), then creeps along the floor: after 40 steps it is only at (−3.23, 0)." />

The tangent guess works in many dimensions too: $f(p + \Delta) \approx f(p) + \nabla f \cdot \Delta$ for a small step
$\Delta$. Step by $\Delta = -\eta \nabla f$ and the guess falls by $\eta\,|\nabla f|^2$, so a small enough step against
a nonzero gradient always goes downhill. [Gradient descent](/learn/gradient-descent) is that one idea, repeated.

The gradient only knows the ground where you stand. It crosses the contour line there at right angles, and that aims at
the top (or, for $-\nabla f$, the bottom) from everywhere only when the contours are circles round it, as on a round
bowl. In a long valley $-\nabla f$ points mostly across the valley: that is why the path above dropped to the floor
first, and why big steps zigzag.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- $f'(a)$ is the limit of the secant slope as $h \to 0$, from both sides. At a corner, like $|x|$ or ReLU at 0, the
  sides disagree and there is none.
- The sign of $f'$ says rising or falling, it is 0 at smooth peaks and valleys, and its size says how sensitive $f$ is.
  Near $a$, $f(a + h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,h$.
- Rules: $x^n$ gives $n x^{n-1}$, constant factors stay, sums split, $e^x$ stays $e^x$, and $\sin x$ gives $\cos x$.
- The forward difference's error is proportional to $h$, the central difference's to $h^2$, and too small an $h$ loses
  digits to rounding.
- The gradient stacks the partial derivatives. It points the way $f$ rises fastest, its length is that slope, it
  crosses contour lines at right angles, and the slope along a unit vector $u$ is $\nabla f \cdot u$.
- Next: [gradient descent](/learn/gradient-descent) walks against the gradient, and
  [backpropagation](/learn/backpropagation) computes it for every weight. Then try
  [Backpropagate through a neuron by hand](/code/neuron-gradients).

</Section>
