<Section step="hook" title="The best line is a shadow">

Put some measurements on a chart and ask a spreadsheet for a trend line. It draws one in an instant, and no line could
fit better. In the lesson on learning from examples, the best line was the one with the smallest squared error, found by
trying lines. This lesson finds it in one step, with no trying at all.

The trick is a picture you have already seen. In the dot product lesson, a vector cast a **shadow** on another one's
line, and the leftover part was at right angles to it. The best fit's predictions, one for each data point, are that same
shadow, dropped in more dimensions. You will use everything from this stage: the dot product, matrix multiplication,
span and solving $Ax = b$ by elimination.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="projection p">The shadow of $y$ on a line (or a flat space): the point on it closest to $y$.</Term>
  <Term name="residual e">What is left over, $e = y - p$: for a fitted line, the gaps between each data point and the line, measured straight up or down.</Term>
  <Term name="X (the design matrix)">One row per data point. To fit $\hat{y} = b + w \cdot x$, its columns are a column of 1s (for $b$) and the column of $x$ values (for $w$).</Term>
  <Term name="weights θ">All the numbers the fit chooses, as one vector. For a line, $\theta = (b, w)$, so $X\theta$ lists the line's prediction for every point.</Term>
  <Term name="Xᵀ, X transpose">$X$ with its rows and columns swapped, so row $j$ of $X^\mathsf{T}$ is column $j$ of $X$.</Term>
  <Term name="normal equations">$X^\mathsf{T} X \theta = X^\mathsf{T} y$: a small square system whose solution $\theta$ is the best fit.</Term>
  <Term name="least squares">Choosing the weights that make the sum of the squared residuals as small as possible.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="The closest point is a shadow">

Start with one direction. The dashed line holds every multiple $t \cdot a$ of $a = (4, 2)$. Which point on it is closest
to $y = (1, 3)$?

<Explore slug="least-squares" settings={{ preset: 'shadow' }} caption="Two guesses on the line, then the closest point p, where the gap meets the line square on. Drag a or y." />

The shortest gap from a point to a line meets the line at a right angle, so the closest point $p = t \cdot a$ is the
one whose gap $e = y - t \cdot a$ has $e \cdot a = 0$. That one condition gives $t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(y - t\,a) \cdot a &= 0 \\
a \cdot y - t\,(a \cdot a) &= 0 \\
t &= \frac{a \cdot y}{a \cdot a}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $a \cdot y = 10$ and $a \cdot a = 20$, so $t = 0.5$ and $p = (2, 1)$. The gap is $e = (-1, 2)$, and
$e \cdot a = -4 + 4 = 0$: a right angle. Its length, $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$, is shorter than the gap to any other
point on the line.

<KeyIdea>

**The closest point leaves a gap at right angles.** To drop $y$ onto $a$'s line, solve $e \cdot a = 0$:
$p = \frac{a \cdot y}{a \cdot a}\, a$. Least squares is this drop, done onto everything a matrix $X$ can reach.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: fit a line to five points">

Five points: $(1, 1)$, $(2, 3)$, $(3, 2)$, $(4, 5)$ and $(5, 4)$. We want $\hat{y} = b + w \cdot x$. Each point asks for
$b + w \cdot x_i = y_i$, so there are 5 equations and only 2 unknowns. Written as a matrix, $X\theta = y$ with
$\theta = (b, w)$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} b \\ w \end{bmatrix}
\approx
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 2 \\ 5 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Can all five equations hold at once? The challenge below asks you first. When they can't, take the closest point that
*can* be reached, the shadow of $y$. In the span lesson's words, $X\theta$ is $b$ times the 1s column plus $w$ times the
$x$ column, so it can only reach the span of those two columns. Because $X\theta$ can be any mix of the columns, the
gap $e = y - X\theta$ must be at right angles to each of them, just as $e$ was to $a$.

$X^\mathsf{T} e$ is exactly that check: each row of $X^\mathsf{T}$ is a column of $X$, so $X^\mathsf{T} e$ lists each
column of $X$ dotted with $e$. Setting it to 0 and multiplying out mirrors $a \cdot y - t\,(a \cdot a) = 0$ from the
shadow:

$$
\begin{aligned}
X^\mathsf{T}(y - X\theta) &= 0 \\
X^\mathsf{T} y - X^\mathsf{T} X \theta &= 0 \\
X^\mathsf{T} X \theta &= X^\mathsf{T} y
\end{aligned}
$$

These are the **normal equations**. Step through and predict each piece: what goes in $X^\mathsf{T} X$ and
$X^\mathsf{T} y$, the slope, and what a nudge does.

<PredictChallenge slug="least-squares" settings={{ preset: 'line' }} goal={4} />

No line goes through all five points, so the shadow is the best we can do. Each entry of $X^\mathsf{T} X$ is a column of
$X$ dotted with a column: the 1s with themselves count the points, the 1s with $x$ add up the $x$ values, and $x$ with
itself adds up their squares. $X^\mathsf{T} y$ holds the sum of $y$ and the sum of $x \cdot y$:

$$
X^\mathsf{T} X = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 15 \\ 15 & 55 \end{bmatrix}, \quad X^\mathsf{T} y = \begin{bmatrix} 15 \\ 53 \end{bmatrix}
$$

That is two equations in two unknowns:

$$
\begin{aligned}
5b + 15w &= 15 \\
15b + 55w &= 53
\end{aligned}
$$

Eliminate as before: the second row minus 3 times the first leaves $10w = 8$, so $w = 0.8$, and then
$5b = 15 - 15 \times 0.8 = 3$, so $b = 0.6$. The best line is $\hat{y} = 0.6 + 0.8x$. Its residuals add up to 0 and
so do the residuals times $x$, and their squares add up to 3.6. Raise the slope by 0.1 and that grows to 4.15. Any
change to $b$ or $w$ does the same, because $X\theta$ is already the closest point to $y$ that a line can reach: that
is why it's called **least squares**.

Here a residual is $e = y - \hat{y}$, the opposite sign of the error $\hat{y} - y$ in the lesson on learning from
examples. Squaring doesn't care about the sign, and neither do right angles. And dividing by the number of points
doesn't change which line wins, so the line with the least squared error also has the least mean squared error. That
lesson's revision data, solved this way, gives exactly the line it promised: $w = 1.05$ and $b = 1.35$, with a mean
squared error of 0.295.

>

"At right angles" is easy to picture in the wrong place. In the chart, every residual is a **vertical** gap: the real
$y$ minus the line's $\hat{y}$ at the same $x$. The right angle is between lists of numbers, one number per data point:
the list of residuals $e$ and each column of $X$ have a dot product of 0. With 5 points those are vectors with 5
parts, so the shadow is dropped in 5 dimensions, which no chart can draw.

</Misconception>

      q: 'A fitted line’s residuals add up to 0, but Σx·e = −4. What does that tell you?',

>

$\Sigma e = 0$ is only one of the two conditions: it says $e$ is at right angles to the column of 1s. Every line
through the middle of the data, the point (mean $x$, mean $y$), satisfies it, steep or flat. For the five points that
middle is $(3, 3)$, and the flat line $\hat{y} = 3$ has $\Sigma e = 0$, yet $\Sigma x \cdot e = 8$ and a squared error
of 10, against 3.6 for the best line. The best line must also be at right angles to the $x$ column:
$\Sigma x \cdot e = 0$. Together they are $X^\mathsf{T} e = 0$.

</Misconception>

<Section step="do" title="Bend the fit, then build it">

Nothing in the recipe needs a straight line. Give $X$ a third column, $x^2$, and the same normal equations fit a
parabola $\hat{y} = c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2$, with $\theta = (c_0, c_1, c_2)$ and a $3 \times 3$ $X^\mathsf{T} X$.

<Explore slug="least-squares" settings={{ preset: 'quadratic' }} caption="Six points, the best line through them, then a third column, x², and a 3×3 system. Drag the points." />

The best line through these six points has a squared error of 14.676. The best parabola,
$\hat{y} = 8.4 - 4.132x + 0.625x^2$, gets it down to 0.093. Adding a column can never make the fit to the same data
worse, because a line is a parabola with $c_2 = 0$. That doesn't make more columns better, though: a curve bent to every
point can do badly on new data, which the lesson on overfitting takes up later.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- The closest point to $y$ on $a$'s line is $p = \frac{a \cdot y}{a \cdot a}\, a$, and the gap $y - p$ is at right angles to $a$.
- To fit $\hat{y} = b + w \cdot x$, put a column of 1s and a column of $x$ in $X$, and the weights in $\theta = (b, w)$. Usually no $\theta$ makes $X\theta = y$ exactly.
- The best $\theta$ leaves residuals at right angles to every column, $X^\mathsf{T} e = 0$, so it solves $X^\mathsf{T} X \theta = X^\mathsf{T} y$.
- In the chart residuals are vertical gaps; the right angle is between lists of numbers.
- More columns ($x^2$, …) bend the fit with the same recipe, and never fit the same data worse.

</Section>
