<Section step="hook" title="The directions a matrix leaves alone">

Apply a matrix and almost every arrow gets knocked off its line: it turns as well as stretches. But most matrices have
a few special directions where the arrow stays on its own line and is only stretched, shrunk or flipped.

Those directions explain what the matrix does when you apply it over and over, which is why they show up everywhere:
Google's PageRank, the long-run state of a Markov chain, the principal components of a dataset, and whether a
recurrent network's signal explodes or fades.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="eigenvector">A non-zero vector $v$ with $Av = \lambda v$: the matrix sends it somewhere on its own line.</Term>
  <Term name="eigenvalue λ">The stretch factor along that line. $\lambda > 1$ stretches, $0 < \lambda < 1$ shrinks, $\lambda < 0$ flips, $\lambda = 0$ crushes the line to the origin.</Term>
  <Term name="eigen line">All multiples of an eigenvector. The matrix maps the line onto itself.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Sweep a vector around the circle">

<Explore slug="eigenvectors" settings={{ preset: 'symmetric' }} caption="v sweeps around the circle; A·v traces the dashed ellipse. Where A·v lands on v's own dashed line, v is an eigenvector." />

For this symmetric matrix the arrow lines up twice, at 45° (where $A$ triples it) and at 135° (where it leaves it
alone). At the end, the whole grid is transformed: the two eigen lines stay exactly where they were.

<KeyIdea>

**Along an eigen line the matrix is just multiplication by a number.** Write any vector as a mix of eigenvectors and the
matrix scales each part by its own $\lambda$, which is why repeated multiplication is dominated by the largest $|\lambda|$.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Break it on purpose">

Try the examples below, then drag î and ĵ at the end of a run to see the eigen lines move.

- **Shear:** only one eigen line, the x-axis. Every other arrow leans to the right.
- **Rotation:** no arrow ever lines up, so there are no real eigenvectors (the eigenvalues are complex numbers).
- **Squash flat:** one eigenvalue is 0. A whole line is crushed onto the origin, which is why $\det A = 0$.

<Misconception
  id="always-two"
  title="Every matrix has two eigenvectors"
  probes={[
    {

      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'The shear leaves î where it is (λ = 1), but pushes every other direction sideways, so the x-axis is its only eigen line.',
    },
    {
      q: 'A rotation by 60°: how many real eigenvectors does it have?',
      choices: ['Two, at right angles', 'One, along the x-axis', 'None: every direction turns by 60°'],
      answer: 2,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'An eigenvector must stay on its own line, and a 60° turn moves every line through the origin. Its eigenvalues are complex numbers.',
    },
  ]}
>

Only some do. A rotation turns every direction, so none survives; a shear has just one eigen line. What is always true:
the eigenvalues (counting complex ones) multiply to $\det A$ and add up to $a + d$, the trace.

</Misconception>

<Misconception
  id="unchanged"
  title="An eigenvector is a vector the matrix leaves unchanged"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'A = [[2, 0], [0, 5]] and v = (1, 0). Is v an eigenvector?',
      choices: ['No: A changes it to (2, 0)', 'Yes, with λ = 2: it stays on its own line', 'Yes, with λ = 5', 'No: only (0, 0) is left unchanged'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'A·v = (2, 0) = 2·v. The length changes, but the direction stays on v’s line, which is all an eigenvector needs.',
    },
    {
      q: 'Av = 3v. What can you say about A·v?',
      choices: ['It is at right angles to v', 'It is v itself, unchanged', 'It lies on v’s line and is three times as long'],
      answer: 2,
      trap: 1,
      why: 'λ = 3 is the stretch along v’s line. Leaving v unchanged is the special case λ = 1.',
    },
  ]}
>

Only the eigenvector's *line* survives. Its length is multiplied by $\lambda$, and a negative $\lambda$ flips it to point
the other way along the same line. A vector that comes back exactly as it was is the special case $\lambda = 1$.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- An eigenvector stays on its own line: $Av = \lambda v$. The eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the stretch along it.
- Find them geometrically by sweeping a direction and watching where $Av$ lines up with $v$.
- Rotations have none (real), shears have one, symmetric matrices have two at right angles.
- Eigenvalues multiply to $\det A$ and add up to the trace. Next: eigenvectors of a covariance matrix are the principal components of data (PCA).

</Section>
