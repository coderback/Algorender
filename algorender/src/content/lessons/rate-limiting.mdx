<Section step="hook" title="One client's bug, everyone's outage">

An API's servers can handle about 1,000 requests a second, shared by thousands of customers. One customer ships a bug: a
script that retries in a tight loop, 2,000 times a second. Now more work arrives than the servers can do, and the
[queueing lesson](/learn/queueing) showed what a line does once the load passes 100%: it grows without end, until
everyone times out.

A **rate limiter** at the door turns away each client's requests over its agreed rate at once, with a short reply that
costs almost nothing. The buggy script gets errors; everyone else gets fast answers. This lesson takes apart the
**token bucket** the queueing lesson met in passing, then its rivals, and what a client should do when told no.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="rate limit">An agreed rate for one client, such as 100 requests a minute per API key. A **rate limiter** turns away the requests over it.</Term>
  <Term name="token bucket">Holds up to **capacity** tokens and refills at **rate** tokens a second. A request spends one token, or is rejected if the bucket holds less than one, so a **burst** of up to capacity requests passes at once.</Term>
  <Term name="fixed and sliding windows">Limiters that count requests per window of time. A fixed window resets on the clock, say each minute; a sliding window looks back one window from now.</Term>
  <Term name="leaky bucket">A queue that drains at a fixed rate. Requests wait their turn, and are rejected only when the queue is full.</Term>
  <Term name="429 Too Many Requests">The HTTP status for a rejected request, often with a **Retry-After** header saying when to try again.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Two numbers per client">

A token bucket remembers two numbers per client: its **tokens**, and the time, **last**, when it counted them. No timer
drips tokens in; when a request arrives at time t, the bucket works out what a drip would have added since:

`tokens = min(capacity, tokens + rate × (t − last))`, then `last = t`.

With at least one token, the request spends it and goes through; otherwise it is rejected. The `min` stops a quiet
client saving up: however long it waits, it comes back to at most a full bucket.

<Explore slug="token-bucket" settings={{ input: 'capacity = 4, rate = 2, pattern = spike, seed = 4' }} caption="A bucket of 4 tokens refilling at 2 a second. Three requests, about a second apart, each find it full. Then 12 arrive in under 2 s: the first five get through on the stored tokens and the refill, then only two of the other seven, half a second apart, as fast as the refill pays. The three after the spike find tokens again: 13 of 18 get through." />

That is O(1) work per request, and a million clients' buckets fit in tens of megabytes. A bucket idle for capacity ÷
rate seconds is full again, so a limiter can forget idle clients and hand them a full bucket when they return.

<KeyIdea>

**In any stretch of t seconds, a token bucket lets through at most capacity + rate × t requests.** It starts the stretch
with at most capacity tokens, the refill adds at most rate × t, and each request that gets through spends one. So the
capacity sets the largest burst and the rate caps the long run. The dashed line labelled capacity + rate × t on the
second chart is that bound: 4 + 2 × 8 = 20 in 8 s, against the 13 that got through above.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

**Choosing the numbers.** The rate is the load you will pass on for good: a client's fair share, and for a bucket
guarding a whole service, comfortably below what it can sustain, since latency explodes near full load. The capacity is
the burst you will absorb at once. Real clients are bursty (one page load can fire a dozen calls), so a capacity of 1
turns honest users away, while a huge one lets a flood through before the rate bites.

<Explore slug="token-bucket" settings={{ input: 'capacity = 8, rate = 2, pattern = spike, seed = 4' }} input caption="The spike from before against a bucket of 8: 17 of 18 get through. Change only the capacity: 1 lets 10 through, 4 lets 13, and 9 or more all 18. Each token of capacity passes one more request of the burst." />

**Other ways to count.** The rivals differ in what they remember:

- A **fixed-window counter** keeps one count per client per minute on the clock, reset when the minute turns. The
  cheapest, but the reset is a cliff, as the first mistake below shows.
- A **sliding-window log** keeps the time of each request it let through in the last window, and allows a new one only
  if fewer than the limit are there. Exact, but a limit of 1,000 an hour for a million clients can mean a billion
  timestamps.
- A **sliding-window counter** keeps this minute's count and the last one's, weighting the old one by how much of it
  still lies in the past 60 seconds: 15 seconds into a minute, three quarters. Two numbers, and close when traffic is
  fairly even.
- A **leaky bucket** sends queued requests on at a fixed rate: perfectly smooth output, but requests wait, where a token
  bucket passes a burst at once.

A fixed window forgets its count the moment it resets, so a client can send the whole limit just before the minute turns
and again just after: twice the limit within a second or two, though each window's count looks fine. A sliding-window
log counts the last minute exactly, and a token bucket keeps bursts to a size you choose.

</Misconception>

**Many servers, one limit.** A limiter usually runs in the API gateway, keyed by API key, user or IP address (the
crudest: many users share an address, and an attacker can use many). A big service runs many gateways, and a key's
limit must hold across them all. Keep the buckets in a shared store, refilling, checking and spending in one
atomic step, since a separate read and write is a race (the [concurrency lesson](/learn/concurrency)); let each machine
enforce its share, with no extra round trip; or send each key to the one limiter that owns it, picked by
[consistent hashing](/learn/consistent-hashing).

A machine only sees the requests sent to it, so if N machines each enforce the full limit, a client spread over them
gets up to N times the limit. Give each machine 1/N (right when requests spread evenly), share one atomic count per key,
or send each key to one limiter.

</Misconception>

**What the client sees.** A rejected request gets a **429**, cheap to send and clear about why. A well-behaved client
waits as long as its Retry-After header says. With no header it backs off **exponentially**, waiting about 1 s, then 2,
then 4, up to a cap, so a struggling service gets more room each time. And it adds **jitter**, a random part of each
wait, because clients rejected together would otherwise all retry together.

Retrying at once earns more 429s, since the bucket refills no faster for being asked, and adds load to a service already
shedding it. A fixed schedule, even one that backs off, is no better at scale: clients rejected together retry together,
in waves as big as the one turned away. Honour Retry-After; otherwise back off exponentially, with random jitter.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A rate limiter turns away requests over a client's agreed rate at once, with a 429, so no one client can push a
  service to where its queues explode.
- A token bucket keeps two numbers per client and refills lazily from the time since it last counted: O(1) per request.
- In any t seconds it passes at most capacity + rate × t. Set the rate comfortably below what the service sustains, and
  the capacity to the bursts you will absorb.
- A fixed window can pass twice its limit across a boundary, a sliding log is exact but stores every request, a sliding
  counter estimates, and a leaky bucket smooths traffic by making it wait.
- Across machines, share one atomic count, split the limit, or route each key to one limiter. Clients honour Retry-After
  and back off exponentially, with jitter.
- Next: [system design](/learn/system-design) and [An API that survives a spike](/design/rate-limited-api) put a limiter
  in front of a whole service. Practise keeping state per key and spacing out work with
  [Least Recently Used Cache](/practice/lru-cache) and [Schedule Tasks with a Cooldown](/practice/task-scheduler).

</Section>
