<Section step="hook" title="A loss with two knobs">

In the lesson on learning from examples, a line had two knobs, $w$ and $b$. Every choice of the
pair gave one number, the loss. So the loss is a rule with **two inputs** and one output. A real model has millions of
knobs, but two are enough to see the problem: how do you draw a rule like that?

A graph with one input is a curve. With two inputs, every point $(w, b)$ of a flat grid gets its own height, so the
picture is a whole landscape, with hills, valleys and passes. Maps for walkers have long drawn landscapes flat:
each line on the map joins places at the same height. That map is a **contour map**, and it is how machine learning
draws a loss over two weights. A later lesson sends gradient descent walking downhill on one.

This lesson teaches you to read one: what a height is, which way is level, where the ground is steep, and what a slice
through a point looks like.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="function of two inputs">A rule $f(x, y)$ that takes two numbers, $x$ and $y$, and gives back one: a height above the point $(x, y)$.</Term>
  <Term name="table with two inputs">Its values written out: a column for each $x$, a row for each $y$, and in each cell the height $f(x, y)$.</Term>
  <Term name="contour line">A line through every point where $f$ has one height, say 5. On a hiking map, the lines of equal height.</Term>
  <Term name="contour map">Many contour lines at evenly spaced heights, drawn on the same flat grid: the whole landscape, seen from above.</Term>
  <Term name="slice">Hold one input fixed and let the other vary. What is left is an ordinary curve of one input, like the graphs you already know.</Term>
  <Term name="saddle">A point where the ground rises one way and falls the other, like the middle of a horse's saddle or a mountain pass.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="From a table of heights to a map">

Our landscape is a hill:

$$
f(x, y) = 8 - x^2/2 - y^2
$$

Put in two numbers, get one height. At the top, $f(0, 0) = 8$. Two units along $x$, $f(2, 0) = 8 - 2^2/2 - 0 = 6$. Two
units along $y$ instead, $f(0, 2) = 8 - 0 - 4 = 4$. The same distance from the top, but not the same height: this hill
isn't round, and the map will show it.

Work out the height at every whole-number point with $x$ from −3 to 3 and $y$ from 2 down to −2, and you have a table
with two inputs: 7 columns, one for each $x$, and 5 rows, one for each $y$. The largest $y$ goes on top, so the table
sits the same way up as the map. Two of its rows:

- the top row, $y = 2$: −0.5, 2, 3.5, 4, 3.5, 2, −0.5
- the middle row, $y = 0$: 3.5, 6, 7.5, 8, 7.5, 6, 3.5

<Explore slug="contour-maps" settings={{ preset: 'table' }} caption="The hill's heights at the grid points, filled in row by row, then joined into contour lines one height at a time, from 7 down to 1." />

Then the heights become a map. Pick a height, say 7, and draw one line through every point where the height is exactly
7: that is the contour line $f = 7$. It passes through the grid points $(0, 1)$ and $(0, -1)$, whose heights are 7, and
curves between them through points the table doesn't list.

Picture the hill standing in a lake filled to height 7: seen from above, the shoreline is the contour $f = 7$. Raise
the water to 8 and the shore shrinks to the single point at the top. Do the same for 6, 5, 4 and on down to 1, and the
lines form rings round the top, 1 apart in height, like the lines on a hiking map.

The rings alone don't say which way is up: a hollow looks just the same. The numbers on the lines and the shading do.
A loss map is a hollow: its rings circle the lowest point, the best knobs.

A contour line doesn't need a grid point. No grid point has height exactly 3: the contour $f = 3$ runs between them, for
example between $(-3, 0)$ at height 3.5 and $(-3, 1)$ at height 2.5. Somewhere between those two the ground must pass
through 3, so the line crosses there.

<KeyIdea>

**A function of two inputs is a landscape: one height above every point $(x, y)$.** A contour line joins the points of
one height, and a contour map draws many of them, evenly spaced in height, on the flat grid. You read the function off
the map the way a walker reads a hill.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: walk on the map">

Now $P$ stands on the hill at $(1, 1)$. The faint lines are the contours, one for each whole-number height, and the
shading is darker on higher ground. Predict what happens to the height as you walk along the contour line through $P$, then
as you step straight across the lines, first along $x$ and then along $y$, and say which way the ground is steeper.

<PredictChallenge slug="contour-maps" settings={{ preset: 'walk' }} goal={5} />

$P$ is at height $f(1, 1) = 8 - 0.5 - 1 = 6.5$, between the contour lines for 6 and 7. Walking along its own contour
line, the height reads 6.5 after every step, and the line curves round into an oval that brings you back to $P$. That
is what a contour line is: the path that stays level.

Stepping across is different. From $(0.5, 1)$ to $(1.5, 1)$, one unit along $x$ through $P$, the height falls from
6.875 to 5.875, a change of −1. From $(1, 0.5)$ to $(1, 1.5)$, one unit along $y$, it falls from 7.25 to 5.25, a change
of −2. So the ground near $P$ is twice as steep along $y$.

Look at the map to see it. The lines are 1 apart in height, so where the ground drops 2 per unit they come about half a
unit apart; where it drops 1 per unit, more than twice that. **Close contour lines mean steep ground; wide gaps mean
gentle ground.**

Contour lines look like paths, so it is tempting to think water would run along them. It is the opposite: a contour line
joins points of one height, so walking along it you never climb or drop. To go downhill you cross the lines, from a
higher one to a lower one. The way that drops fastest is the way the next lesson finds.

</Misconception>

Crowded lines can look like a busy, flat patch, but every gap between neighbouring lines is the same change in height.
If that change happens over a short distance the ground is steep; over a long distance it is gentle. So the closer the
lines, the steeper the ground.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Slice the surface, then build the table">

A contour map shows the whole landscape. To see its shape through one point, **slice** it. Hold $y$ fixed and let only
$x$ vary, as if walking west to east (left to right) along one line of the map, and the heights along that line make an ordinary curve
of one input: the **x-slice**. Hold $x$ fixed instead and you get the **y-slice**.

Here $P$ is at $(2, 0)$ on the same hill. Step through, then drag $P$ and watch both slices move with it.

<Explore slug="contour-maps" settings={{ preset: 'slices' }} input caption="The map with the two lines through P, and beside or below it the chart “Slices through P”: the heights along each line drawn as a curve of one input, with a dashed line through P on each." />

Both slices share the chart's axis: $x$ for the x-slice, $y$ for the y-slice. So $P$ appears twice, once on each curve,
at the same height 6.

Holding $y = 0$ turns the hill into $f(x, 0) = 8 - x^2/2$, an upside-down parabola: the $-$ in front of $x^2$ flips it,
just as a negative $a$ flipped a graph in the lesson on functions. $P$ sits on its right-hand side, where it falls.

How steeply? Measure it by rise over run across a small step either side of $P$. From $x = 1.9$ to $x = 2.1$ the height
goes from 6.195 to 5.795: a rise of −0.4 over a run of 0.2, a rate of −2 per unit. The dashed line in the chart falls
at that rate. Right at $P$ the ground drops at a rate of 2 per unit of $x$; further out it drops faster (2.5 from
$x = 2$ to 3), because the curve bends. The next lesson makes "the slope at a single point" exact.

Holding $x = 2$ instead gives the y-slice, $f(2, y) = 6 - y^2$, another upside-down parabola, and $P$ is at its top.
At $y = -0.1$ and at $y = 0.1$ the height is 5.99 both times: a rise of 0, so the rate is 0. Along $y$ the ground at
$P$ is level, and along $x$ it falls. Two inputs, so two slopes, one for each slice. The next lesson calls these
**partial derivatives**.

On a hill both slices through the top are hills. A saddle is different:

<Explore slug="contour-maps" settings={{ preset: 'saddle' }} input caption="The saddle (x² − y²)/2 sliced through P = (0, 0): one slice along x, one along y." />

On the saddle $f(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2)/2$, the x-slice through $(0, 0)$ is $x^2/2$, an upright parabola, a valley, and $P$
is at its bottom. The y-slice is $-y^2/2$, a hill, and $P$ is at its top. Both slopes are 0, so the ground at $P$ is
level both ways, yet $P$ is neither a peak nor a valley of the surface: walk along $x$ and you climb, walk along $y$ and
you drop. Loss landscapes of big models are full of saddles like this, so a level point is not always the bottom.

On the map a saddle's contours ring nothing: they open away from $P$, and the contour $f = 0$ crosses itself at $P$ in
an X.

A slice only shows the ground along one line. $P$ can be the top of one slice and the bottom of another, as at a
saddle. To be a peak, $P$ must be higher than everything around it, whichever way you walk, so one slice is never
enough to tell.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A function of two inputs, $f(x, y)$, gives one height above every point: a table with two inputs, or a landscape.
- A contour line joins the points of one height. Walking along it the height never changes; to climb or drop you cross the lines.
- Neighbouring contour lines are the same height apart, so close lines mean steep ground and wide gaps mean gentle ground.
- A slice holds one input fixed and leaves an ordinary curve of the other, with its own slope: two inputs, two slopes.
- At a saddle one slice is a valley and the other a hill, so one slice can't tell you a point is a peak.

</Section>
