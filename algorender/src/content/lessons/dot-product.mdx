<Section step="hook" title="One number that says “how alike”">

A music app suggests a song like the one you just played. A search engine finds pages about your question even when
they use other words. A chatbot looks up the passages most related to what you asked. Under all three sits one small
calculation on vectors: multiply the matching parts of two vectors and add the results.

That is the **dot product**. It turns two vectors into a single number that is positive when they point roughly the
same way, zero when they are at right angles, and negative when they point apart. A neuron in a neural network does the
same sum: each input times a weight, all added up. This lesson builds the dot product from the parts, then from the
picture, and then uses it to say which words are alike.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="dot product u·v">Multiply the matching parts and add: $u \cdot v = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + \dots + u_n v_n$. Said “u dot v”. The answer is one number.</Term>
  <Term name="angle θ">The angle between two arrows drawn from the same point, measured the shorter way round, so it is between 0° and 180°.</Term>
  <Term name="perpendicular (orthogonal)">At right angles, 90°. Two vectors are perpendicular exactly when their dot product is 0.</Term>
  <Term name="shadow (projection)">How far one vector goes in another's direction: the shadow it casts on the other's line when light falls at right angles to that line. Drawn as an arrow along the line, it is called the projection.</Term>
  <Term name="cosine similarity">The dot product divided by both lengths: $\cos\theta$, between $-1$ and $1$. It compares direction only.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Multiply the parts, add them up">

<Explore slug="dot-product" settings={{ preset: 'parts' }} caption="u = (2, −1, 3) and v = (1, 4, −1): each product becomes a bar, and the running total ends at −5." />

The dot product of $u = (2, -1, 3)$ and $v = (1, 4, -1)$ takes the parts in pairs, first with first, second with
second, third with third:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u \cdot v &= 2 \times 1 + (-1) \times 4 + 3 \times (-1) \\
&= 2 - 4 - 3 = -5
\end{aligned}
$$

A pair whose parts agree in sign adds to the total; a pair that disagrees pulls it down. With the Σ notation from
the sums lesson it is one short line, and in code one loop with a running total:

$$
u \cdot v = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i
$$

You have done this at a till. Buy 3, 1 and 2 of three things priced £2, £5 and £1.50, and the bill is the dot product
of the quantities and the prices: $3 \times 2 + 1 \times 5 + 2 \times 1.5 = 14$.

<Explore slug="dot-product" settings={{ preset: 'bill' }} caption="Quantities (3, 1, 2) dotted with prices (2, 5, 1.5): a bill of 14." />

Dot a vector with itself and every product is a square, so $u \cdot u = |u|^2$: the length squared, from Pythagoras.
That works with any number of parts: $(2, -1, 3)$ has length $\sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{14}$.

<KeyIdea>

**$u \cdot v$ multiplies matching parts and adds them up: two vectors in, one number out.** The vectors must have the
same number of parts, and $u \cdot v = v \cdot u$.

</KeyIdea>

Multiplying the parts gives a list of products, and it is tempting to stop there. The dot product doesn't: it adds the
products into **one number**. That is the point of it. A single number can say how much two vectors agree, and can be
compared with another single number.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: the sign as v turns">

Here $u = (3, 1)$ stays still and $v$, of length 2, starts pointing the same way, then turns away from $u$ in 30°
steps. Before each step, predict the sign of $u \cdot v$.

<PredictChallenge slug="dot-product" settings={{ preset: 'angle' }} goal={6} />

Pointing together, $u \cdot v = 6.325$, its largest value. It shrinks as $v$ turns, is exactly 0 at 90°, and is
$-6.325$ when $v$ points straight back. Every value matched one rule:

$$
u \cdot v = |u|\,|v|\cos\theta
$$

To see why, first notice that turning both arrows by the same angle doesn't change their dot product. Try it with
$(3, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$: their dot product is $3 + 2 = 5$. A quarter turn sends $(x, y)$ to $(-y, x)$, as in the sine and
cosine lesson, so the turned pair is $(-1, 3)$ and $(-2, 1)$, and $2 + 3 = 5$ again. Writing out the turning rule for
any angle shows the same happens every time. So turn the whole picture until $u$ lies along the x-axis. Now
$u = (|u|, 0)$, and from the unit circle, $v = (|v|\cos\theta,\ |v|\sin\theta)$. Multiply the parts and add:
$|u|\,|v|\cos\theta + 0$. Here $|u| = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162$ and $|v| = 2$, so the largest value is
$3.162 \times 2 \approx 6.325$.

Since $\cos\theta$ is positive below 90°, zero at 90° and negative beyond, so is the dot product. That gives a test
for a right angle that needs no protractor: **$u \cdot v = 0$ exactly when $u$ and $v$ are perpendicular.**

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Shadows and similar words">

**Shadows.** Shine light onto $u$'s line so that it falls at right angles to the line. $v$ casts a shadow along it, and
its length is $\frac{u \cdot v}{|u|}$. With $u = (4, 0)$ and $v = (2, 3)$, that is $\frac{8}{4} = 2$: just $v$'s
across part, as you'd expect when $u$ lies along the x-axis.

<Explore slug="dot-product" settings={{ preset: 'shadow' }} input caption="v = (2, 3) casts a shadow 2 long on u's line. The rest of v, (0, 3), is at right angles to u. Drag u or v." />

On a tilted line it works the same way. With $u = (3, 1)$ and $v = (1, 2)$, $u \cdot v = 5$ and $|u| = 3.162$, so the
shadow is $5 / 3.162 = 1.581$ long. As an arrow along $u$, the shadow is
$\frac{u \cdot v}{u \cdot u}\,u = \frac{5}{10}(3, 1) = (1.5, 0.5)$. What is left of $v$,
$(1, 2) - (1.5, 0.5) = (-0.5, 1.5)$, has a dot product of 0 with $u$: it is at right angles. Later in the course,
splitting a vector into a part along a line and a part at right angles to it is how we'll find the best-fitting line
through data.

<Explore slug="dot-product" settings={{ preset: 'tilted' }} input caption="u = (3, 1), v = (1, 2): the shadow is 1.581 long, and the leftover part (−0.5, 1.5) is at right angles to u." />

Turn the shadow rule round and it says what the dot product measures: $u \cdot v = |u| \times$ the shadow of $v$, that
is, how far $v$ goes in $u$'s direction, scaled by $u$'s length.

**Similar words.** Language models store each word as a vector, and words used in similar ways get vectors pointing in
similar directions. The four word vectors below are toys, placed by hand to make the picture clear; real ones have
hundreds of parts and are learned from text. Which is most like *puppy* = (2, 1)?

<Explore slug="dot-product" settings={{ preset: 'similar' }} input caption="Rank the four toy words by dot product, then by direction. Drag puppy to move it." />

The dot product picks *elephant*, with 18, but only because its arrow is long: a long vector multiplies everything up.
Divide by both lengths and only the direction is left:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{u \cdot v}{|u|\,|v|}
$$

This **cosine similarity** is 1 for vectors pointing the same way, 0 for vectors at right angles and $-1$ for
opposites. *dog* points exactly along *puppy*, so its cosine is 1, while *elephant* drops to 0.949. *car* points more
than 90° away, so its cosine is negative.

The dot product mixes two things: how much the vectors point the same way, and how long they are. A long vector can
beat a short one that points exactly the right way. To compare direction alone, divide by both lengths and use the
cosine similarity.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- $u \cdot v = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + \dots + u_n v_n$: two vectors in, one number out. In code, one loop with a running
  total.
- $u \cdot v = |u|\,|v|\cos\theta$: positive below 90°, zero at a right angle, negative beyond. $u \cdot u = |u|^2$.
- $u \cdot v = 0$ means perpendicular. The shadow of $v$ on $u$'s line is $\frac{u \cdot v}{|u|}$ long (negative if it
  falls behind the origin).
- The dot product grows with length; the cosine similarity $\frac{u \cdot v}{|u|\,|v|}$ compares direction alone.

</Section>
