<Section step="hook" title="Making something out of nothing">

Image generators start every picture from pure static: random noise, different every time. Then, in a few dozen small
steps, the static turns into a face or a landscape. Nothing in the noise says "face"; the knowledge is in a network that
has learned one narrow skill: looking at something noisy and guessing which noise was added.

This lesson shows the whole idea on the smallest possible example: clouds of 2D points instead of pictures, so you can
watch every point move.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="forward process">Adding a little Gaussian noise at each step $t = 1, \dots, T$, until the data is indistinguishable from pure noise.</Term>
  <Term name="noise schedule">How much noise each step adds, $\beta_t$. Its running product $\bar\alpha_t = (1-\beta_1)\cdots(1-\beta_t)$ is how much of the original signal survives to step $t$.</Term>
  <Term name="denoiser">A neural network that takes a noisy point $x_t$ and the step $t$ and predicts the noise $\varepsilon$ in it.</Term>
  <Term name="reverse process">Generating: start from fresh noise and repeatedly remove a share of the predicted noise, from $t = T$ down to $1$.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Destroy the data, then rebuild it">

<Explore slug="diffusion" settings={{ preset: 'spiral', n: 80 }} caption="First the spiral is noised away into a round blob. Then fresh noise flows back onto the spiral, one small step at a time." />

The forward process needs no learning at all. Because Gaussian noise adds up to Gaussian noise, you can jump straight
to any step:

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
$$

As $t$ grows, $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ (the signal) falls towards 0 and $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ (the noise) rises
towards 1, so every shape ends as the same blob: a standard normal around the origin.

Training is a guessing game built on that formula: take a data point, pick a random $t$ and a random $\varepsilon$,
compute $x_t$, and ask the network for $\hat\varepsilon$. The loss is $\lVert \hat\varepsilon - \varepsilon \rVert^2$.
Our denoiser here is a small network with two hidden layers of 48 units, trained once when the site was built.

<KeyIdea>

**Predicting the noise is the same as predicting the clean point.** Rearranging the formula gives the denoiser's guess
of where the point came from, $\hat x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$. The
dashed arrow in the reverse process points at that guess.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Why so many steps?">

When the noise is large, the guess $\hat x_0$ is poor: a point that is mostly noise could have come from almost
anywhere on the shape, so the best guess is a blurry average of all of them, near the middle. Jumping straight to it
would land every point in the same place. Instead, each step moves only a little towards the guess and adds a little
fresh noise back; at the next step the point is slightly less noisy and the guess slightly better.

Try it with the controls below:

- **Change the seed:** the generated points are different every time, yet they always land on the shape. They are new
  samples, not copies of the training points.
- **Watch the arrow** on the ring: early on it swings around; near the end it is short and steady.
- **Compare the shapes:** the thin spiral is the hardest, and a few points end up between its arms.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- The forward process adds noise until data becomes $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$; the closed form jumps to any step.
- A network is trained to predict the added noise $\varepsilon$ from $x_t$ and $t$.
- Generating runs backwards from fresh noise: remove a share of the predicted noise, add a little fresh noise, repeat.
- Many small steps are needed because the denoiser's guess only sharpens as the noise fades.
- Image models do exactly this to every pixel at once, with a far bigger network that also reads a text prompt.

</Section>
