<Section step="hook" title="Why a server at 90% feels slow">

A server takes 100 ms to handle a request and is busy 90% of the time. It still has 10% to spare, so requests should take
about 100 ms, maybe a little more. In fact they take about **a second** on average, and the unluckiest 1 in 100 more than
four and a half seconds.

The reason is that requests don't arrive politely one after another. They arrive at random, so sometimes several land at
once and have to wait in line, and a busy server has little slack to work the line down. This lesson shows how a line
forms, why it explodes as the load nears 100%, why the slowest requests matter more than the average, and how a rate
limiter keeps a service out of that danger zone. It is the first tool of capacity planning, and of system design.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="arrival rate λ">How many requests arrive per second, on average. They come at random moments (a Poisson process), so they bunch up.</Term>
  <Term name="service rate μ">How many requests one server finishes per second, on average. Its mean service time is 1/μ: μ = 10 means 100 ms.</Term>
  <Term name="load ρ">The share of the servers' capacity the arrivals use: ρ = λ / (c·μ) for c servers. At 1 or more the line grows forever.</Term>
  <Term name="latency">Waiting in line plus being served. It is what a user feels.</Term>
  <Term name="p50 and p99">Percentiles of latency: half the requests are faster than p50, and 1 in 100 is slower than p99, the "tail".</Term>
  <Term name="token bucket">A rate limiter: a bucket of tokens that refills at a steady rate. Each request spends one, and an empty bucket turns requests away.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

One server, 100 ms per request on average, 8 requests a second: a load of 80%. Step through and watch the line in the top
row. Each bar below is one request: the lower part is time spent waiting, the upper part time being served.

<Explore slug="queueing" settings={{ preset: 'busy', n: '16', seed: '4' }} caption="One server at 80% load. The line comes and goes in bursts, and the requests that arrive during a burst wait far longer than they are served." />

<KeyIdea>

**Waiting grows like $1/(1-\rho)$.** For one server with random traffic the mean latency is $\frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}$, which is
the service time divided by the spare capacity $1-\rho$. At 50% load that is 2 times the service time, at 80% it is 5
times, at 90% 10 times and at 95% 20 times. Halving the spare capacity doubles the latency, so it explodes near 100%.

</KeyIdea>

A handy check on any of these numbers is **Little's law**: the average number of requests in the system equals the
arrival rate times the average latency, $L = \lambda W$. At 8 requests a second and 500 ms each, 4 requests are inside on
average. It holds for any queue, random or not.

</Section>

Utilisation and latency are not proportional. Random arrivals bunch up, and a busy server has little slack to work a line
down, so the wait grows like $1/(1-\rho)$: at 90% load a request waits about nine times its service time. Plan capacity so
the load stays well below 100%, often 60–70% for anything latency matters for.

</Misconception>

<Misconception
  id="average-is-enough"
  title="The average latency tells you what users feel"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'One server at 80% load serves requests in 100 ms on average, and its mean latency is 500 ms. Roughly what is its p99?',
      choices: ['500 ms', '600 ms', 'about 2.3 s', 'about 50 s'],
      answer: 2,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'For one server with random traffic, latency follows an exponential distribution, so p99 = ln(100) ≈ 4.6 times the mean: about 2.3 s.',
    },
    {
      q: 'A page makes 50 backend calls in parallel and waits for all of them. Each call is slower than its p99 one time in 100. How often is the page slow?',
      choices: ['About 1% of the time', 'About 40% of the time', 'Half of the time', 'Never, if the average call is fast'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'The page is fast only if all 50 calls are: 0.99⁵⁰ ≈ 0.6. So about 40% of page loads hit at least one slow call. Fan-out turns rare tails into common ones.',

A queue's latency has a long tail: most requests are quick, a few wait a long time. The average hides them, yet they are
real users, and a page that waits for many calls is only as fast as its slowest one. That is why latency targets are set on
p99 (or p99.9), not on the mean.

</Misconception>

<Section step="do" title="Protect it with a rate limit">

A service can only stay out of the danger zone if traffic can't push it there. A **token bucket** in front of it holds up
to a fixed number of tokens and refills at a steady rate. Each request spends a token; a request that finds the bucket
empty is turned away straight away instead of joining a line that would take seconds. The capacity decides how big a burst
gets through at once, and the refill rate caps the long run: in any $t$ seconds, at most capacity $+$ rate $\times t$ requests.

<Explore slug="token-bucket" settings={{ preset: 'bursty', n: '4', seed: '1' }} input caption="A bucket of 4 tokens refilling at 2 a second, hit by bursts of 7. Each burst's first four get through." />

<Misconception
  id="average-rate-enough"
  title="Limiting traffic to the average rate stops bursts and queues"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'A token bucket with capacity 10 that refills 2 tokens a second protects a service that handles 2 requests a second. After a quiet minute, 10 requests arrive at once. What happens?',
      choices: ['All 10 pass at once and queue at the service', 'Only 2 pass and 8 are rejected', 'They pass one every half second', 'All 10 are rejected'],
      answer: 0,
      trap: 2,
      why: 'After a quiet minute the bucket is full, so all 10 requests get a token at once. A token bucket passes bursts up to its capacity; it doesn’t space them out.',
    },
    {
      q: 'A full token bucket holds 4 tokens and refills 2 a second. At most how many requests can it let through in any 10 seconds?',
      choices: ['4', '20', '24', 'As many as arrive'],
      answer: 2,
      trap: 1,
      why: 'Its 4 tokens at once, plus 2 a second for 10 seconds: 4 + 20 = 24. The rate caps the long run; the capacity is a burst allowance on top.',
    },
  ]}
>

A token bucket caps the long-run rate but lets bursts of up to its capacity straight through, and random traffic below the
limit still queues at the service behind it. Set the rate comfortably below what the service can sustain, and the capacity
to the burst it can absorb without its line exploding.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Load is $\rho = \lambda / (c\mu)$. Random arrivals bunch up, so a line forms well before $\rho = 1$.
- For one server, mean latency is $1/(\mu - \lambda)$: the service time divided by the spare capacity. It explodes near 100%.
- Latency has a long tail. Set targets on p99, not the mean, especially when one page waits for many calls.
- Little's law, $L = \lambda W$, links the number inside, the arrival rate and the latency, for any queue.
- One shared line beats a line per server.
- A token bucket caps the long-run rate at its refill rate and lets bursts of up to its capacity through: keep the rate well
  under what the service can sustain.

</Section>
