<Section step="hook" title="The calculation AI hardware is built for">

A layer of a neural network mixes its inputs with a grid of numbers. A game turns a character and then stretches it,
and wants both moves done in one step. Both are **matrix multiplication**: combining two grids of numbers into a third.
Training and running a large language model is mostly this one operation, repeated billions of times, which is why the
chips that run AI are designed around it.

You already have the two ideas it needs. From the dot product: multiply matching parts and add. From the lesson on
matrices moving space: a matrix's columns are where î and ĵ land. This lesson puts them together, counts the cost, and
shows why the order of a product matters.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="m×n matrix">A grid of numbers with $m$ rows and $n$ columns, said “m by n”. Rows first, always. A square matrix has as many rows as columns.</Term>
  <Term name="entry Aᵢⱼ">The number in row $i$, column $j$ of $A$, counting from 1. (Code counts from 0, so $A_{12}$ is `A[0][1]`.)</Term>
  <Term name="product AB">The matrix $C$ whose entry $C_{ij}$ is row $i$ of $A$ dotted with column $j$ of $B$.</Term>
  <Term name="identity I">The square matrix with 1s down its diagonal and 0s elsewhere. It changes nothing: $AI = A$.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Each cell is a row times a column">

<Explore slug="matrix-multiplication" settings={{ preset: 'cells' }} caption="A is 2×3 and B is 3×2, so C = AB is 2×2. Each cell lights up the row of A and the column of B it is built from." />

$A$ has 2 rows and 3 columns; $B$ has 3 rows and 2 columns. To fill $C_{11}$, take row 1 of $A$, $(1, 2, 0)$, and
column 1 of $B$, $(2, 0, 1)$, and dot them:

$$
C_{11} = 1 \times 2 + 2 \times 0 + 0 \times 1 = 2
$$

Every other cell works the same way: $C_{12} = 7$, $C_{21} = 8$ and $C_{22} = -2$.

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 8 & -2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

For the row and the column to pair up, a row of $A$ must be as long as a column of $B$. That is the **shape rule**: an
$m \times n$ matrix times an $n \times p$ one gives an $m \times p$ result. The inner numbers must match, and they
disappear: $2 \times 3$ times $3 \times 2$ gives $2 \times 2$.

<KeyIdea>

**$C_{ij}$ is row $i$ of $A$ dotted with column $j$ of $B$.** So $(m \times n)(n \times p) = m \times p$, and if the
inner numbers differ, $AB$ doesn't exist.

</KeyIdea>

<Explore slug="matrix-multiplication" settings={{ preset: 'mismatch' }} caption="A 3×2 matrix times a 3×2 one: a row of A has 2 numbers and a column of B has 3, so they can't be paired." />

<Misconception
  id="cellwise"
  title="Multiply matrices by multiplying the matching cells"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'What is [[1, 2], [3, 4]] times the identity [[1, 0], [0, 1]]?',
      choices: ['[[1, 0], [0, 4]]', '[[1, 2], [3, 4]]', '[[2, 2], [3, 5]]'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Row 1 of A dotted with column 2 of I is 1 × 0 + 2 × 1 = 2, and so on: the identity changes nothing. Multiplying matching cells would wipe out the 2 and the 3.',
    },
    {
      q: 'Which numbers does C₂₁ of C = AB use?',
      choices: ['Only A₂₁ and B₂₁', 'All of row 2 of A and all of column 1 of B', 'All of column 2 of A and row 1 of B'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'C₂₁ is row 2 of A dotted with column 1 of B: every number in that row and that column.',
    },
  ]}
>

Adding matrices does work cell by cell, so it is natural to expect multiplying to work that way too. It doesn't: each
cell of $AB$ is a whole row of $A$ dotted with a whole column of $B$. That is why the shapes don't have to be the same,
only the inner numbers, and why multiplying by the identity leaves a matrix unchanged.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<PredictChallenge slug="matrix-multiplication" settings={{ preset: 'square' }} goal={5} />

The result is $AB = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: $A$ with its columns swapped.

**The cost.** Each cell took 2 multiplications and there are 4 cells, so 8 in all. In general $C$ has $m \times p$
cells and each takes $n$ multiplications: $m \cdot n \cdot p$. The first example took $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 12$. For two
$n \times n$ matrices it is $n^3$, so in Big-O terms the simple method is $O(n^3)$: doubling $n$ makes 8 times the work,
and two $1000 \times 1000$ matrices need a billion multiplications.

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Two moves in one matrix">

In the lesson on matrices moving space, a $2 \times 2$ matrix moved the whole plane, and its columns were where
$î$ and $ĵ$ landed. Applying $B$ and then $A$ to a vector $v$ gives $A(Bv)$, and the product $AB$ is the single matrix
that does both: $(AB)v = A(Bv)$. $B$ is the one next to $v$, so it acts first.

<Explore slug="matrix-multiplication" settings={{ preset: 'compose' }} input caption="B stretches across by 2, then A turns a quarter turn: î ends at (0, 2). Doing A first and then B sends î to (0, 1)." />

Here $B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ stretches the plane across by 2, and
$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ turns it a quarter turn anticlockwise. $B$ sends $î$ to its first
column, $(2, 0)$. Then $A$ moves that. Each part of $A(2, 0)$ is a row of $A$ dotted with $(2, 0)$:
$0 \times 2 + (-1) \times 0 = 0$ and $1 \times 2 + 0 \times 0 = 2$, so $î$ ends at $(0, 2)$, the first column of $AB$.
Every column of $AB$ is $A$ times a column of $B$, worked out with rows dotted with columns, which is exactly the
cell-by-cell rule:

$$
AB = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Now do them the other way round. $A$ first turns $î$ to $(0, 1)$, then $B$ stretches only the across direction, so
$(0, 1)$ stays put. $BA$ sends $î$ to $(0, 1)$, not $(0, 2)$: a different matrix, a different picture.

<Misconception
  id="ab-equals-ba"
  title="AB and BA are the same, as with ordinary numbers"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'A swaps the axes and B stretches the plane up by 3. AB sends î = (1, 0) to (0, 1). Where does BA send î?',
      choices: ['(0, 1)', '(0, 3)', '(3, 0)'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'BA means A first: î goes to (0, 1), then B stretches up by 3, giving (0, 3). AB stretched first, when î had nothing up to stretch.',
    },
    {
      q: 'A = [[1, 1], [0, 1]] and B = [[1, 0], [1, 1]], and AB = [[2, 1], [1, 1]]. What is BA?',
      choices: ['[[2, 1], [1, 1]]', '[[1, 1], [1, 2]]', '[[1, 0], [0, 1]]'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Row 1 of B, (1, 0), dotted with A’s columns gives (1, 1); row 2, (1, 1), gives (1, 2). BA ≠ AB.',
    },
  ]}
>

With ordinary numbers $3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3$, so it is easy to assume matrices behave the same. They usually don't:
$AB$ means “B, then A”, and doing two moves of the plane in the other order usually ends somewhere else. Some pairs do
agree (two turns, or anything with the identity), but that is the exception. Grouping is safe, though:
$(AB)C = A(BC)$, because both mean “C, then B, then A”. Swapping is not.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- $C_{ij}$ = row $i$ of $A$ · column $j$ of $B$. Shapes: $(m \times n)(n \times p) = m \times p$; inner numbers must match.
- It costs $m \cdot n \cdot p$ multiplications, $n^3$ for two $n \times n$ matrices.
- $(AB)v = A(Bv)$: $B$ acts first. Each column of $AB$ is $A$ times a column of $B$.
- $AB \neq BA$ in general, but $(AB)C = A(BC)$, and $AI = IA = A$ for an identity of the right size.

</Section>
