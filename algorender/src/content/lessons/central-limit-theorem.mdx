<Section step="hook" title="Why bell curves are everywhere">

Heights, measurement errors, exam averages and polling results all tend to follow the same bell-shaped curve, even though
the things being added up look nothing like a bell. A single die roll is flat; the average of many dice is a bell.

The **central limit theorem** says this is no coincidence: average enough independent samples of almost anything and the
averages pile up into a normal distribution, with a spread you can predict exactly.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="population">Where the samples come from, with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. It can have any shape.</Term>
  <Term name="sample of size n">$n$ independent draws from the population.</Term>
  <Term name="sample mean x̄">The average of one sample. Draw many samples and you get many means.</Term>
  <Term name="standard error">How spread out the means are: $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Pile up the averages">

<Explore slug="central-limit-theorem" settings={{ preset: 'skewed', n: '5' }} caption="Each sample of 5 is drawn from a lopsided population and averaged; the averages pile up in the bottom histogram." />

The population is lopsided, but the averages are not: big and small draws partly cancel, so averages land near $\mu$
more often than far from it, and they fall away symmetrically on both sides.

<KeyIdea>

**Averages of $n$ independent draws are roughly normal, centred on $\mu$, with spread $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.** The shape of
the population hardly matters once $n$ is moderately large. Quadrupling $n$ halves the spread.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Misconception title="Large samples make the data normal">

The theorem is about the **averages**, not the individual values. A skewed population stays skewed however many values
you collect; it is the distribution of their *mean* that becomes a bell.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- The means of samples of size $n$ cluster around $\mu$ with spread $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
- For moderate $n$ their distribution is close to normal, whatever the population looks like.
- This is why confidence intervals, A/B tests and polls can use the normal curve, and why averaging more data helps only like $\sqrt{n}$.

</Section>
