<Section step="hook" title="Does a new column add anything?">

A table of furniture has a column for each item's length in metres and another for the same length in centimetres.
That looks like two pieces of information, but the second column is just the first one times 100: it tells a model
nothing new. In a big table this kind of repeat is rarely so easy to spot. A column can be a mix of three others.

This lesson gives the tools for asking “what is really new here?”. The **span** of some vectors is everywhere their
combinations can reach. Vectors are **independent** when none of them is a mix of the others, and the **rank** of a
matrix counts how many independent directions its columns give. Elimination, from the last lesson, counts it for you.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="combination">Any $a \cdot u + b \cdot v$: some amount of $u$ plus some amount of $v$, where $a$ and $b$ are ordinary numbers (they can be negative or fractions).</Term>
  <Term name="span">Every point the combinations can reach. Written $\text{span}\{u, v\}$.</Term>
  <Term name="independent">No vector in the set is a combination of the others. Two vectors are independent when neither is a multiple of the other.</Term>
  <Term name="basis">A set of independent vectors that spans the whole space. Every point then has exactly one recipe $(a, b)$.</Term>
  <Term name="pivot">In elimination, the non-zero entry used to clear the entries below it. Each row holds at most one, and so does each column.</Term>
  <Term name="staircase (echelon) form">What elimination leaves: every entry below a pivot is 0, and each pivot sits to the right of the one above it, so the pivots step down like a staircase.</Term>
  <Term name="rank">How many independent directions a matrix's columns give. It equals the number of pivots elimination finds.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Two directions reach the plane">

<Explore slug="span" settings={{ preset: 'span' }} caption="Start with u = (2, 1) on its own, then add v = (1, 2) and look at every combination of the two. Where does w = (0, 3) fit in?" />

One vector reaches only a line: every multiple $a \cdot u$ of $u = (2, 1)$ lies on the line through the origin and $u$.
Add $v = (1, 2)$ and combine them. Two $u$'s and then one $v$ from its tip:

$$
2u + v = (4, 2) + (1, 2) = (5, 4)
$$

The whole-number combinations sit on a tilted grid, and fractions fill the gaps between its lines, so $u$ and $v$
together reach every point of the plane. To reach $w = (0, 3)$, find $a$ and $b$ with $a \cdot u + b \cdot v = w$.
That is one equation per part:

$$
\begin{aligned} 2a + b &= 0 \\ a + 2b &= 3 \end{aligned}
$$

Its answer is $a = -1$, $b = 2$: back one $u$, then forward two $v$'s, and $-u + 2v = (-2, -1) + (2, 4) = (0, 3)$.

Put $u$ and $v$ side by side as the columns of a matrix. A matrix times a vector $(a, b)$ is $a$ times its first
column plus $b$ times its second, which is exactly a combination:

$$
\begin{aligned} &\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} \\ &= a \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + b \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}
$$

So solving for the recipe is exactly the $Ax = b$ of the last lesson, with $u$ and $v$ as the columns of the matrix.
Its determinant is $\det\,[u\ v] = 3$, not 0, so there is exactly one answer.

<KeyIdea>

**Two vectors in the plane span all of it, unless one is a multiple of the other.** Then they are a **basis**: every
point has exactly one recipe $(a, b)$, and $\det\,[u\ v] \neq 0$.

</KeyIdea>

Now make $v$ a multiple of $u$. Here $v = (-2, -1)$, which is $-u$: the same line, pointing the other way.

<Explore slug="span" settings={{ preset: 'parallel' }} caption="u = (2, 1) and v = (−2, −1), with a target w = (1, 3). Predict where the combinations can reach before you step." />

Every $a \cdot u + b \cdot v$ is now just some amount of $u$, so the tilted grid collapses onto one line and
$\det\,[u\ v] = 0$. The point $w = (1, 3)$ is off that line, so no recipe reaches it. The closest point on the line is
$(2, 1)$, still $2.24$ away. Points that are on the line have many recipes instead of one, because some $u$ can always be
traded for some $v$. Two vectors, but only one direction: they are **dependent**, and they are not a basis.

Counting vectors is not counting directions. Two vectors give two directions only if neither is a multiple of the
other; if one is, every combination stays on a single line. The quick test for two vectors in the plane: put them side
by side as a matrix. $\det\,[u\ v] = 0$ means they lie on one line.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: rank by elimination">

With more than two vectors, or vectors with more than two parts, drawing stops working, and elimination takes over.
Put the vectors in a matrix $A$ as its columns. The rank of $A$ is how many independent directions those columns give,
and every pivot elimination finds is one of them. Swapping rows and subtracting a multiple of one row from another
never change the rank. The reason: a row operation does the same thing to every column at once. If column 2 is twice
column 1 before it, then after it column 2 is still twice column 1, so the columns keep exactly the same mixes.

<Explore slug="span" settings={{ preset: 'rank' }} caption="Elimination on a 3×3 matrix, column by column. Watch which columns get a pivot." />

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 3 \\ 3 & 6 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The first pivot is the 1 in the top-left corner. Subtracting $2$ times row 1 from row 2 leaves $(0, 0, 1)$, and
subtracting $3$ times row 1 from row 3 leaves $(0, 0, 2)$. Now column 2 has only 0s from row 2 down: no pivot. The
next pivot is in column 3, but in row 2: when a column has no pivot, the next pivot goes in the same row, one column
further right. So pivots step down a staircase and need not sit on the diagonal, as they did in the last lesson.
Clearing below it turns row 3 into $(0, 0, 0)$. Two pivots, so the rank is 2. The
column that missed out is the repeat: column 2 $= 2 \cdot$ column 1.

Now a matrix where the top-left entry is 0, so a row has to be swapped up first. This visualiser swaps up the first row
below with a non-zero entry, which keeps the numbers whole. The last lesson (and the exercise below) swaps up the row
with the biggest entry instead. Both find the same number of pivots, so both give the same rank. Before each step, predict
whether the next column has a pivot, and what multiple clears the entry below it.

<PredictChallenge slug="span" settings={{ preset: 'rank-full' }} goal={5} />

Row 1 started with a 0, which can't be a pivot, so elimination swapped in row 2 first. After that the pivots were
$1$, $2$ and $3.5$: one in every column, so the rank is 3 and all three columns are independent.

The number of columns is only the most the rank could be. Count the pivots after elimination: a column that gets no
pivot is a mix of the columns before it and adds no new direction. And the rank can never be more than the number of
rows either, since each row holds at most one pivot.

</Misconception>

Go back to the two vectors and drag them. Try these:

- Drag $v$ onto $u$'s line. The tilted grid collapses, $\det\,[u\ v]$ becomes 0 and the rank drops to 1.
- Drag $w$ around while $u$ and $v$ point different ways. Every place you put it has a recipe.
- Make $u$ and $v$ point nearly the same way. They are still a basis, but the recipes get large: small changes in $w$
  need big changes in $a$ and $b$.

î and ĵ are the most convenient basis, because the recipe for a point is just its coordinates. But any two independent
vectors are a basis: the tilted grid they make still covers the plane, and each point still sits at exactly one
crossing of it. Bases at right angles come back later in this stage, where they make projections simple.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A **combination** is $a \cdot u + b \cdot v$; the **span** is every point the combinations reach.
- Two vectors span the plane unless one is a multiple of the other, when $\det\,[u\ v] = 0$ and they reach only a line.
- A **basis** is independent and spans: every point has exactly one recipe. It need not be at right angles.
- **Rank** = independent directions = pivots after elimination. A column with no pivot is a mix of earlier ones.
- Next: **change of basis**, reading the same point with a different basis's recipe.

</Section>
