<Section step="hook" title="How far is “near”?">

A shop recommends things bought by customers like you. A spam filter compares a new email with emails it has seen. A
simple but strong kind of model, **nearest neighbours**, labels a new example by finding the training examples closest
to it. Grouping data into clusters works the same way. All of them need one thing first: a way to measure how far apart
two examples are.

On paper the answer seems obvious: a ruler. But a taxi in a city of square blocks can't drive through buildings, and a
chess king moves diagonally as cheaply as straight. Each is a different, sensible way to measure distance, and choosing
between them can change which example counts as nearest. This lesson builds three of them from the vectors you know.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="gap d = q − p">The vector from point $p$ to point $q$. Every distance below measures its size.</Term>
  <Term name="L2 (straight line)">$\sqrt{d_1^2 + d_2^2}$: Pythagoras, the length $|d|$ from the vectors lesson. Also called the Euclidean distance.</Term>
  <Term name="L1 (city blocks)">$|d_1| + |d_2|$: the sizes of the parts added up. Also called the Manhattan or taxicab distance.</Term>
  <Term name="L∞ (biggest step)">$\max(|d_1|, |d_2|)$, where max picks the larger number: only the biggest part counts. Said “L infinity”; the name is explained below.</Term>
  <Term name="norm">A way of measuring a vector's size. The distance from $p$ to $q$ is the norm of the gap $q - p$. Written with double bars and the ruler's name: $\lVert d \rVert_1$, $\lVert d \rVert_2$, $\lVert d \rVert_\infty$.</Term>
</Terms>

The bars $|d_1|$ around a single number mean its size without the sign: $|-4| = 4$.

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="One gap, three rulers">

<Explore slug="norms" settings={{ preset: 'distance' }} caption="p = (−2, −1) and q = (1, 3). Predict each distance before you step, then drag either point." />

From $p = (-2, -1)$ to $q = (1, 3)$ the gap is $d = q - p = (3, 4)$: 3 right and 4 up. Measured three ways:

- **L2**, the straight line: $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$. It is also $\sqrt{d \cdot d}$, since $d \cdot d$ adds the squares.
- **L1**, along the grid lines: $3 + 4 = 7$. Every route that never doubles back is this long.
- **L∞**, a king's steps: it steps diagonally, covering both directions at once, until the shorter one (3) runs out,
  then goes straight on, so it needs $\max(3, 4) = 4$ steps.

<KeyIdea>

**Measure the gap $d = q - p$, then pick a ruler: L2 is the straight line, L1 adds the parts' sizes, L∞ takes the
biggest.** For every gap, $L\infty \le L2 \le L1$, and all three agree when the gap runs along one axis.

</KeyIdea>

Why that order? The straight line is the shortest route on paper, so L1's grid route can't beat L2. And the straight
line is the long side of a right-angled triangle whose short sides are $|d_1|$ and $|d_2|$, so it is at least as long
as the longer of them, which is L∞. With more parts nothing changes: add up all the squares, all the sizes, or take the
biggest of them all.

A gap of $-3$ means “3 the other way”, which is still 3 away. If the signs are left in, a step right and a step left
cancel, and two different points can come out at distance 0. Every distance measures the parts' sizes: L1 adds
$|d_i|$, L2 squares (which hides the sign anyway), and L∞ takes the largest $|d_i|$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: which point is nearest?">

A query point $q = (0, 0)$ and two training points, $A = (3, 0)$ and $B = (2, 2)$. Before each step, predict which is
nearer to $q$ under that ruler.

<PredictChallenge slug="norms" settings={{ preset: 'nearest' }} goal={3} />

By the straight line, $B$ is nearer: $\sqrt{8} = 2.828$ against 3. Along the grid, $A$ wins, 3 against 4. A king picks $B$
again, 2 against 3. Same three points, a different nearest neighbour, so a nearest-neighbour model could give $q$ a
different label depending on the ruler.

  title="The nearest point is the same whichever distance you use"

    },
    {
      q: 'A nearest-neighbour model is switched from L2 to L1 distance. Can its answer for a new point change?',
      choices: ['No: the nearest training point stays the same', 'Yes: a different training point can become nearest'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'The rulers rank points differently when gaps are diagonal, so the nearest training point, and its label, can change.',

The rulers agree about gaps along one axis but rank diagonal gaps differently: L1 punishes them most and L∞ least. So
whenever the candidates lie in different directions, the nearest one can change with the ruler. Choosing the distance is
part of designing the model, not a detail.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Unit balls, then code">

Collect every point at distance 1 or less from the origin and you get the ruler's **unit ball** (in three dimensions,
L2's is a real ball). The pictures draw its outline, the points at distance exactly 1. Before you step, guess the shape
of each ruler's outline.

<Explore slug="norms" settings={{ preset: 'balls' }} input caption="Predict each outline's shape, then drag t across them and watch which ones it leaves." />

Under L2 the outline is the circle of radius 1. Under L1 the points with $|x| + |y| = 1$ make a diamond, with its
corners on the axes. Under L∞ the points whose bigger part is 1 make a square. The point $t = (0.6, 0.6)$ has L1 length
1.2, L2 0.849 and L∞ 0.6: it is outside the diamond ($0.6 + 0.6 = 1.2$), inside the circle
($\sqrt{0.72} \approx 0.849$) and inside the square.

All three belong to one family, $L_p = (|x|^p + |y|^p)^{1/p}$. The power $\frac{1}{p}$ means the $p$-th root, so
$p = 2$ gives the square root of $x^2 + y^2$, which is L2, and $p = 1$ gives L1. As $p$ grows, the diamond puffs out
into the circle and on towards the square, and as $p$ grows without limit the formula ends up picking just the biggest
part: that is why the biggest-step ruler is called L∞. Later in the course, the diamond's sharp corners explain why one
training trick switches some of a model's inputs off completely.

$|a_i - b_i|$ are the parts of $d$, as before.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- The distance from $p$ to $q$ is the norm of the gap $d = q - p$: $\lVert d \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum d_i^2} = \sqrt{d \cdot d}$,
  $\lVert d \rVert_1 = \sum |d_i|$, $\lVert d \rVert_\infty = \max |d_i|$.
- Drop the signs: a gap of $-3$ is still 3 away.
- $L\infty \le L2 \le L1$ always. Their unit balls: L∞ a square, L2 a circle, L1 a diamond.
- The nearest point can change with the ruler, so choosing the distance is part of designing a model.

</Section>
