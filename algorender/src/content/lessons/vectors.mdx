<Section step="hook" title="A short list of numbers">

A game moves a character 3 steps right and 2 steps up every second. A weather station records each day as a
temperature and a rainfall. A machine learning model sees a house as its number of rooms, its floor area and its age.
In each case one thing is described by a short list of numbers, and the same few things keep being done to those lists:
add two of them, stretch one, measure how big one is.

A list of numbers used this way is called a **vector**. This lesson draws vectors as arrows, so that adding, stretching
and measuring become things you can see, and then treats data points as vectors too.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="vector">A move written as a list of numbers, such as $v = (3, 2)$: 3 across, then 2 up. In general $v = (x, y)$. Drawn as an arrow from a tail to a tip.</Term>
  <Term name="parts (components)">The numbers in the list. $v_1 = 3$ is the first part of $v$ and $v_2 = 2$ the second.</Term>
  <Term name="scalar">An ordinary single number, such as $2$ or $-1.5$, used to scale (stretch) a vector. Written $k$.</Term>
  <Term name="length |v|">How far the move takes you in a straight line, read “the length of v”. For a single number the same bars mean the size without the sign, $|-3| = 3$, so this is the same idea for a vector.</Term>
  <Term name="origin">The point $(0, 0)$, where an arrow is usually drawn from.</Term>
</Terms>

The pictures here are flat, so every vector has two parts. Nothing below depends on that: a vector can have three parts,
or a hundred, and every rule works part by part in the same way.

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="An arrow is a move, not a place">

<Explore slug="vectors" settings={{ preset: 'arrow' }} caption="v = (3, 2) drawn from the origin, then the same move made from P = (−2, 1). Drag the tip or the start." />

Drawn from the origin, $v = (3, 2)$ ends on the point $(3, 2)$, so it is easy to think of a vector as that point. But
start the same move at $P = (-2, 1)$ instead: go 3 right and 2 up and you land at $Q = (1, 3)$. The arrow from $P$ to
$Q$ is the same vector $v$. A vector records only how far and which way, never where it is drawn.

That gives a rule for the arrow between two points: subtract the start from the end, part by part.

$$
Q - P = (1, 3) - (-2, 1) = (3, 2)
$$

Going back the other way, $P - Q = (-3, -2)$: the same length, pointing the opposite way. We write it $-v$.

<KeyIdea>

**A vector is a move: so far across, so far up.** The same move drawn anywhere is the same vector, and the vector from
a point $P$ to a point $Q$ is $Q - P$.

</KeyIdea>

<Misconception
  id="vector-is-a-point"
  title="A vector is just a point"

      answer: 1,

    },
    {
      q: 'What is the vector from P = (2, 5) to Q = (3, 1)?',
      choices: ['(3, 1)', '(1, −4)', '(5, 6)'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Subtract the start from the end: Q − P = (3 − 2, 1 − 5) = (1, −4). (3, 1) is where the arrow ends, not the move.',
    },
  ]}
>

A point says **where** something is. A vector says **which way and how far** to go. They are written the same way,
which is why they get mixed up, and an arrow drawn from the origin does end on the point with the same numbers. But
move the arrow and its numbers don't change, because the move doesn't. To get the vector between two points, subtract:
end minus start.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: add two moves">

Adding two vectors means making one move and then the other. Predict each answer before you take the next step.

<PredictChallenge slug="vectors" settings={{ preset: 'add' }} goal={3} />

To add, put the tail of $v$ on the tip of $u$ (**tip to tail**) and see where you end. In numbers, add the matching
parts:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u + v &= (3, 1) + (1, 2) \\
&= (3 + 1,\ 1 + 2) = (4, 3)
\end{aligned}
$$

Either order lands in the same place, $u + v = v + u$: the two routes go round opposite halves of a parallelogram and
meet at the same corner. Subtracting works part by part too: $u - v = (2, -1)$, the arrow from $v$'s tip to $u$'s tip.

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Scale, measure, and treat data as vectors">

**Scaling.** Multiplying a vector by a scalar $k$ multiplies every part by $k$, written $kv$. $2v$ is the same move made
twice over: the same direction, twice as long. $0.5v$ is half as long, and a negative $k$ also flips the arrow round.

<Explore slug="vectors" settings={{ preset: 'scale' }} input caption="v = (2, 1). Then 2v, then −v, then −1.5v = (−3, −1.5): every multiple of v lies on one line through the origin." />

**Measuring.** The length of $v = (3, 4)$ is the long side of a right-angled triangle whose short sides are 3 across
and 4 up, so by Pythagoras:

$$
|v| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5
$$

<Explore slug="vectors" settings={{ preset: 'length' }} input caption="|v| = 5. Doubling v doubles its length; −v is just as long; and v divided by 5 is (0.6, 0.8), a vector of length 1." />

For any $v = (x, y)$ the length is $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$. The squares hide any minus signs, so a length is never negative:
$|-v| = |v|$. Dividing $v$ by its own length, which means multiplying it by $\frac{1}{5}$ (divide each part by 5),
gives a **unit vector** of length 1 that keeps only the direction: $(3, 4) / 5 = (0.6, 0.8)$. It lands on the unit
circle from the sine and cosine lesson.

<Misconception
  id="lengths-add"
  title="The length of u + v is the length of u plus the length of v"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'u = (3, 0) and v = (0, 4). How long is u + v?',
      choices: ['7', '5', '12'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'u + v = (3, 4), whose length is √(9 + 16) = 5. Going 3 right and then 4 up is 7 steps of walking, but it ends only 5 from the start.',
    },
    {
      q: 'u = (2, 0) and v = (−2, 0). How long is u + v?',
      choices: ['4', '0', '2'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'v undoes u: u + v = (0, 0), the zero vector, of length 0.',
    },
  ]}
>

Lengths only add up when the two arrows point the same way. Otherwise the second move partly cancels the first, or
goes off at an angle, and the sum is shorter. With $u = (3, 1)$ and $v = (1, 2)$, the lengths are $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.162$
and $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$, so $|u| + |v| = 3.162 + 2.236 = 5.398$, but $u + v = (4, 3)$ has length 5. Add the vectors
part by part first, then measure.

</Misconception>

**Data as vectors.** A dataset is a pile of vectors. Here three students each have a maths score and an art score, so
each student is a vector. The difference $b - a$ says how two students differ, score by score, and its length says how
different they are overall. The average student is the sum of all the vectors, scaled by $\frac{1}{3}$:

<Explore slug="vectors" settings={{ preset: 'data' }} input caption="The three students add, tip to tail, to (9, 9); a third of that is the mean, (3, 3). Drag a student and the mean follows." />

That is the mean from the Σ lesson, $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}(x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n)$, where each $x_i$ is now a whole
vector instead of a single number. Adding and scaling are all it takes.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A vector is a move, a list of numbers with a length and a direction. The vector from $P$ to $Q$ is $Q - P$.
- Add part by part (tip to tail): $u + v = v + u$. Subtract part by part too.
- $kv$ multiplies every part by $k$: it stretches, shrinks or (for negative $k$) flips the arrow, along one line.
- $|v| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, never negative. $|u + v|$ is never more than $|u| + |v|$, and equal only when the two point
  the same way. $v / |v|$ has length 1.
- Data points are vectors, so their mean is “add them all, then scale by $\frac{1}{n}$”.

</Section>
