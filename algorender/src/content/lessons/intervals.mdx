<Section step="hook" title="Busy when, and in how many rooms">

A team's shared calendar holds the day's bookings: 9–11, 10–12 and 14–15. Two questions keep coming up. When is
somebody busy? 9–11 and 10–12 run into each other, so the busy times are 9–12 and 14–15. And how many meeting
rooms do the bookings need, if a room holds one meeting at a time?

Each booking is an **interval**: a start and an end. Comparing every pair to find the clashes takes n(n − 1)/2
comparisons, about 50 million for 10,000 bookings. Both have a faster answer that starts the same way: sort the
intervals by start, in O(n log n) with [merge sort](/learn/merge-sort). Then one pass merges the busy times, and
one pass with a [heap](/learn/heaps) counts the rooms.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="interval">A start and an end, written [s, e] or s–e, with the start before the end. The visualisers letter them A, B, C… in input order.</Term>
  <Term name="overlap">Two intervals overlap when each starts before the other ends. Whether two that only touch, like [1, 4] and [4, 5], count as overlapping is each problem's choice.</Term>
  <Term name="nested">One interval lies inside another: it starts no earlier and ends no later, like [2, 3] inside [1, 5].</Term>
  <Term name="sweep">One pass over the intervals in order of start, carrying a little state: the current merged interval, or the time each room becomes free.</Term>
  <Term name="min-heap of end times">A heap holding, for each room opened so far, the time it is next free, with the earliest on top: the room that frees up first.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Sort by start, then look back once">

Merge Intervals turns every group of overlapping intervals into one. Here intervals that touch count as overlapping:
[1, 4] and [4, 5] leave no gap between them.

<Explore slug="merge-intervals" settings={{ preset: 'touching' }} caption="Sorting puts B [1, 4] first, then A [4, 5], D [6, 7], E [7, 9] and C [9, 12]. A touches B at 4, so the merged interval grows to [1, 5]. D starts at 6, after 5, so [1, 5] is final and D opens [6, 7]. E touches it at 7 and stretches it to [6, 9], and C touches that at 9: 5 intervals become [1, 5] and [6, 12]." />

Why is one look back enough? The merged intervals come out in order and apart, each ending before the one after it
begins. The interval being checked starts no earlier than the last merged one, which began after every earlier one had
ended, so the last is the only one it can reach. And once an interval starts after the last merged end, that merged
interval is final: everything still to come starts later too.

The second question is **meeting rooms**: the fewest rooms that hold every meeting, never two at once in one room.
Activity selection in [greedy algorithms](/learn/greedy) asked the reverse: with one room, keep the most meetings, by
earliest finish. Here every meeting is held: meetings get rooms in order of start, and a min-heap holds each room's
end time. A room is free again the moment its meeting ends.

<Explore slug="meeting-rooms" settings={{ preset: 'textbook' }} caption="Sorted by start: A, C, B, E, D, F. A holds Room 1 until 15, so C, at 2, opens Room 2. B starts at 5 as C ends and reuses Room 2. At 6, E finds the heap’s top, Room 2, busy until 8, so Room 3 opens. D and F take the rooms freed at 8 and 10: 3 rooms, since at time 6 A, B and E are all in progress." />

Only the top of the heap matters: if the room that frees up first is still busy, every room is. And a room opens only
when every room's meeting has started and not yet ended, so at that moment one more meeting than there are rooms is
running. The rooms never outnumber the most meetings running at once, and no schedule can use fewer: each of those
meetings needs its own room.

<KeyIdea>

**Sorted by start, each interval needs one comparison: with the last merged interval, or with the room that frees up
first.** A merge stretches the last end to the larger of the two ends, or starts a new interval. A meeting reuses
the room on top of a min-heap of end times if that room is free by its start, or opens a new one, and the rooms
opened equal the most meetings running at once. Both are O(n log n): the sort, then O(1) per interval to merge, or
O(log n) per meeting for the heap.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="The merge in code, and three ways to miscount">

Here is the merge as a function, on intervals already sorted by start. `merged` starts with a copy of the first
interval, so the input never changes. `last` is a second name for the last merged interval, not a copy, so writing
`last[1]` changes the interval inside `merged`: two arrows point at the same list.

<TraceCode
  snippet="merge-sorted-intervals"
  question="When the loop reaches [4, 8], it compares 4 with `last[1]`. What is `last[1]` then?"
  choices={['3', '5', '8', '4']}
  answer={1}
  why="[2, 3] lies inside [1, 5], so `max(5, 3)` left the end at 5. 4 ≤ 5, so [4, 8] stretches it to 8, and the program prints [1, 8] [10, 12]. With `last[1] = end`, the end would have dropped to 3 and [4, 8] would have started its own interval."
/>

[2, 3] is the case that catches people out: it starts after [1, 5] but ends earlier. Here one interval contains
all the others.

Sorting by start orders the starts, not the ends: [1, 12] comes before [2, 4]. Setting the end to x.end shrinks the
merged interval whenever x is nested, and later intervals then look separate. Take max(last.end, x.end): a later
end stretches the merged interval, and a nested one leaves it alone. Test a merge on a nested interval followed by
one that overlaps only the outer one.

</Misconception>

Rooms have a rule about touching too: Meeting Rooms reuses a room whose end time is no later than the next start.

Touching is each problem's choice, and the code shows it in one character: `<=` or `<`. Merge Intervals joins [1, 4]
and [4, 5], which leave no gap, as does [Merge Overlapping Intervals](/practice/merge-intervals). Meeting Rooms lets

The last trap mixes the two questions up. Merging says when someone is busy, not how many rooms that takes:

<Explore slug="meeting-rooms" settings={{ input: '1-4, 3-6, 5-8, 7-10, 9-12' }} caption="Each meeting overlaps the next, so merged they would make one block, 1–12. But A frees Room 1 at 4, in time for C at 5, and B frees Room 2 at 6, in time for D at 7, so the rooms take turns: two rooms, because no more than two meetings ever run at once." />

<Misconception

  title="A block of overlapping meetings needs a room for each meeting"
  probes={[

      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Overlapping doesn’t pass along a chain. With A 2–6, B 5–9 and C 8–12, A ends at 6, before C starts at 8, so A and C share a room and two rooms are enough.',
    },
    {
      q: 'What number does the meeting rooms sweep compute?',
      choices: ['How many meetings overlap at least one other', 'The most meetings in progress at one moment', 'How many busy blocks the meetings merge into'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'A room opens only when every room is busy at a meeting’s start, so the count is the most meetings running at once. A chain of overlaps can need just two rooms.',

Overlapping doesn't pass along a chain: A overlaps B and B overlaps C, yet A may end before C starts. A merged block
says only that the busy time has no gap. The rooms needed are the most meetings running at any one moment: anything
from 1, for meetings that only touch, up to the number of meetings in the block.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Two intervals overlap when each starts before the other ends; whether touching counts is the problem's choice,
  one character in the code.
- Sorted by start, an interval can only overlap the last merged one, so merging is one pass: extend the end to
  max(last.end, x.end), not x.end, since a nested interval ends earlier; or start a new interval. O(n log n), for the
  sort.
- Meeting rooms: in start order, reuse the top of a min-heap of end times if that room is free by the next start, and
  open one otherwise. O(n log n). The rooms are the most meetings running at once, not the size of a merged block.
- Next: [greedy algorithms](/learn/greedy), where sorting by finish keeps the most intervals in one room. Practise
  with [Merge Overlapping Intervals](/practice/merge-intervals),
  [Insert a Range into a Sorted List](/practice/insert-interval) and [Fewest Meeting Rooms](/practice/meeting-rooms-ii),
  or open the [merge intervals](/visualisers/merge-intervals) and [meeting rooms](/visualisers/meeting-rooms)
  visualisers.

</Section>
