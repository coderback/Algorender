<Section step="hook" title="How many servers does it take?">

A maps app has 200 million daily users. Before anyone draws boxes and arrows, someone has to ask: how many requests a
second is that? A hundred? A million? The answer decides everything after it. A hundred fits on one machine, while a million needs a
fleet, caches and careful sharding.

You don't need a spreadsheet to answer. A few known facts, round numbers and a minute of arithmetic get you to the right
**power of ten**, and that is all the first sketch of a design needs. This is back-of-the-envelope estimation (also called
Fermi estimation, after the physicist who was famous for it). It is the first step of every system-design interview and of
real capacity planning, and this lesson gives you the method, the numbers to remember and practice.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will use">

<Terms>
  <Term name="daily active users (DAU)">How many people use the product on a typical day. Most estimates start here.</Term>
  <Term name="QPS">Queries (requests) per second. Traffic per day divided by the seconds in a day gives the average.</Term>
  <Term name="peak factor">How much busier the busiest time is than the daily average: often 2 to 10 times, more during a launch or a big event.</Term>
  <Term name="order of magnitude">The power of ten a number is closest to. 23,000 and 40,000 are both about 10^4; an estimate aims to get this right.</Term>
  <Term name="headroom">Spare capacity on purpose: servers planned to run well below 100% at the peak, because latency explodes near full load.</Term>
  <Term name="hot set">The small share of data that gets most of the traffic. Often about 20% of the keys get 80% of the reads, so a cache only needs the hot set.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="From users to requests a second">

Start with what you know and multiply your way to what you need, keeping the units in sight:

- **Requests a day** = daily users × requests each. 200 million users × 10 tiles = 2 × 10^9 requests a day.
- **Requests a second** = requests a day ÷ seconds in a day. A day has 86,400 seconds; call it **10^5**. So 2 × 10^9 ÷ 10^5 = 2 × 10^4: about **20,000 requests a second**. (The exact figure, 23,100, is the same power of ten.)

<KeyIdea>

**Work in powers of ten.** Round every input to one digit times a power of ten, multiply the digits and add the exponents.
The inputs are guesses anyway ("about 10 requests each"), so extra digits in the answer are false precision. An estimate within
a factor of 2 is excellent, and within a factor of 10 it still points to the right design.

</KeyIdea>

Try one. Type your estimate in any form you like (`23000`, `23k`, `2e4`), then compare your working with the steps.

<Section step="predict" title="Why not run servers at 100%?">

Once you know the traffic, it is tempting to divide by what one server can do and stop there. But a server planned at 100%
of its capacity has no slack to work a line down. Here one server is 95% busy: its service time is 100 ms, yet a request
takes about **2 seconds** on average, 20 times as long. The player stops before arrivals and asks whether each one will wait.

<PredictChallenge slug="queueing" settings={{ preset: 'full', n: '16', seed: '7' }} goal={4} />

That is why capacity estimates divide by a **target** load, often 50–70% of what a server can do flat out, and then add a
spare or two so the service survives a machine failing at the peak.

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Peaks and servers">

Traffic follows the clock: evenings, Monday mornings, a big match or a launch. Size the system for its **peak**. Find the
average, multiply by a peak factor, then divide by what one server can do at its target load.

    {
      q: 'A system serves 864 million requests a day, and its busiest hour runs at 3 times the daily average. What rate must it handle?',
      choices: ['About 10,000 a second', 'About 30,000 a second', 'About 36 million a second', 'About 864 million a second'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: '864 million ÷ 86,400 = 10,000 a second on average, and the busiest hour is 3 times that: 30,000 a second.',
    },
  ]}
>

The daily average hides the busy hours, and that is exactly when users notice a slow service. Find the average, multiply by a
peak factor (2 to 10 is typical), then divide by what one server handles at a comfortable load, not flat out. Add spare
servers so that losing one at the peak still leaves enough.

</Misconception>

<Section step="do" title="Storage and bandwidth">

Storage is items × size × time, then × the number of copies kept. Bandwidth is requests a second × bytes per response.
Remember the prefixes: 10^3 is kilo, 10^6 mega, 10^9 giga, 10^12 tera and 10^15 peta. And networks are sold in bits:
multiply bytes by 8.

<Section step="do" title="Latency numbers, and the hot set">

Every design is built on a few physical facts, which between them span about six powers of ten. They come from Jeff Dean's well-known list, and exact values vary with the hardware:

- a main-memory reference takes about **100 ns**;
- a random read from an SSD takes about **100 µs**, a thousand times slower;
- a round trip within a data centre takes about **0.5 ms**;
- a disk seek takes about **10 ms**;
- a round trip from California to Europe and back takes about **150 ms**.

The [estimation drills page](/design/estimate) has the full table.

<EstimationDrill kind="latency" seed={2} />

These gaps are why caches exist. Reads are skewed (often 80% of them go to 20% of the keys), so keeping the hot 20% in memory
answers most reads in microseconds instead of milliseconds.

A memory reference is about a thousand times faster than a random SSD read and a hundred thousand times faster than a disk
seek, and a round trip across an ocean is slower still: these are powers of ten apart, not a few times. A design
that needs several disk seeks or cross-ocean round trips per request can't answer in a few milliseconds, however many
servers it has. Count the slow steps on the request's path first; that is where caches, indexes and data placement earn
their keep.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Requests a second = daily users × requests each ÷ 10^5 (a day is 86,400 seconds).
- Size for the **peak**, not the average, and plan servers at 50–70% of their capacity, with spares.
- Storage = items × size × time × copies; bandwidth = requests a second × bytes each (× 8 for bits).
- Memory (~100 ns), SSD (~100 µs), a data-centre round trip (~0.5 ms), a disk seek (~10 ms) and an ocean crossing (~150 ms)
  span about six powers of ten. Count the slow steps on a request's path.
- Round to one digit and a power of ten. Within 2× is excellent; within 10× still picks the right design.
- More practice: the [estimation drills](/design/estimate).

</Section>
