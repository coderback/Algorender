<Section step="hook" title="How alike are two words?">

Type “recieve” and a spell checker offers “receive”. A diff tool lines up two versions of a file and shows only the
lines that changed. Both are asking how alike two sequences are: what they share, in order, and how few changes turn
one into the other.

Trying every answer is hopeless: a word of m letters has $2^m$ subsequences, about a million for 20 letters and a
trillion for 40. The [dynamic programming lesson](/learn/dynamic-programming) filled a table of smaller answers
instead. With two sequences, the smaller answers are about **prefixes**: the first i letters of one and the first j of
the other. One table holds every pair, and each cell costs a single comparison.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="subsequence and substring">A subsequence keeps some letters in their order and may skip the rest: “hat” is a subsequence of “chart”. A substring is a run of consecutive letters, such as “har”.</Term>
  <Term name="prefix">A[1..i], the first i letters of A, counted from 1. The empty prefix, ε, has none; row 0 and column 0 stand for it.</Term>
  <Term name="L[i,j] and D[i,j]">What one cell means. L[i,j] is the length of the longest common subsequence (LCS) of A[1..i] and B[1..j]; D[i,j] is the fewest edits that turn A[1..i] into B[1..j].</Term>
  <Term name="edit distance">The fewest single-letter edits (insert, delete or replace) that turn one word into another, also called the Levenshtein distance.</Term>
  <Term name="traceback">A walk from the table's corner back to its edge, following the neighbour each cell was built from, to recover the letters or edits themselves.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="One comparison and three neighbours">

For the longest common subsequence, the four decisions of dynamic programming are:

- **State:** L[i,j] is the length of the LCS of A[1..i] and B[1..j].
- **Base cases:** an empty prefix shares nothing, so row 0 and column 0 are all 0.
- **Recurrence:** look only at the last letters, A[i] and B[j]. If they match, that letter can end a common
  subsequence, so extend the best for the shorter prefixes: L[i,j] = L[i−1,j−1] + 1. If they differ, at most one of
  them can end it, so leave out A[i] (the cell above) or B[j] (the cell to the left) and keep the better:
  L[i,j] = max(L[i−1,j], L[i,j−1]).
- **Fill order:** a cell reads the cells above, to the left and diagonally up-left, so fill row by row, left to right.

<Explore slug="longest-common-subsequence" settings={{ preset: 'similar' }} caption="DYNAMIC against PANORAMIC: 63 cells, one comparison each. The corner reaches 5, and the walk back reads NAMIC. Its letters sit side by side in DYNAMIC, but in PANORAMIC the walk steps left past R and O, letters the subsequence skips." />

The answer is the corner, L[m,n], the one cell that covers both whole words. The letters come from the walk back: a
match steps diagonally and keeps its letter; otherwise the walk steps to the larger neighbour (up on a tie).

<KeyIdea>

**To compare two sequences, let each cell answer the question for a pair of prefixes: the first i items of one and
the first j of the other. Only the last item of each prefix is new, so a cell is one comparison plus a look at three
neighbours: above, left and diagonally up-left. Row 0 and column 0 are the empty prefixes, the answer is the
corner, and the (m + 1) × (n + 1) table takes O(mn) time.**

</KeyIdea>

With a different rule, the same table gives the edit distance, D[i,j]. Turning i letters into nothing takes i
deletes, and building j letters from nothing takes j inserts, so row 0 and column 0 count up from 0. For any other cell, think about the **last** edit of a cheapest script:

- **Delete A[i]**, after turning A[1..i − 1] into B[1..j]: the cell above, plus 1.
- **Insert B[j]**, after turning A[1..i] into B[1..j − 1]: the cell to the left, plus 1.
- **Replace A[i] with B[j]**, after turning A[1..i − 1] into B[1..j − 1]: the diagonal, plus 1.

D[i,j] is the smallest of the three. When A[i] = B[j] there is nothing to replace, so the cell copies the diagonal,
which the other two can never beat.

</Section>

makes a table of zeros, then fills it from row 1 and column 1.

The table has (m + 1)(n + 1) cells at O(1) each: O(mn) time, for edit distance too. Each row reads only itself and the
row above, so for the number alone two rows are enough: O(n) memory. The walk back can pass through any row, so
recovering the letters or edits keeps the whole table. (Hirschberg's algorithm avoids that, at about twice the
work.)

The word **subsequence** matters: DYNAMIC and PANORAMIC share NAMIC, five letters, as a subsequence, but only AMIC,
four, as an unbroken run.

A subsequence keeps the order but may skip letters; a substring skips nothing. So the LCS is at least as long as the
longest common substring, and often longer. On a mismatch the LCS table keeps the better neighbour, where a table of
common substrings drops back to 0 because the run is broken. The [string matching lesson](/learn/string-matching)
looks for substrings; diff tools and DNA alignment want subsequences.

</Misconception>

Edit distance has a tempting shortcut too: count the positions where the letters differ.

<Misconception
  id="position-by-position"
  title="Edit distance counts the positions where two words differ"
  probes={[
    {

      choices: ['5', '1', '4'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,

    },
    {
      q: 'Two words of the same length differ at all 6 of their positions. What can you say about their edit distance?',
      choices: ['It is exactly 6', 'It is at most 6, and can be much smaller', 'It is at least 6'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Six replacements always work, so it is at most 6, but a delete and an insert can shift letters into line: “banana” and “ananab” differ at every position, and their distance is 2.',
    },
  ]}
>

Counting the positions that differ (the Hamming distance) only replaces letters. An insert or a delete shifts every
later letter one place, which is often cheaper: “stop” and “tops” differ at all four positions, but deleting the s and
adding one at the end takes 2. Every path from D[0,0] to the corner is one way of lining the words up, and the table
keeps the cheapest.

</Misconception>

One sequence can be compared with itself, too. A **longest increasing subsequence** (LIS) keeps values that strictly
rise from left to right. Whether v[i] can extend a run depends on the value the run ends with, so dp[i] is the length
of the longest increasing run that ends **exactly at** v[i]: 1 more than the largest dp[j] over earlier positions j
with a smaller value, or 1 if there is none. Each position looks back at every earlier one: n(n − 1)/2 comparisons,
O(n²).

<Explore slug="longest-increasing-subsequence" settings={{ input: '3, 8, 4, 9, 6, 1, 2' }} caption="dp comes out 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 1, 2. At the 9, the runs ending at 8 and at 4 tie at length 2, and the first found, 8, is kept. The largest entry, 3, comes first at dp[3], and prev leads back through 8 to 3: the answer is 3, 8, 9, though the last entry, dp[6], is only 2. 21 comparisons." />

<Misconception
  id="last-entry-is-answer"
  title="The answer is the last entry, dp[n − 1]"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'For 4, 7, 9, 2, dp is 1, 2, 3, 1. How long is the longest increasing subsequence?',
      choices: ['1', '3', '4'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'dp[3] = 1 only says the best run ending at the 2 is the 2 alone. The best run, 4, 7, 9, ends at index 2: the answer is the largest entry, 3.',
    },
    {
      q: 'Why does the LCS read its answer from the corner cell, while LIS takes the largest entry?',
      choices: [
        'L[m,n] covers both whole words, but dp[i] counts only runs ending exactly at v[i], and the best run can end anywhere',
        'LIS fills its table from right to left',
        'They don’t differ: in both, the answer is always the last cell',
      ],
      answer: 0,
      trap: 2,
      why: 'Every common subsequence of the two whole words counts in L[m,n]. dp[n − 1] counts only runs that end at the last value; one that ends earlier is counted in its own cell.',
    },
  ]}
>

dp[i] answers a narrower question than L[i,j]: not the best run among the first i + 1 values, but the best that ends
exactly at v[i]. Fixing the end is what makes the recurrence work, but the best run can end anywhere. So the answer is
the largest entry, and the walk back starts there.

</Misconception>

A faster version keeps tails[k], the smallest value so far that ends an increasing run of length k. A smaller end
leaves more room for later values, and tails always rises, so each value finds its place by
[binary search](/learn/binary-search): it replaces the first tail at least as large as itself or, if there is none,
extends the longest run. That is O(n log n).

<Explore slug="longest-increasing-subsequence" settings={{ input: '3, 8, 4, 9, 6, 1, 2 | fast' }} caption="The same seven values, placed by binary search: 4 replaces 8, 6 replaces 9, then 1 and 2 replace 3 and 4, so tails ends as 1, 2, 6. Its length, 3, is the answer, but 1, 2, 6 is not a subsequence (the 6 comes first); prev recovers 3, 4, 6. 10 comparisons, against 21." />

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Cell (i, j) answers the question for the first i items of one sequence and the first j of the other. Row 0 and
  column 0 are the empty prefixes, and the answer is the corner.
- LCS: a match adds 1 to the diagonal; a mismatch keeps the larger of above and left. Edit distance: the edges count
  up from 0, and a cell is the smallest of above + 1, left + 1 and diagonal + 1, or the diagonal when the letters match.
- Both take O(mn) time. Two rows give the number; listing the letters or edits walks back from the corner through the
  whole table.
- A subsequence may skip letters, so the LCS is often longer than any shared run. Inserts and deletes shift letters,
  so edit distance can be far below the count of positions that differ.
- LIS: dp[i] is the longest increasing run ending exactly at v[i], O(n²), and the answer is the largest entry, not the
  last. Binary searching the smallest tail of each length makes it O(n log n).
