<Section step="hook" title="A 90% accurate test says you're sick. Are you?">

A disease affects 1 person in 100. A test catches 90% of people who have it, and wrongly flags 9% of people who don't.
You test positive. What is the chance you have the disease?

Most people, including many doctors in published studies, answer "about 90%". The true answer is about **9%**. This
lesson shows why, with a picture that makes the answer obvious instead of surprising.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="H and E">H is the hypothesis ("has the disease"); E is the evidence ("tests positive").</Term>
  <Term name="prior P(H)">How likely H is before you see any evidence: here 1%.</Term>
  <Term name="likelihoods">$P(E \mid H)$, how often H produces the evidence (90%), and $P(E \mid \neg H)$, how often it appears without H (9%).</Term>
  <Term name="posterior P(H | E)">How likely H is once you know E happened. This is what you want.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Draw everyone as a square">

<Explore slug="bayes" settings={{ preset: 'rare-disease' }} caption="Split 1000 people by who has the disease, then shade who tests positive. Once you know the test was positive, only the bottom strip is left." />

The sick column is a sliver, 10 people out of 1000. Even though the test catches 9 of them, it also flags 9% of the 990
healthy people, about 89. So among the 98 positives, only 9 are sick:

$$
P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)} = \frac{0.9 \times 0.01}{0.9 \times 0.01 + 0.09 \times 0.99} \approx 0.09
$$

<KeyIdea>

**The posterior is the share of the evidence strip where H holds.** It depends on the prior just as much as on how good
the evidence is: when H is rare, even rare false alarms can outnumber the true hits.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Misconception
  id="flip-conditional"
  title="P(E | H) and P(H | E) are the same thing"
  probes={[
    {
      q: '90% of people with a certain disease have a fever. Does that mean 90% of people with a fever have the disease?',
      choices: ['Yes: it’s the same statement turned around', 'No: that also depends on how many people have a fever for other reasons', 'Yes, as long as the disease is rare'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'P(fever | disease) is a fact about the disease. P(disease | fever) also depends on how common the disease is and how many healthy people have fevers, which is usually far more people.',
    },
    {
      q: 'A condition affects 2 people in 100. A test flags 95% of people who have it and 5% of people who don’t. About what share of positives have the condition?',
      choices: ['About 28%', '95%', '2%', '50%'],
      answer: 0,
      trap: 1,
      why: 'Out of 1000 people, 20 have it and 19 of them test positive; 5% of the 980 others is 49 false positives. 19 of 68 positives is about 28%.',
    },
  ]}
>

"90% of sick people test positive" is not "90% of positive people are sick". Swapping them is called the *prosecutor's
fallacy*. The first is a fact about the test; the second also depends on how many people were sick to begin with.

</Misconception>

<Misconception
  id="fitting-evidence-supports"
  title="Evidence that fits H always makes H more likely"
  probes={[
    {
      q: '60% of liars avoid eye contact, and so do 60% of honest people. Someone avoids eye contact. What happens to the chance they are lying?',
      choices: ['It goes down', 'It goes up: avoiding eye contact fits lying', 'It stays the same', 'It becomes 60%'],
      answer: 2,
      trap: 1,
      why: 'The evidence is just as likely either way, so the two strips are the same height and lying keeps exactly its prior share.',
    },
    {
      q: 'A clue turns up in 30% of cases where H is true and in 50% of cases where it is false. You see the clue. What happens to P(H)?',
      choices: ['It rises, since the clue fits H', 'It stays the same', 'It falls'],
      answer: 2,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'The clue is more common without H than with it, so seeing it shifts belief away from H, even though it is consistent with H.',
    },
  ]}
>

Evidence supports H only if it is **more likely when H is true than when it is false**. If both strips are the same
height, H keeps the share it started with; if the evidence is more common without H, seeing it makes H *less* likely,
even though it "fits" H.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Draw the whole population, split by the prior, then shade the evidence in each part.
- $P(H \mid E)$ is H's share of the evidence: $P(E \mid H)P(H) / P(E)$.
- Rare hypotheses need very clean evidence: false alarms from the large majority swamp the true hits.
- Updates chain: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. Naive Bayes spam filters do this word by word.

</Section>
