<Section step="hook" title="How a model learns: walk downhill">

Training a neural network means finding the weights that make its error, the *loss*, as small as possible. There can be
billions of weights, so trying them all is hopeless. Instead, start somewhere, feel which way is downhill, take a small
step, and repeat.

That is gradient descent, and almost every model you have heard of was trained by some version of it. To use it you need
one idea from calculus, the slope, and one decision, how big a step to take.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="derivative f′(x)">The slope of $f$ at a single point: how much $f$ changes per unit step in $x$, for a tiny step.</Term>
  <Term name="gradient ∇f">The slope in every direction at once, as a vector. It points the way $f$ rises fastest.</Term>
  <Term name="step size η">"Eta", also called the learning rate: how far to move against the gradient each step.</Term>
  <Term name="contour lines">Lines of equal $f$ on a map, like height lines on a hiking map. The gradient crosses them at right angles.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="First, the slope at a point">

A slope needs two points. Take $P$ and a second point $Q$ a gap $h$ away, measure the slope of the line through them, and
shrink $h$: the slopes settle on one number, the derivative.

<Explore slug="derivative" settings={{ preset: 'cubic' }} caption="The secant through P and Q turns into the tangent as h shrinks. Then the slope is measured everywhere to draw f′." />

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}
$$

In two dimensions, do the same along $x$ and along $y$ and stack the two slopes into a vector: the gradient $\nabla f$.
It points uphill, so $-\nabla f$ points downhill, and that gives the whole algorithm:

$$
x \leftarrow x - \eta \, \nabla f(x)
$$

<Explore slug="gradient-descent" settings={{ preset: 'bowl' }} caption="Each step: the gradient (uphill) is measured, and x moves against it. On a round bowl that points straight at the bottom." />

<KeyIdea>

**Gradient descent only ever looks at the ground under its feet.** That is why it scales to billions of weights, and
also why it can zigzag, crawl, overshoot or settle in a valley that isn't the lowest.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Choose the step size">

Pick an example and drag the start point. Things to try:

- **Too big a step:** with $\eta = 1.05$ on $x^2 + y^2$, every step overshoots further than the last. Does $f$ ever fall?
- **Narrow valley:** the step that suits the gentle slope along the valley is too big for its steep sides, so the path zigzags.
- **Tiny steps:** safe, but 40 steps barely move.
- **Two valleys:** start on the right and descent settles in the higher valley. Drag the start to the left half.

Only up to a point. On $f = x^2 + y^2$ a step multiplies the distance to the bottom by $1 - 2\eta$: $\eta = 0.5$
lands in one step, $\eta = 0.9$ zigzags, and anything above $1$ makes the distance *grow* every step.

</Misconception>

  title="The gradient points downhill"

The gradient points the way $f$ *rises* fastest. Gradient descent steps against it, along $-\nabla f$; stepping along
the gradient is gradient *ascent*, and climbs the hill.

<Section step="do" title="Better steps: momentum and Adam">

Plain gradient descent forgets everything after each step. **Momentum** keeps a running velocity, so steps that agree
build up speed and steps that zigzag cancel out. **RMSProp** divides each direction's step by how steep that direction
has recently been, so steep and gentle directions move at similar speeds. **Adam**, the default for training most neural
networks, does both.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- The derivative is the limit of secant slopes; the gradient collects the slopes in every direction and points uphill.
- Gradient descent repeats $x \leftarrow x - \eta \nabla f(x)$ until the ground is flat.
- The step size matters most: too small crawls, too big overshoots or diverges, and narrow valleys make plain descent zigzag.
- It finds a *local* minimum. Momentum, RMSProp and Adam add memory to move faster and more evenly.

</Section>
