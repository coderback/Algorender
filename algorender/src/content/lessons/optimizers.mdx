<Section step="hook" title="Give gradient descent a memory">

Plain gradient descent has no memory: each step measures the slope underfoot, moves against it and forgets it. In the
[gradient descent lesson](/learn/gradient-descent) that made it zigzag across a narrow valley while it crept along the
floor. A network's loss has millions of directions, some far steeper than others, so this is the normal case.

A ball rolling down the valley does better because it keeps its speed: pushes along the floor add up, and pushes from
side to side cancel. That is **momentum**. Shrinking the steps of directions that have been steep lately is **RMSProp**.
Doing both is **Adam**, the usual default for training neural networks.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="velocity v">Momentum's memory: past gradients added up, each shrunk by β for every step since. Momentum steps against v, as plain descent steps against g.</Term>
  <Term name="decay β">How much of the memory survives each step. At β = 0.9 it spans about 1/(1 − β) = 10 recent gradients.</Term>
  <Term name="running average">An average that forgets slowly: m ← βm + (1 − β)g. RMSProp keeps one of g², Adam one of g and one of g².</Term>
  <Term name="bias correction">Adam's averages start at 0, so they are too small at first. Dividing by 1 − βᵗ at step t scales them back up.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Four ways to turn a gradient into a step">

The race below runs four optimisers from one start for 60 steps. Each step, each one measures the gradient $g$ where it
stands ([derivatives and gradients](/learn/derivatives-gradients) shows how) and turns it into a step.
**Plain descent** steps against it, $x \leftarrow x - \eta g$. **Momentum** shrinks its velocity by $\beta$, adds $g$,
and steps against the velocity:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &\leftarrow \beta v + g \\
x &\leftarrow x - \eta v
\end{aligned}
$$

**RMSProp** keeps a running average $s$ of $g^2$, coordinate by coordinate (weight by weight, in a network), and
divides each coordinate's step by $\sqrt{s}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &\leftarrow \rho s + (1 - \rho)\, g^2 \\
x &\leftarrow x - \eta \, \frac{g}{\sqrt{s}}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Adam** keeps a running average $m$ of $g$ as well as RMSProp's $s$, corrects both for starting at 0, and steps by
their ratio:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &\leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1 - \beta_1)\, g \\
s &\leftarrow \beta_2 s + (1 - \beta_2)\, g^2 \\
x &\leftarrow x - \eta \, \frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat s} + \epsilon}
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\hat m = m / (1 - \beta_1^t)$ and $\hat s = s / (1 - \beta_2^t)$ at step $t$; the tiny $\epsilon$ only prevents a
division by 0. The race uses $\beta = \beta_1 = \rho = 0.9$ and $\beta_2 = 0.999$, and its own $\eta$ per optimiser and
surface, so read it as a picture of behaviour, not a benchmark. On the narrow valley $f = x^2/20 + 5y^2$ the gradient,
$(x/10,\ 10y)$, changes 100 times faster across the valley than along it.

<Explore slug="optimizers" settings={{ preset: 'valley' }} caption="All four start at (−3.5, 1.5). Plain descent bounces from wall to wall, then creeps along the floor; momentum swings across in slow waves as it gathers speed. RMSProp gets within 0.01 of the bottom first, at step 33, and Adam at step 42. After 60 steps Adam is 0.011 above it, momentum 0.027 and plain descent 0.069." />

Step once: plain descent ($\eta = 0.18$) jumps 2.7 across the valley and 0.063 along it, while Adam moves exactly its
$\eta$, 0.1, in each coordinate: at $t = 1$ the corrections give $\hat m = g$ and $\hat s = g^2$, so the step is
$\eta \, g/|g|$.

Momentum's velocity is a sum, not an average: on a steady slope it grows towards $g/(1 - \beta)$, ten times $g$ at
$\beta = 0.9$, so momentum gets a smaller $\eta$ (0.03 here). Along the floor the gradient keeps its direction, so
momentum builds up speed there: after 60 steps it is at $x = -0.41$, plain descent at $-1.18$. A gradient that flips sign
every step adds $+g$ and $-g$ in turn, so the velocity settles at about $g/(1 + \beta)$. The price is inertia: momentum
overshoots the floor (its $f$ rises at step 3), then swings across, a little less each time.

RMSProp divides by how big each coordinate's gradients have been lately, so while a gradient holds steady,
$g/\sqrt{s}$ is about $\pm 1$ and the step about $\eta$, steep or gentle. Adam's $m$ is momentum written as an average,
about the size of $g$, so dividing by $\sqrt{\hat s}$ again makes each step about $\eta$: Adam walks down the steep
wall in steps of about 0.1 instead of leaping across, though its momentum still carries it past the floor in slow swings.

<KeyIdea>

**Every optimiser here sees only the gradient where it stands, plus a memory of gradients it has already met.** Momentum
remembers their sum, so steady slopes speed it up and alternating ones cancel. RMSProp remembers their size in each
coordinate and divides by it. Adam remembers both.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

A bigger $\eta$ scales every step alike, including the ones that already bounce across a narrow valley, and those blow up
first. Momentum builds up only where the gradient keeps agreeing with itself, up to ten times $g$, so it runs with a
*smaller* $\eta$ than plain descent, and its slowness to turn round is why it overshoots.

</Misconception>

The memory holds only gradients met along each optimiser's own path. Adam's $s$ is not curvature, and none of them knows
where the lowest valley is: momentum crossed the two-valleys ridge only because its steep start happened to give it
speed. In a network, that is one reason the random starting weights can change where training ends up.

</Misconception>

Real gradients come from small random batches, so they are noisy, and the running averages smooth that too. SGD with
momentum is still widely used beside Adam, and sometimes generalises a little better. One setting never goes away.

Adam adapts the *balance* between coordinates, not the overall length of its steps: while a gradient is steady, the step

$\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$) are only a starting point: $\eta$ is still tuned per network, and usually lowered as
training goes on.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Plain gradient descent uses only the current gradient; momentum, RMSProp and Adam add a memory of past ones.
- Momentum steps against $v \leftarrow \beta v + g$: steady gradients build it up to about $1/(1 - \beta)$ times the
  gradient (10 at $\beta = 0.9$), flipping ones cancel, and its speed makes it overshoot.
- RMSProp divides each coordinate's step by the root of its running average of $g^2$, so steep and gentle directions
  both step about $\eta$.
- Adam is momentum as an average, plus RMSProp, plus bias correction.
- None of them sees the surface: they can settle in a local minimum, and $\eta$ still has to be chosen.
- Next: [neural networks](/learn/neural-networks) and the [neural network playground](/visualisers/mlp), which trains with
  momentum. [Backpropagation](/learn/backpropagation) shows where the gradients come from.

</Section>
