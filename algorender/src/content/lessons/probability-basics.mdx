<Section step="hook" title="Which sum is most likely?">

Roll two dice and add them up. The sum can be anything from 2 to 12: eleven possible sums. So is each one a
1 in 11 chance? Plenty of people think so, but a sum of 7 turns up about six times as often as a sum of 2.

Chance is the language machine learning speaks. A spam filter gives each message a chance of being spam, and a language
model gives every possible next word a chance. All of that rests on a few rules about **events**: what an event is, how to find its chance by
counting, and when chances may be added or multiplied.

Two dice are the perfect place to learn them: every outcome fits on one small board. This lesson builds the board,
finds chances as shares of it, and ends with a question that trips up even experts: when is it safe to multiply two
chances?

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="outcome">One complete result of the experiment. For two dice, a pair $(a, b)$: the first die shows $a$, the second shows $b$.</Term>
  <Term name="event">A yes-or-no question about the outcome, such as "is the sum 7?", or, the same thing, the set of outcomes where the answer is yes. Events get capital letters: $A$, $B$.</Term>
  <Term name="P(A)">The probability, or chance, of event $A$: a number from 0 (it never happens) to 1 (it always happens).</Term>
  <Term name="not A">The event that $A$ does not happen: every outcome $A$ leaves out. Also called the complement of $A$.</Term>
  <Term name="A or B">At least one of $A$ and $B$ happens (perhaps both).</Term>
  <Term name="A and B">Both $A$ and $B$ happen, on the same roll.</Term>
  <Term name="independent">Knowing that one event happened doesn't change the chance of the other.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Every outcome on one board">

How many outcomes do two dice have? The first die can show 6 faces, and whatever it shows, the second can show 6. By the
multiplication principle from the lesson on [counting](/learn/counting-combinatorics), that makes $6 \times 6 = 36$
outcomes. Draw them as a board: a row for each face of the first die, a column for each face of the second, and one
cell for each outcome.

Each die is fair, so each face is equally likely, and the dice don't affect each other. That makes all 36 cells equally
likely. When outcomes are equally likely, the chance of an event is the share of them it covers:

$$
P(A) = \frac{\text{outcomes in } A}{\text{all outcomes}}
$$

Take $A$ = "the sum is 7". Step through to see its cells.

<Explore slug="two-dice" settings={{ preset: 'sum-seven' }} caption="The 36 outcomes of two dice as a 6 × 6 board, then the cells where the sum is 7, the cells where it is not, and how many cells each sum gets." />

The sum is 7 on six cells: $(1, 6)$, $(2, 5)$, $(3, 4)$, $(4, 3)$, $(5, 2)$ and $(6, 1)$. They run along a diagonal of
the board. And $(1, 6)$ and $(6, 1)$ are different cells: a 1 on the first die and a 6 on the second is not the
same outcome as a 6 on the first and a 1 on the second. Paint the dice different colours and you can tell them apart.
So

$$
P(\text{sum is } 7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}
$$

Thrown many times, about one throw in every 6 should come out at 7.

Now the eleven sums. A sum of 2 needs both dice to show 1: just one cell, so its chance is $1/36$. A sum of 12
needs two 6s: also one cell. A sum of 7 has six cells, the most of any sum, which is why it turns up six times as
often as 2. The sums are not equally likely, so you can't count sums. You count the 36 cells, which are.

**Not A.** Every outcome is either in $A$ or not in $A$, never both. So the cells of "not $A$" are the $36 - 6 = 30$
cells that $A$ leaves out, and $P(\text{not } A) = 30/36 = 5/6$. The two chances always add up to 1:

$$
P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)
$$

Often the quickest way to a chance is to count what is left out.

<KeyIdea>

**An event is a region of the board, and when every outcome is equally likely, its chance is its share of the board.**
Count equally likely outcomes, never results like the eleven sums, and remember that "not $A$" is whatever $A$ leaves
out: $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: or">

"$A$ or $B$" means at least one of them happens. On the board it is every cell in $A$, in $B$, or in both. Here $A$ is
"the first die shows 6", the bottom row, and $B$ is "the second die shows 6", the last column. Predict how many cells
"$A$ or $B$" covers, then two more counts.

<PredictChallenge slug="two-dice" settings={{ preset: 'or' }} goal={3} />

$A$ has 6 cells and $B$ has 6 cells, but "$A$ or $B$" has only 11. Adding $6 + 6$ counts the corner $(6, 6)$ twice:
once in the row and once in the column. That cell is in both events, so take it away once:
$6 + 6 - 1 = 11$, and $P(A \text{ or } B) = 11/36$. In general:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A \text{ or } B) &= P(A) + P(B) \\
&\quad - P(A \text{ and } B)
\end{aligned}
$$

When two events have no cell in common, the overlap is empty and the chances simply add. A sum of 7 and a double
(both dice the same) are like that: the 7s run along one diagonal and the doubles along the other, and they never meet,
because a double always has an even sum. So $P(\text{a 7 or a double}) = 6/36 + 6/36 = 12/36 = 1/3$.

The last question counted the cells with no 6 at all: both dice show 1 to 5, so the first five rows meet the first
five columns in a $5 \times 5$ square of 25 cells. "At least one 6" is everything else, so $P(\text{at least one } 6) = 1 - 25/36 = 11/36$: the same
answer as adding and taking away the overlap. Two ways of counting, one answer.

A list of possible results is not a list of equally likely outcomes. The sum 7 can be made six ways and the sum 2
only one way, so 7 is six times as likely. To find a chance by counting, first find outcomes that really are equally
likely, here the 36 ordered pairs, and count those.

Adding the two chances counts every outcome in both events twice. Take the overlap away once:
$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$. Only when the events can't happen together is the overlap
empty, and then adding is right.

</Misconception>

<Section step="do" title="And: rows times columns">

"$A$ and $B$" means both happen on the same roll: on the board, the cells where the two regions overlap. Take $A$ = "the
first die is even" and $B$ = "the second die shows 5 or 6".

<Explore slug="two-dice" settings={{ preset: 'and' }} caption="A is three whole rows, B is two whole columns, and A and B is where they cross; then B's share of each row." />

$A$ is three whole rows (2, 4 and 6), so $P(A) = 18/36 = 3/6$. $B$ is two whole columns (5 and 6), so $P(B) = 12/36 = 2/6$. Where
they cross is a rectangle of $3 \times 2 = 6$ cells, so

$$
P(A \text{ and } B) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{3}{6} \times \frac{2}{6}
$$

The chance of both is the product of the two chances. That works because every row of $A$ meets every
column of $B$: in each row of the board, $B$ takes the same 2 cells of 6, so whatever the first die shows, the second
still shows 5 or 6 a third of the time. Knowing $A$ happened tells you nothing new about $B$. That is what
**independent** means, and it is exactly when the product rule holds:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&A \text{ and } B \text{ are independent exactly when} \\
&P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)
\end{aligned}
$$

One die can't affect the other, so any event about the first die alone and any event about the second alone are
independent: they always make a rectangle of rows and columns. Trouble starts when an event, like a sum, depends on
both dice. Step through three such pairs; in Predict mode you count each overlap before you see it.

<Explore slug="two-dice" settings={{ preset: 'dependent' }} caption="Three pairs of events: a sum of 10 or more with a first die of 6, a 7 with a double, and a 7 with a first die of 6. Each time the product of the chances is set against the real count." />

**A sum of 10 or more, and a first die of 6.** Each has 6 cells, so each has chance $1/6$, and multiplying gives
$1/6 \times 1/6 = 1/36$: one cell. But the bottom row holds three cells with a sum of 10 or more, $(6, 4)$, $(6, 5)$
and $(6, 6)$, so $P(A \text{ and } B) = 3/36 = 1/12$, three times the product. A first die of 6 makes a big sum much
likelier: half of that row has a sum of 10 or more, against a sixth of the whole board. The events are not independent,
so multiplying gives the wrong answer.

**A sum of 7, and a double.** They never happen together, so $P(A \text{ and } B) = 0$, yet the product is
$1/36$. Events that can't happen together are as far from independent as events can be: knowing one happened rules the
other out.

**A sum of 7, and a first die of 6.** These sound related, since the first die is part of the sum. But they meet in
exactly one cell, $(6, 1)$, and $1/36 = 1/6 \times 1/6$: they are independent. Every row of the board has exactly one 7,
so whatever the first die shows, a 7 stays a 1 in 6 chance. You can't tell independence from how two events sound.
You check it by counting.

The product rule holds only for independent events: those where knowing one happened leaves the other's chance where it
was. Events about two separate dice are independent; events that share a die, like a sum and one of the faces, may
not be. When in doubt, count the cells in both and compare with the product.

</Misconception>

**Rolling instead of counting.** The board gives chances exactly. What happens if you actually throw the dice, again and
again? Tally each throw in its cell, and watch the share of throws that sum to 7.

<Explore slug="two-dice" settings={{ preset: 'throws' }} caption="One run of throws of two dice, tallied on the board after 36, 360, 3600 and 36,000 throws, with the share of 7s against the exact 1/6." />

After 36 throws this run had 8 sevens, a share of 0.222, well above $1/6 \approx 0.167$; and 15 of the 36 cells had no
throw at all. After 360 throws it had 66 sevens (0.183), after 3600 it had 607 (0.169), and after 36,000 it had 5970
(0.166). The share closes in on the chance counted on the board. The count of sevens is never exactly a sixth of the
throws; it even drifts further from it, 2 off, then 6, 7 and 30. That doesn't matter: what settles is the share. The
**law of large numbers**, a later lesson, says why, and how fast.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- An outcome is one complete result; an event is a set of outcomes, a region of the board.
- When outcomes are equally likely, $P(A)$ is the outcomes in $A$ over all outcomes: two dice have $6 \times 6 = 36$, and a sum of 7 has 6 of them.
- Count equally likely outcomes, never results like the 11 sums: $(1, 6)$ and $(6, 1)$ are different outcomes.
- $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$, and $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$.
- $A$ and $B$ are independent when $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$: knowing one doesn't change the other's chance. Check it by counting.
- Thrown again and again, the share of throws in an event settles near its chance; dice have no memory, so nothing is ever "due".
- Next: what happens to a chance once you know another event happened. That is **conditional probability and Bayes' rule**, where the test result changes the chance of the disease.

</Section>
