<Section step="hook" title="Count before you guess">

How likely is it that two dice show a total of 7? That a shuffled deck comes out in perfect order? Both start the same
way: count how many ways something can happen, then how many ways anything at all can happen. Chance comes later;
counting comes first.

Counting sounds easy until the numbers get big. A deck of 52 cards can be shuffled into about $8.07 \times 10^{67}$
different orders, a number with 68 digits. No computer will ever list them, yet one line of arithmetic counts them. A
model that reads 20 numbers about each example, its **features**, can be trained on any group of them, from none to
all: 1,048,576 groups.

This lesson builds three rules: choices made one after another **multiply**, picking in order without repeats shrinks
each choice by one, and when order doesn't matter you **divide** away the orders counted twice. Then you build Pascal's
triangle, which lists every "how many ways to choose" at once.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="multiplication principle">If one choice has $a$ options and, for each of them, a second choice has $b$ options, there are $a \times b$ ways to make both.</Term>
  <Term name="ordered pick">A list of $k$ different things taken from $n$, where the order counts: 1st, 2nd, 3rd. Also called an arrangement, or a permutation.</Term>
  <Term name="factorial">$n!$, read "n factorial": $n \times (n - 1) \times \cdots \times 2 \times 1$, the number of orders of $n$ things. So $3! = 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6$, and $0! = 1$.</Term>
  <Term name="choice">A group of $k$ things taken from $n$, where the order doesn't count: a team, a hand of cards. Also called a combination.</Term>
  <Term name="n choose k">$C(n, k)$, also written $\binom{n}{k}$: the number of ways to choose $k$ things from $n$ when order doesn't count.</Term>
  <Term name="Pascal's triangle">A table of every $C(n, k)$: row $n$ lists $C(n, 0), C(n, 1), \ldots, C(n, n)$. Its edges are 1, and every number inside is the sum of the two above it.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="AND multiplies">

Start with a wardrobe: 3 tops (red, blue, green) and 4 bottoms (jeans, shorts, a skirt and chinos). An outfit is one
top **and** one bottom. How many outfits are there?

Draw a table with a row for each top and a column for each bottom. Every cell is exactly one outfit and every outfit is
exactly one cell, so counting outfits is counting cells.

<Explore slug="counting" settings={{ preset: 'outfits' }} caption="The outfits as a table: a row for each top and a column for each bottom, filled in a row at a time. Then shoes join in." />

The red top goes with each of the 4 bottoms: a row of 4 outfits. The blue top makes a whole new row of 4, not just one
more outfit, and so does the green. Three rows of four: $3 \times 4 = 12$ outfits.

Adding would give $3 + 4 = 7$, which counts something else: the pieces of clothing. If you were giving away one item, a
top **or** a bottom, you would have 7 to pick from. "Or" between separate piles adds; "and" across choices multiplies.

Now add shoes: trainers or boots. Every one of the 12 outfits can be worn with either pair, so the table appears twice:
$3 \times 4 \times 2 = 24$. Each new choice multiplies the count by its number of options.

When the same choice is made again and again, the product is a power. A phone PIN has 4 digits, each one of the 10
digits 0 to 9, and repeats are allowed, so there are

$$
10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 10^4 = 10{,}000
$$

PINs. This is the exponent from the lesson on [exponents and logarithms](/learn/exponents-logs): $k$ choices, each from
the same $n$ options, give $n^k$ ways. It is also a product, $\prod$, as in the lesson on
[sums and products](/learn/sums-notation): multiply one factor per choice.

The 20 features from the start work the same way. Each feature is either in the group or out of it: two options, made
20 times. That is $2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576$ groups, the empty group and the full one included.

<KeyIdea>

**Choices made one after another multiply.** If every option of one choice goes with every option of the next, the
number of ways is the product of the numbers of options: $3 \times 4 = 12$ outfits, $10^4$ PINs, $2^{20}$ groups of
features. Adding is for "or": one thing from one pile or another.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: a podium">

Five runners, Ada, Ben, Cy, Dee and Eli, run a race. A **podium** is who comes 1st, who comes 2nd and who comes 3rd.
The order matters: Cy first and Ada second is a different podium from Ada first and Cy second. And nobody can take two
places.

In the table each row is a place and each column a runner. A tick means that runner could still take that place, and a
solid cell means they have already been placed. Predict how many runners each place can choose from, how many podiums
there are in all, and then what happens when every runner is ranked.

<PredictChallenge slug="counting" settings={{ preset: 'orders' }} goal={4} />

Any of the 5 runners can win. Whoever wins, 4 are left for 2nd place, and whoever comes 2nd, 3 are left for 3rd. Every
way of filling the earlier places goes with each option for the next, so by the multiplication principle there are

$$
5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60
$$

podiums. The usual wrong answers each come from a real slip. Adding, $5 + 4 + 3 = 12$, treats the places as separate
piles. Multiplying runners by places, $5 \times 3 = 15$, forgets that each place has its own choice. And $5^3 = 125$
lets one runner take every place: that would be right for a PIN, where a digit can repeat, but not for a race.

Ranking all five, the 4th place has 2 runners left and the 5th has just 1, so there are
$5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 120$ orders. That product is $5!$, "five factorial". With a sixth runner,
1st place has 6 choices, and whoever wins, the other five finish in any of those 120 orders. So 6 runners finish in
$6! = 6 \times 5! = 720$ orders: multiplied by 6, not 6 more. Factorials grow fast. Ten runners finish in $10! = 3{,}628{,}800$ orders, and a deck of
52 cards in $52!$, the $8.07 \times 10^{67}$ from the start.

One more link between the two counts. Every podium is the start of exactly 2 full rankings, because the two runners left
off it can come 4th and 5th in $2! = 2$ orders. So the 120 rankings are the 60 podiums, each counted 2 times:

$$
\text{podiums} = \frac{5!}{2!} = \frac{120}{2} = 60
$$

In general, an ordered pick of $k$ things from $n$ has $k$ factors, $n \times (n - 1) \times \cdots$, which is
$n! \div (n - k)!$: the orders of all $n$, divided by the orders of the $n - k$ left off, as with the podiums. The [permutations](/visualisers/permutations) visualiser draws every order as a leaf of a tree, one
level per place, each with one fewer branch; the lesson on [backtracking](/learn/backtracking) builds that tree in code.
And $0! = 1$: there is exactly one way to put nothing in order, which keeps $n! \div 0! = n!$ right when $k = n$.

<Misconception
  id="add-not-multiply"
  title="Choices made one after another add up"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'A menu has 8 starters and 29 main courses. A meal is one starter and one main course. How many different meals are there?',
      choices: ['37', '232', '841'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Each of the 8 starters goes with each of the 29 mains, so 8 × 29 = 232 meals. 37 counts the dishes on the menu, not the meals.',
    },
    {
      q: 'A flag has two stripes, and each stripe is painted one of 17 colours (both stripes may be the same colour). How many different flags are there?',
      choices: ['289', '34', '272'],
      answer: 0,
      trap: 1,
      why: 'The top stripe has 17 options and, for each, the bottom stripe has 17: 17 × 17 = 289. Adding 17 + 17 = 34 counts each stripe’s colours separately, as if a flag had only one stripe.',
    },
  ]}
>

Two numbers of options, so it is tempting to add them. But adding counts the options in separate piles, one thing from
one pile **or** the other. When you choose one from each pile, **and**, each option of the first goes with every option
of the second: a row of outfits for each top, not one outfit. Draw the table and count its cells: rows times columns.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="When order doesn't matter, then Pascal's triangle">

Now the five runners pick a team of two for a relay. A team has no order: Ada with Ben is the same team as Ben with Ada.
How many teams are there?

Count ordered picks first, then fix the double counting. In the table, a cell is an ordered pick: the first person's
row and the second person's column.

<Explore slug="counting" settings={{ preset: 'pairs' }} caption="Ordered picks of two from five people, one cell for each pick, then sorted into teams of two." />

Nobody can be picked twice, so the 5 cells where the row and the column are the same person are walls, a diagonal line
across the table. That leaves $5 \times 4 = 20$ ordered picks out of the 25 cells. Each team sits in two of them, mirror
images across the walls: AB (Ada first, Ben second) above them and BA below. Keep the copy above the walls and drop its
mirror: $20 \div 2 = 10$ teams.

For teams of three, the same idea. There are $5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60$ ordered picks of three, and each team of three
shows up once for each of its orders: $3! = 6$ of them. So there are $60 \div 6 = 10$ teams of three. In general:

$$
C(n, k) = \frac{n \times (n - 1) \times \cdots \ (k \text{ factors})}{k!}
$$

Both answers came out as 10, and that is no accident. Choosing 3 of the 5 to run is the same as choosing the 2 who sit
out, so $C(5, 3) = C(5, 2)$. In general $C(n, k) = C(n, n - k)$.

<Misconception
  id="order-counted-twice"
  title="Choosing a group counts every order of it"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'At a meeting of 11 people, everyone shakes hands once with everyone else. How many handshakes are there?',
      choices: ['55', '110', '121'],
      answer: 0,
      trap: 1,
      why: 'There are 11 × 10 = 110 ordered pairs, but Kai shaking hands with Lena is the same handshake as Lena with Kai, so each one is counted twice: 110 ÷ 2 = 55.',
    },
    {
      q: 'A club of 12 picks 3 members to go to a conference, all with the same role. How many different groups of 3 are there?',
      choices: ['4', '1,320', '220'],
      answer: 2,
      trap: 1,
      why: '12 × 11 × 10 = 1,320 counts ordered picks, and each group of 3 appears in 3! = 6 orders, so there are 1,320 ÷ 6 = 220 groups. 4 is how many groups of 3 the club splits into, not how many ways to choose one.',
    },
  ]}
>

The product $n \times (n - 1) \times \cdots$ counts **ordered** picks, so it counts each group once for every order its
members can be listed in. When only the group matters, divide by that number of orders: by 2 for pairs, by $3! = 6$ for
groups of three, by $k!$ for groups of $k$. Ask yourself: would swapping two of the picks make a different answer? If
not, divide.

</Misconception>

There is also a way to get every $C(n, k)$ by adding alone, all in one table: **Pascal's triangle**, whose row $n$
lists $C(n, 0), C(n, 1), \ldots, C(n, n)$.

The edges are easy. There is exactly one way to choose none of $n$ things, and exactly one way to choose all of them,
so every row starts and ends with 1. To the right of the diagonal, $k$ is more than $n$, and there is no way to choose more
than you have: those cells are 0.

The inside comes from one argument. Take five people and choose a team of 2, and look at one person, say Eli. Either
Eli is in the team, and the other member is chosen from the 4 others, $C(4, 1)$ ways; or Eli isn't, and both members
come from the other 4, $C(4, 2)$ ways. Every team is exactly one of the two, so

$$
C(5, 2) = C(4, 1) + C(4, 2)
$$

and in general

$$
C(n, k) = C(n - 1, k - 1) + C(n - 1, k)
$$

In the table, those are the cell above on the left and the cell straight above. Each cell is the sum of the two cells
above it. Step through and watch the arrows.

<Explore slug="counting" settings={{ preset: 'pascal' }} caption="Pascal's triangle filled row by row, with arrows into each new cell, and each row's sum in the last column." />

For example, $C(4, 2) = C(3, 1) + C(3, 2) = 3 + 3 = 6$. The usual slips give other numbers: the cell straight above
plus the one above on the right gives $3 + 1 = 4$; the cell to the left plus the one above, as in a grid of paths,
gives $4 + 3 = 7$; multiplying gives $3 \times 3 = 9$.

Row 6 reads 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1. It is symmetrical, as $C(n, k) = C(n, n - k)$ says it must be, and its middle,
$C(6, 3) = 20$, is the biggest: there are more ways to choose half than any other number.

Now add up each row: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. Each row doubles the last, and row $n$ adds up to $2^n$. In the table,
every cell of a row is added into two cells of the row below, so the row below adds up to twice as much. In counting
terms, row $n$ counts every group you can take from $n$ things, sorted by size, and there are $2^n$ such groups, since
each thing is in or out. A group taken from some things is called a **subset** of them. Four things have $2^4 = 16$
subsets but $4! = 24$ orders; the [subsets](/visualisers/subsets) visualiser draws all 16 as a tree, taking or leaving
one thing per level.

<Misconception
  id="choose-none-is-zero"
  title="There is no way to choose nothing"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'A shelf holds 19 books. In how many ways can you choose 0 of them to take on holiday?',
      choices: ['0', '1', '19'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'There is exactly one way: take none of them. C(19, 0) = 1, like every left edge of Pascal’s triangle.',
    },
    {
      q: 'A laptop comes with any selection of 7 add-ons: all of them, some of them, or none. How many different selections are there?',
      choices: ['127', '128', '8'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Each add-on is in or out, so 2⁷ = 128 selections. 127 leaves out the selection with no add-ons, which is a selection too, and 8 counts only the selections of one add-on or none.',
    },
  ]}
>

Choosing nothing sounds like no choice at all, but it is one way to choose: take none. That is why every row of Pascal's
triangle starts with 1, why $0! = 1$, and why the row sums are exactly $2^n$. Leave out the empty choice and every count of
groups comes out one short.

</Misconception>

Now build the triangle yourself. Write `choose(n, k)`: make a table with `table(n + 1, n + 1, 0)` (rows and columns
numbered 0 to $n$), set each row's edges to 1, fill every cell between them from the two cells above it, and return
`C[n][k]`, or 0 when $k$ is more than $n$. Watch the example: every cell your code writes gets an arrow from the two
cells it read.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Choices made one after another multiply; "or" between separate piles adds. The same choice made $k$ times from $n$ options gives $n^k$.
- Picking $k$ of $n$ in order, without repeats: $n \times (n - 1) \times \cdots$ with $k$ factors. All $n$ in order: $n!$, and $0! = 1$.
- When order doesn't matter, divide by the $k!$ orders of each group: $C(n, k) = n \times (n - 1) \times \cdots \div k!$, and $C(n, k) = C(n, n - k)$.
- Pascal's triangle: edges 1, every other number the sum of the two above it, $C(n, k) = C(n - 1, k - 1) + C(n - 1, k)$, and 0 when $k$ is more than $n$.
- Row $n$ adds up to $2^n$: every group of $n$ things, sorted by size, with the empty group counted once.
- Next comes **Chance, events and independence**, which turns these counts into chances, starting with two dice.

</Section>
