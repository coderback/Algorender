<Section step="hook" title="Six towns, nine possible cables">

A county wants to join six towns, A to F, with fibre. Cable could go along nine routes, each with a cost, from 2
between C and F up to 15 between B and D. Every town must reach every other, directly or through other towns, for the
smallest total bill.

The cheapest network never has a loop: any one cable on a cycle could be left out and every town would still be
connected. So the answer is a tree, five cables for six towns. Trying every tree is hopeless: ten towns that could all
be linked to each other allow 100 million of them. Instead, two greedy algorithms, **Kruskal's** and **Prim's**, keep
taking the cheapest cable that is safe to take. This lesson is about what makes a cable safe.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="spanning tree">A set of a graph's edges that connects every node and contains no cycle. With V nodes it always has exactly V − 1 edges.</Term>
  <Term name="minimum spanning tree (MST)">A spanning tree with the least total weight, in a connected, undirected, weighted graph. A disconnected graph gets a minimum spanning forest, one tree per component.</Term>
  <Term name="component">A group of nodes joined by the edges chosen so far. Kruskal's starts with every node in a component of its own and tracks them with union-find.</Term>
  <Term name="cut">A split of the nodes into two groups. An edge crosses the cut when its two ends are in different groups.</Term>
  <Term name="key[v]">In Prim's algorithm, the weight of the cheapest single edge known from the tree to v, or ∞ while there is none.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Take the cheapest safe edge">

Why should the cheapest edge be safe? Split the nodes into any two groups. Some edge must cross between them, or the
groups could never be connected. Call the lightest crossing edge e, and suppose a cheapest tree leaves e out. Adding e
to that tree makes a cycle, which crosses the split along e and so must cross back along another tree edge, f, at
least as heavy as e. Swap f for e: still connected, no heavier, so a cheapest tree contains e. That swap is an
exchange argument, as in the [greedy algorithms lesson](/learn/greedy), and it proves the **cut property**.

**Kruskal's algorithm** sorts the edges by weight and tries them cheapest first. If an edge's ends are in different
components, it keeps the edge and merges them. Every lighter edge was tried earlier and now has both ends in one
component, so the new edge is the lightest leaving its component. If the ends are already in one component, the edge
would close a cycle and is rejected. [Union-find](/learn/union-find) answers "same component?" in nearly constant
amortised time.

<Explore slug="kruskals" settings={{ preset: 'classic' }} caption="Kruskal's sorts the nine edges and tries them cheapest first. C–F (2), D–E (6), A–B (7) and B–C (8) each join two components. A–C (9) is rejected, because A and C are already connected through B. E–F (9) joins the last two components: five edges, total 32. C–D, A–F and B–D are never tried." />

**Prim's algorithm** grows one tree instead, from a start node. Its cut is always the tree against everything else, so
the cheapest edge leaving the tree is safe. A [min-heap](/learn/heaps) finds that edge: every node outside the tree has
a key, and when a node joins, its edges can lower its neighbours' keys. Old heap entries are skipped as stale.

<Explore slug="prims" settings={{ preset: 'classic' }} caption="Prim's grows the tree from A, adding A–B (7), B–C (8), C–F (2), F–E (9) and E–D (6). D's key falls from 15, through B, to 11, through C, then to 6, through E, as the tree grows towards it. The same five edges as Kruskal's, in a different order, total 32." />

<KeyIdea>

**For any split of the nodes into two groups, the lightest edge between them belongs to a minimum spanning tree.**
Kruskal's keeps the cheapest edge that joins two of its components; Prim's adds the cheapest edge leaving its one tree.
Every edge either takes is the lightest across some split, so both end with a minimum spanning tree, though neither
ever undoes a choice.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Kruskal's in code, and what an MST is not">

Here is Kruskal's algorithm on four nodes, 0 to 3, with the edges already sorted, each as `[weight, u, v]`. `forest`
is a union-find parent list, every node starting as its own root. For each edge the code climbs from u to its root, a,
and from v to its root, b. Equal roots mean the edge would close a cycle; different roots mean `forest[b] = a` joins
two components.

To stay short, the code skips union by rank and path compression. With both, each climb is nearly O(1) amortised,
so the sort dominates: Kruskal's is O(E log E), which is also O(E log V), since E is at most V². A real implementation
also stops at V − 1 edges, as the visualiser does. Edge 1-3 is where a tempting shortcut goes wrong.

  id="both-ends-in-tree"
  title="Kruskal's skips an edge whose ends are both already in the tree"

    {
      q: 'Which check tells Kruskal’s that the edge u–v would close a cycle?',
      choices: ['find(u) = find(v): u and v are already in one component', 'u and v each touch an edge kept so far', 'v was reached before u'],
      answer: 0,
      trap: 1,
      why: 'A cycle closes only when u and v are already connected, which is when union-find gives them the same root. Touching kept edges is not enough.',
    },
  ]}
>

Kruskal's doesn't grow one tree. It holds a forest of components that merge as it goes, so an edge between two nodes
that already have edges can still join two pieces. In the six towns, B–C joins A, B to C, F, and E–F joins D, E to the
rest, though both ends of each were already touched. The test is "are both ends in the same component?":
find(u) = find(v).

</Misconception>

Stopping Kruskal's while k components remain is a simple **clustering**, the same as deleting the k − 1 heaviest edges
of the finished tree: the closest members of different groups end up as far apart as possible. The finished tree also
approximates the travelling salesman: when going direct is never longer than a detour, walking round it and skipping
towns already seen gives a round trip at most twice the shortest.

Prim's has the shape of [Dijkstra's algorithm](/learn/dijkstra), down to the min-heap, the stale entries and
O(E log V) with a binary heap. What differs is the number each node holds. Dijkstra's asks whether dist[u] + w beats
dist[v], a whole route from the start; Prim's asks whether w alone beats key[v], one edge into the tree.

Its prev column links B and C to A, D and F to C, and E to F: a tree weighing 38, not 32. An MST minimises one number, the total weight of its edges, and promises nothing about any single route. The six
towns' tree has no A–C, so its route from A to D runs the whole chain, A–B–C–F–E–D, for 32, where A–C–D takes 20. For
quickest routes, use Dijkstra's algorithm; its tree never weighs less in total than the MST, and often weighs more.

</Misconception>

When edges weigh the same, the order they are tried in is a tie-break, and another tie-break can give another tree.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A spanning tree joins all V nodes with V − 1 edges and no cycle; a minimum spanning tree has the least total weight.
- The cut property: across any split of the nodes, the lightest edge belongs to a minimum spanning tree. It makes both
  greedy algorithms correct.
- Kruskal's tries edges cheapest first and keeps each one whose ends have different roots in union-find: O(E log E),
  for the sort. Touched ends can still be in different components.
- Prim's grows one tree from a start, adding the cheapest edge leaving it with a min-heap: O(E log V). It is Dijkstra's
  shape, comparing w with key[v] instead of dist[u] + w with dist[v].
- The tree minimises the total, not routes: its path between two nodes can be far longer than the shortest. Equal
  weights can give several minimum spanning trees; distinct weights give exactly one.
- Next: **clustering**, by stopping Kruskal's early or with [k-means](/learn/k-means). Practise the component check with
  [The Extra Edge](/practice/redundant-connection) and [Count Connected Groups](/practice/connected-components), and
  shortest routes, for contrast, with [Signal Spread Time](/practice/network-delay-time). In the
  [Prim's](/visualisers/prims) and [Kruskal's](/visualisers/kruskals) visualisers, try Two islands.

</Section>
