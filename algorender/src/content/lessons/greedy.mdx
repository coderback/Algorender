<Section step="hook" title="Take the best now, never look back">

A lecture hall takes bookings for one day, and you want to fit in as many talks as you can, never two at once. With 30
requests there are about a billion sets of talks to check. Or follow one rule: of the talks that still fit, take the
one that **finishes first**, and never change your mind. That rule fits the most talks every time.

It is a **greedy algorithm**: make the choice that looks best now and never take it back. Looking best is not always
being best: in the [dynamic programming lesson](/learn/dynamic-programming), the biggest coin first paid out 6 with
three coins where two would do. This lesson shows how to tell a safe greedy rule from one that only sounds safe.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="greedy choice">The option a fixed rule ranks best right now, such as the activity that finishes first. It is taken at once and never undone.</Term>
  <Term name="greedy-choice property">Some best solution starts with the greedy choice: not necessarily every best solution, just one.</Term>
  <Term name="optimal substructure">After a choice, what is left is a smaller problem of the same kind, and its best solution completes a best solution.</Term>
  <Term name="exchange argument">The usual proof of the greedy-choice property: swap a choice in any best solution for the greedy one, and show the result is still allowed and no worse.</Term>
  <Term name="counterexample">One input on which a rule does worse than the best possible. One is enough to prove the rule wrong.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Finish first, then do it again">

Five activities want one room. A runs from 0 to 10, and B, C, D and E fill the time from 1 to 9 in four short slots.

<Explore slug="activity-selection" settings={{ preset: 'longest-trap' }} caption="A starts first but finishes last, so sorting by finish time moves it to the bottom. B (1–3) is chosen first, then C (3–5), D (5–7) and E (7–9), each starting just as the last choice ends. A starts at 0, before 9, so it is skipped: 4 of 5." />

Sort by start time instead and A goes first, holding the room until 10, by when every other activity has started: one
activity. What matters is when an activity **finishes**, because the one that frees the room soonest leaves the most
time for the rest.

That is a hunch; here is the proof. Take any schedule with the most activities, and look at its first activity. B
finishes at 3, the earliest of all, so it ends no later than that one. Swap B in. Every other activity in the schedule
starts no earlier than the old first one ends, so no earlier than B ends either: no overlaps, and the same count. So
some best schedule starts with B, and choosing B loses nothing. What is left is the same problem, smaller: the
activities that start at 3 or later. The same argument picks the next choice, and the next, so one pass in finish
order is enough, remembering only **last**, the time the latest choice ends.

<KeyIdea>

**A greedy algorithm is correct when two things hold: some best solution starts with the greedy choice (the
greedy-choice property), and what is left after it is a smaller problem of the same kind (optimal substructure).** An
exchange argument proves the first: take any best solution, swap its first choice for the greedy one, and show the
result is still allowed and no worse. Then the same step repeats on what is left.

</KeyIdea>

The same proof works on another problem. A knapsack holds 50 kg of goods that can be cut, like grain or cloth. The
greedy rule: pack the best value per kilo first.

<Explore slug="knapsack-greedy" settings={{ preset: 'textbook' }} caption="A is 10 kg worth 60, 6 per kg; B is 20 kg worth 100, 5 per kg; C is 30 kg worth 120, 4 per kg. A and B go in whole, leaving 20 kg, so 20 of C’s 30 kg go in (67%), worth 80. Total 240, with the knapsack full." />

Here the swap is a kilo for a kilo. If a packing holds a kilo of C while some of A is left out, swapping the two keeps
the weight and adds 6 − 4 = 2 to the value. So a best packing never leaves a better kilo out while a worse one is in,
and that is exactly the packing ratio order makes. Only the last item is ever cut.

Both algorithms sort by the greedy rule, then make one pass that settles each item once and never looks back.

</Section>

Earliest start and shortest first sound as reasonable as earliest finish, and both fail on as few as three activities;
even fewest clashes first fails on some inputs. The exchange argument shows what is special about earliest finish: the
activity swapped in ends no later than the one it replaces, so nothing after it can clash. A shorter or earlier-starting
one can end later, and then the swap breaks. One counterexample proves a greedy rule wrong; only a proof proves it
right.

</Misconception>

The value-per-kilo rule has a limit too. Here it is on three items that can be cut, in a 10 kg knapsack:

<Explore slug="knapsack-greedy" settings={{ input: '6:10, 5:7, 5:7 | 10' }} caption="A is 6 kg worth 10, 1.67 per kg; B and C are 5 kg worth 7 each, 1.4 per kg. A goes in whole, then 4 kg of B, worth 5.6, fills the knapsack, and C stays out: 15.6 in all." />

Now make them a 6 kg laptop and two 5 kg cameras, which can't be cut. The same rule takes A, and the 4 kg left holds
neither camera: 10. Yet B and C weigh exactly 10 kg together and are worth 14.

The ratio rule's proof swaps a kilo for a kilo. Whole items allow no such swap, so a heavy item with a high ratio can
leave a gap that nothing fills. No greedy rule is known to solve this **0/1 knapsack**; dynamic programming solves it
in O(n·W) time for n items and a whole-number capacity W. The [0/1 knapsack visualiser](/visualisers/knapsack-01)
fills that table, and its example Greedy-by-value fails is the laptop and the two cameras.

</Misconception>

Not every greedy algorithm can sort once at the start. [Dijkstra's algorithm](/learn/dijkstra) settles the closest
unsettled node next, and each node it settles can bring others closer, so a [heap](/learn/heaps) hands it each choice
in O(log n). Its proof needs edges of non-negative weight: past a negative edge, a settled node could still get closer.

      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,

>

The pass is O(n) only once the items are in greedy order, and a comparison sort such as [merge sort](/learn/merge-sort)
costs O(n log n), more than the pass: on unsorted input the sort is the cost. Finding each choice by scanning
everything left is worse, n scans of up to n items: O(n²). Sort once when the order never changes, and use a heap when
it does.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A greedy algorithm makes the choice that looks best now and never takes it back. It is usually a sort and one pass:
  O(n log n), with the sort as the cost.
- It is correct only when some best solution starts with the greedy choice and what is left is a smaller problem of the
  same kind. An exchange argument proves the first; one counterexample proves a rule wrong.
- Activity selection: sort by finish time and take each activity that starts no earlier than the last one taken ends.
  Earliest start and shortest first both fail on small inputs.
- Fractional knapsack: pack the best value per kilo first, cutting only the last item. For whole items that rule fails,
  and the 0/1 knapsack needs dynamic programming.
- Next: [minimum spanning trees](/learn/minimum-spanning-trees), where the cheapest edge joining two separate pieces is safe for the same kind of
  reason (try [Kruskal's algorithm](/visualisers/kruskals)), and **Huffman coding**, which keeps merging the two
  rarest groups of symbols. Practise with [Fewest Ranges to Remove](/practice/non-overlapping-intervals), activity
  selection counted the other way, [Reach the Last Index](/practice/jump-game) and
  [Drive Once Around a Ring Road](/practice/gas-station), or fool a greedy cashier in [Break it](/play/break-it).

</Section>
