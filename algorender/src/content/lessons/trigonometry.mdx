<Section step="hook" title="Why a language model needs sine waves">

A transformer, the kind of model behind modern chatbots, looks at all the words of a sentence at once. Its core
step, called attention, has no built-in sense of word order: to it, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" hold the same
words. So the model is told each word's position.

The original transformer (Vaswani and colleagues, 2017) did that by adding a pattern of **sine and cosine waves** to
each word's numbers, a different pattern for each position. Many newer models instead **turn** pairs of a word's
numbers by an angle that grows with its position (rotary embeddings, Su and colleagues, 2021). Games turn sprites with
the same two functions, and robots turn their arms.

Sine and cosine come from one simple picture: a point going round a circle. This lesson builds that picture, uses it
to turn arrows, and shows a mistake that catches many people the first time they use `Math.sin`.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="unit circle">A circle of radius 1 with its centre at $(0, 0)$. Angles start from the point $(1, 0)$ on its right and turn anticlockwise.</Term>
  <Term name="angle θ">How far round a point has turned. θ is the Greek letter theta.</Term>
  <Term name="radian">An angle measured by the distance travelled round the unit circle. A full turn is the whole circumference, $2\pi \approx 6.28$ radians, the same as 360°.</Term>
  <Term name="cos θ and sin θ">The coordinates of the turned point: $\cos\theta$ across, $\sin\theta$ up.</Term>
  <Term name="period">How far along a repeating pattern goes before it starts again. For sine and cosine, $2\pi$: one full turn.</Term>
</Terms>

You may remember that a circle's circumference is $2\pi r$. With radius 1 that is $2\pi$, so a full turn of 360° is
$2\pi$ radians. Divide both by 360 and 1° is $\frac{\pi}{180}$ radians. So to turn degrees into radians, multiply by
$\frac{\pi}{180}$: 180° is $\pi$ radians, 90° is $\frac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.57$, and one radian is about 57.3°.

Why a new unit? Radians tie the angle to a distance, which keeps the maths of sine and cosine simple (the calculus
lessons show why). That is why code uses them.

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Shadows of a turning point">

<Explore slug="unit-circle" settings={{ preset: 'angle' }} caption="Turn P by 60°. The arc it travels is the angle in radians; its shadows on the axes are cos θ and sin θ." />

Turning 60° sends $P$ along an arc of length $1.047$: that is 60° in radians, $60 \times \frac{\pi}{180}$. Where it
stops, $P$ sits at $(0.5, 0.866)$. Those two coordinates are, by definition, the cosine and sine of 60°:

$$
\cos 60^\circ = 0.5
$$

$$
\sin 60^\circ \approx 0.866
$$

The picture draws $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ as two lines. $\cos\theta$ runs along the x-axis from the centre to just below $P$: the
shadow $P$ would cast with a light straight overhead. $\sin\theta$ runs from there straight up to $P$: its height,
the shadow it would cast on the y-axis.

If you remember SOH CAH TOA, this is the same idea. The across distance, the up distance and the radius make a
right-angled triangle, and its hypotenuse is the radius, 1. So $\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{1}$ is the
height, and $\cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{1}$ is the across distance. The circle only adds one thing: it
keeps going past 90°, where a triangle can't.

The same triangle gives a rule that holds at every angle. By Pythagoras (and writing $\cos^2\theta$ for
$(\cos\theta)^2$):

$$
\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1
$$

Squares are never negative, so two squares that add up to 1 are each at most 1. That is why sine and cosine can
never be bigger than 1 or smaller than $-1$.

<KeyIdea>

**Turn a point θ round the unit circle from $(1, 0)$: it lands at $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$.** The angle in radians
is the distance it travelled. Left of the centre, cos is negative; below it, sin is negative.

</KeyIdea>

centre, so $\cos\theta$ is negative. Between 180° and 360° it is below the centre, so $\sin\theta$ is negative.

<Section step="do" title="Turn an arrow">

Now use the circle to turn an arrow $v = (x, y)$, drawn from the centre $(0, 0)$ to the point $(x, y)$, by an angle θ.
We'll build the rule in three small steps.

**A quarter turn first.** On squared paper, the arrow $(2, 1)$ goes 2 right and 1 up. Turn the page a quarter turn
anticlockwise: "right" becomes "up" and "up" becomes "left". So it now goes 2 up and 1 left, to $(-1, 2)$. In
general a quarter turn sends $(x, y)$ to $(-y, x)$.

**Then the two unit steps.** Every arrow is made of two kinds of step: $x$ steps of $(1, 0)$ and $y$ steps of
$(0, 1)$. Turned by θ, the step $(1, 0)$ lands at $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$: that is the unit circle again. The step
$(0, 1)$ is $(1, 0)$ turned a quarter turn, so it stays a quarter turn ahead. Apply the quarter-turn rule to
$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and it lands at $(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$.

**Then add them up.** Turning the whole arrow turns each step but keeps the counts $x$ and $y$. So the turned arrow
$v' = (x', y')$ (said "v prime") is $x$ of the first plus $y$ of the second. Add the across parts, then the up parts:

$$
x' = x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta
$$

$$
y' = x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta
$$

Check it with $(2, 1)$ and 90°, where $\cos 90^\circ = 0$ and $\sin 90^\circ = 1$: $x' = 2 \times 0 - 1 \times 1 = -1$
and $y' = 2 \times 1 + 1 \times 0 = 2$. That is the $(-1, 2)$ from the squared paper.

<Explore slug="unit-circle" settings={{ preset: 'rotate' }} input caption="(2, 1) turned 90° lands at (−1, 2), and its length is still 2.236. Drag v, or type rotate, an angle and a vector." />

An arrow's length is its distance from the centre, $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ by Pythagoras, so $(2, 1)$ has length
$\sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$. Turning never changes it. Rotary embeddings rely on this: the angle a word's numbers are
turned by carries its position, and their size stays the same.

The `sin` and `cos` in Python, JavaScript and almost every other language expect **radians**. `Math.sin(90)`
doesn't fail. It turns 90 radians, about 14.3 full turns, and returns $0.894$: a wrong number, with no warning. The
fix is to convert first: multiply degrees by $\frac{\pi}{180}$ (Python also has `math.radians`).

</Misconception>

In a SOH CAH TOA triangle the sides are lengths, so they are never negative. On the circle, $\cos\theta$ and
$\sin\theta$ are the **coordinates** of the turned point, which can be. Left of the centre $\cos\theta$ is negative,
and below it $\sin\theta$ is negative: $\cos 180^\circ = -1$ and $\sin 270^\circ = -1$.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Turn a point θ round the unit circle from $(1, 0)$: it lands at $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$.
- Radians measure the distance travelled: $360^\circ = 2\pi$. Code's `sin` and `cos` want radians, so multiply degrees
  by $\frac{\pi}{180}$.
- $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$; both repeat every $2\pi$, tracing the sine and cosine waves.
- Turning $(x, y)$ by θ gives $(x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta,\ x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta)$ and keeps its length.

</Section>
