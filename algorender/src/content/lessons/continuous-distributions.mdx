<Section step="hook" title="How likely is a wait of exactly 3 minutes?">

You reach a bus stop where buses come at random moments, every 2 minutes on average. How long will you wait? Maybe 1
minute, maybe 2.7, maybe 0.6. A wait can be any amount of time, not just a whole number of minutes. In
[random variables](/learn/random-variables) every value had its own bar: a die lands on 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6, and each
face has a chance. So what is the bar for a wait of exactly 3 minutes? Not 3.0001, not 2.9999: exactly 3.

The answer is a surprise: 0. Yet waits of about 3 minutes happen all the time. Heights, times, the error of a sensor
and the output of a model all work like this. To give them chances you stop asking about single values and ask
about stretches of values, and a chance becomes an **area** under a curve called a density. The most famous density,
the bell-shaped normal curve, is everywhere in machine learning: in the noise a model expects in its data, in how a
network's starting weights are drawn, and in the models that make pictures out of noise.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="continuous random variable">A random variable $X$ that can land anywhere in a stretch of numbers, like a wait or a height, rather than on a list of separate values like a die.</Term>
  <Term name="density f(x)">A curve over the number line, never below 0, with a total area of 1 under it. Its height is a chance per unit of $x$ (per minute, per centimetre), not a chance.</Term>
  <Term name="P(a ≤ X ≤ b)">The chance that $X$ lands between $a$ and $b$. With a density, it is the area under the curve from $a$ to $b$.</Term>
  <Term name="CDF F(x)">The cumulative distribution function, $F(x) = P(X \le x)$: the area under the density to the left of $x$.</Term>
  <Term name="normal distribution">The bell-shaped density with centre $\mu$ (“mu”, its mean) and spread $\sigma$ (“sigma”, its standard deviation, from [random variables](/learn/random-variables)).</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="From bars to a curve">

Let $X$ be the wait. You can't draw a bar for every value, because there are endlessly many, but you can group the
waits into **bins**: one bar for waits from 0 up to 1 minute, one for 1 up to 2, and so on. Each bar is the chance of
a wait in its bin, just like the bars of a random variable.

<Explore slug="distributions" settings={{ preset: 'bars' }} caption="The chance of a wait in each 1-minute bin, then in half-minute and quarter-minute bins, then every bar divided by its width, and the curve the tops close in on." />

With 1-minute bins, the first bar, $P(0 \le X < 1)$, is 0.393. The bars up to 10 minutes add up to 0.993; the other
0.007 is waits longer than 10 minutes. Now split every bin in two. The first minute's 0.393 splits into 0.221 for the
first half-minute and 0.172 for the second, a little more in the first half because short waits are likelier. Halve
the bins again and the first bar is 0.1175.

Every halving takes each bar to about half its height. Keep halving and the bars shrink towards nothing: a bin of width
0, one exact moment, holds no chance at all. That answers the opening question. $P(X = 3) = 0$, and the same goes for
every single value.

So stop drawing chances, and draw **chance per minute**: divide each bar by its width. The first quarter-minute bar
becomes $0.1175 \div 0.25 = 0.47$. A bar's area, height × width, is then its chance again
($0.47 \times 0.25 = 0.1175$), and the areas of all 40 bars add up to 0.993, as before.

Divided by their widths, bins of all three sizes stand at about the same heights. The first bar is 0.393 tall with
1-minute bins, 0.442 with half-minute bins and 0.47 with quarter-minute bins. Narrower bins only trace the same shape
more finely, and as the width shrinks the tops close in on a smooth curve. For this wait it is

$$
f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-x/2}
$$

for $x \ge 0$, and 0 for negative $x$ (a wait can't be negative). It uses $e$ from
[exponents and logs](/learn/exponents-logs): at $x = 0$ it is $\tfrac{1}{2} e^0 = 0.5$, and it falls away from there.
That curve is the **density** of the wait.

<KeyIdea>

**A density turns chances into areas.** Its height $f(x)$ is a chance per unit of $x$, and the chance of landing in a
stretch is the area under the curve over that stretch:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(a \le X \le b) = {} & \text{area under } f \\
& \text{from } a \text{ to } b
\end{aligned}
$$

The whole area under a density is 1, and any one exact value has chance 0.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="A chance is an area">

How likely is a wait of between 1 and 3 minutes? It is the area under the curve from 1 to 3, and you can estimate an
area the way [integrals](/learn/integrals) did, with rectangles. Drag the ends, $a$ and $b$, to try other stretches.

<Explore slug="distributions" settings={{ preset: 'interval' }} caption="A wait of between 1 and 3 minutes: one rectangle, the exact area, then the stretch squeezed to a single value, and the area outside." />

One rectangle as wide as the stretch, 2 minutes, and as tall as the curve at its middle,
$f(2) = \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-1} = 0.184$, has area $0.184 \times 2 = 0.368$. Ten thinner rectangles give 0.3832, and the
exact area, where thinner and thinner rectangles settle, is 0.3834. So about 38% of waits last between 1 and 3 minutes.

For this curve the area to the left of $x$ has a formula, $1 - e^{-x/2}$. It is 0 at $x = 0$, and its slope is
$\tfrac{1}{2} e^{-x/2}$, the curve itself, as [integrals](/learn/integrals) said the slope of an accumulated area must
be. It is 0.3935 at 1 and 0.7769 at 3, and the area between is the difference: $0.7769 - 0.3935 = 0.3834$, the same answer without any rectangles.

Now squeeze the stretch until both ends sit at 1. It has no width, so it has no area: $P(X = 1) = 0$, even though the
curve there is $f(1) = 0.303$ tall. That height is a chance per minute. Multiply it by a width and you get a chance;
with no width, you get nothing.

That settles a question that never came up with dice: do the end points count? $P(1 < X < 3)$ leaves out the two
single values 1 and 3, each with chance 0, so it is 0.3834 too. For a continuous $X$, $<$ and $\le$ give the same
chance. And because the whole area is 1, the chance of a wait outside the stretch, under 1 minute or over 3, is
$1 - 0.3834 = 0.6166$.

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: the area to the left">

The area to the left of $x$ is so useful it has a name: the **CDF**, $F(x) = P(X \le x)$. With it, every chance of a
stretch is a subtraction, as you just saw: $P(a < X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)$.

This time $X$ is how long a phone call lasts, in minutes. Most calls are quick chats of about 2.5 minutes and the rest
are longer calls of about 6.5, so the density has two humps. The challenge slides $x$ from left to right, shading the
area to its left and plotting that area as a second curve. Predict whether $F$ can ever go down, where it climbs
fastest, what it does far to the right, how steep it is at $x = 4.5$, and the chance of a call longer than 4.5
minutes.

<PredictChallenge slug="distributions" settings={{ preset: 'cdf' }} goal={4} />

At 1.5 minutes the area is only 0.063; by 2.5 it is 0.3, and by 4.5 it is 0.615. Moving $x$ to the right only ever adds
area, and no area is negative, so $F$ can climb or stay level but never fall. Each step to the right adds a thin strip
as tall as the density there, so $F$ climbs steeply under a hump and barely moves where the density is low, as it is
between the two humps. Far to the right it levels off at 1, the whole area.

That strip is the reason **the slope of $F$ is the density**. At 4.5, one tiny step $dx$ to the right adds a strip
about $f(4.5)$ tall and $dx$ wide, so $F$ rises by about $f(4.5) \cdot dx$, and its slope there is $f(4.5) = 0.046$. You
met this in [integrals](/learn/integrals): $F$ is the accumulated area $A(x)$, whose slope is the curve it adds up. And
the chance of a call longer than 4.5 minutes is the area to the right, $1 - F(4.5) = 1 - 0.615 = 0.385$.

>

The height of a density is not a chance. It is a chance **per unit**: per minute, per centimetre, per whatever $x$
measures. A chance is always an area, height × width, and a single value has no width. At $x = 1$ the wait's density is
0.303 tall, yet the chance of a wait of exactly 1 minute is 0. What the height does tell you is how crowded the values
are: a short stretch of width $w$ near $x$ holds a chance of about $f(x) \times w$, so where the curve is twice as tall,
values land twice as often.

</Misconception>

A chance can't be above 1, but a density is not a chance. Only its **area** has to be 1, and a narrow curve has to be
tall to hold that much. Here is a reaction time that is equally likely to be anywhere from 0 to 0.5 seconds: its
density is a flat block 0.5 wide, so to have area 1 it must be 2 tall. A reaction time between 0.1 and 0.3 seconds then
has chance $2 \times 0.2 = 0.4$, a perfectly ordinary chance, even though the curve over it is 2 tall.

<Section step="see" title="The bell curve">

The **normal distribution** has the density

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-(x - \mu)^2 / (2\sigma^2)}
$$

It looks busy, but each piece has one job:

- $(x - \mu)^2$ is the squared distance from the centre $\mu$. It is 0 at $\mu$ and the same either side, so the curve is symmetric about $\mu$.
- The minus sign makes the power of $e$ zero or negative, so $e^{\ldots}$ is 1 at the centre ($e^0 = 1$) and falls away on both sides.
- Dividing by $2\sigma^2$ sets the width: one $\sigma$ from the centre the power is $-\tfrac{1}{2}$, whatever $\sigma$ is, so a bigger $\sigma$ spreads the curve wider.
- The number in front, $1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$, makes the whole area exactly 1. With $\sigma = 1$ it is $1/\sqrt{2\pi} = 0.399$, the height of the peak.

<Explore slug="distributions" settings={{ preset: 'normal' }} caption="The normal curve with μ = 0 and σ = 1: σ doubled, then the area within 1, 2 and 3 σ of μ. Drag μ, and μ + σ to change σ." />

Double $\sigma$ to 2 and the curve gets twice as wide, so the peak halves, from 0.399 to 0.199: the area under every
density is 1, and a curve twice as wide must be half as tall. Then come the areas within one, two and three
$\sigma$ of the centre:

- between $\mu - \sigma$ and $\mu + \sigma$: 0.6827
- between $\mu - 2\sigma$ and $\mu + 2\sigma$: 0.9545
- between $\mu - 3\sigma$ and $\mu + 3\sigma$: 0.9973

That is the **68–95–99.7 rule**. It holds for every normal curve, whatever $\mu$ and $\sigma$, because changing them
only slides and stretches the curve, and its bands slide and stretch with it. If marks in an exam are normal with
$\mu = 60$ and $\sigma = 10$, about 68% of marks are between 50 and 70, and about 95% between 40 and 80. The curve
never quite reaches 0, so 0.0027 of the area lies beyond three $\sigma$: rare, but possible.

Why this curve, of all curves? Add up many small, independent effects, and the total comes out close to normal,
whatever the effects look like on their own. That result, the
**central limit theorem**, is the next lesson, and it is
why the bell turns up in heights, measurement errors and averages alike.

</Section>

The wait's area had a formula, $1 - e^{-x/2}$, but the normal curve's area to the left has no such formula. Computers

<Section step="see" title="Samples pile up into the density">

Where do densities come from in practice? Usually from data: draw many values and make a histogram whose bars stand
count ÷ (n × bin width) tall, for $n$ values in all. Then a bar's area is the share of the samples in its bin, and all
the areas add up to 1. Here are calls drawn at random from the two-humped density.

<Explore slug="distributions" settings={{ preset: 'samples' }} caption="10, then 100, 1,000 and 10,000 calls drawn at random: histograms whose bars are count ÷ (n × bin width), next to the density." />

With 10 calls the bars are lumpy, and the share of calls between 2 and 4 minutes is 0.2, far from the area under the
curve there, 0.43. With 100 calls the share is 0.33, with 1,000 it is 0.425, and with 10,000 it is 0.432. The biggest
gap between a bar and the curve falls from 0.471 to 0.012. Shares of samples settle on areas under the density as the
samples pile up, which is the **law of large numbers** at work: the density is what a
histogram of endless samples would look like. Press New values to draw a fresh set: the 10-call histogram changes a lot, the 10,000-call one hardly
at all.

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A measurement that can be any number gives each single value chance 0. Its chances live in a **density** $f$: the chance of landing between $a$ and $b$ is the area under $f$ from $a$ to $b$.
- A density's height is a chance per unit, never a chance. It is never negative and its whole area is 1, but it can be taller than 1 where it is narrow.
- The CDF $F(x) = P(X \le x)$ is the area to the left of $x$. It climbs from 0 to 1 and never falls, its slope is $f$, and $P(a < X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)$.
- The normal density $e^{-(x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} / (\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$ is a bell centred on $\mu$, with about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of its area within 1, 2 and 3$\sigma$.
- Histograms of samples, scaled as count ÷ (n × bin width), settle on the density as the samples pile up.

</Section>
