<Section step="hook" title="A perfect score that means nothing">

In [learning from examples](/learn/supervised-learning) a memoriser scored a perfect 0 on its training examples and
still guessed worse than a plain line on new ones. That was an extreme case of a question every model raises. A model
has a setting for how flexible it is: how many weights, how many layers, how long to train. Turn it up and the model
can follow its training data in more detail. Is that what you want?

This lesson fits curves of rising flexibility to ten noisy points, scores them on points kept apart, asks *why* the
two scores part ways, and ends with k-fold cross-validation, the standard way to choose a model when data are scarce.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="polynomial of degree d">A curve $\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x + w_2 x^2 + \dots + w_d x^d$: a sum of powers of $x$, each times a weight, so $d + 1$ weights in all. Degree 1 is a line, degree 2 a parabola; each extra degree lets the curve bend once more.</Term>
  <Term name="training set, training error">The points a model is fitted to, and its mean squared error (MSE) on them.</Term>
  <Term name="validation set, validation error">Points kept apart from fitting, used only to score models and choose between them, and the MSE there.</Term>
  <Term name="test set">Points kept back until every choice has been made, then used once, for one honest score at the very end.</Term>
  <Term name="underfitting, overfitting">A model too stiff to follow the pattern in the data underfits. One so flexible that it follows the random noise as well overfits.</Term>
  <Term name="bias, variance">Imagine fitting the same kind of model to many training sets. Bias is how far their average fit is from the truth; variance is how much the fits disagree with each other. (This bias has nothing to do with the weight $b$ that linear regression also calls a bias.)</Term>
  <Term name="k-fold cross-validation">Cut the data into $k$ groups called folds. Fit $k$ models, each on all the folds but one, and score each on the fold it left out.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Curves made of powers of x">

The line in [linear regression](/learn/linear-regression), $\hat{y} = wx + b$, has two weights. A polynomial has more:
degree 3 is

$$
\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x + w_2 x^2 + w_3 x^3
$$

It draws a curve, yet for fitting it is no harder than a line. For each point, list its **features**, the powers
$1, x, x^2, x^3$. The prediction is the [dot product](/learn/dot-product) of that row with the weights
$(w_0, w_1, w_2, w_3)$, a weighted sum, just as $wx + b$ is the dot product of $(1, x)$ with $(b, w)$. For
$x = -0.5$ the row is $[1, -0.5, 0.25, -0.125]$. Stack one row per point into a table $X$ and least squares works
exactly as it did for the line: find the weights whose predictions have the smallest mean squared error.
[Gradient descent](/learn/gradient-descent) would get there step by step; the exact least-squares formula gets there in
one go, and that is what the widgets here use. Writing the table $X$ is a short exercise:
[the powers of x](/code/poly-features).

<KeyIdea>

**More degrees, more weights, more bend.** A degree-$d$ polynomial has $d + 1$ weights. The data decide the weights;
you decide the degree, and with it how flexible the curve may be.

</KeyIdea>

The data in this lesson come from one smooth curve,

$$
y = \sin(2.5x) + 0.6x^2 - 0.3
$$

with random noise added to every point, as real measurements have: typically about 0.2 up or down, and its average square is 0.04. The training set is 10 points whose $x$ values are
evenly spread from $-1$ to $1$. The validation set is 40 more points from the same curve and the same noise, at random
$x$ values; no curve is ever fitted to them. Both errors are mean squared errors, so they are on the same scale. In
real life nobody knows the true curve, only the points. That is why the points kept apart matter: they are the only
stand-in for the new data a model will meet.

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: training error against validation error">

Fit degree 1 to the ten training points, then degree 3, then degree 9, and finally every degree from 0 to 9. Predict how
each change moves the training error, where degree 9 ends up on each set of points, which degree the validation points
pick, and how far to trust that pick.

<PredictChallenge slug="overfitting" settings={{ preset: 'degree' }} goal={5} />

The line's training error is 0.23 and degree 3's is 0.0546. Lower, and it had to be: a line *is* a degree-3 curve,
one whose $w_2$ and $w_3$ are 0, so the best degree-3 curve can only match the training points at least as well as the
best line. The same holds for every step up, since degree $d + 1$ can do anything degree $d$ can, with one weight to
spare. So the training error never rises. For degrees 0 to 9 it is 0.676, 0.23, 0.156, 0.0546, 0.0497, 0.0452, 0.0322,
0.00544, 0.00542 and 0. At degree 9, ten weights for ten points, the curve threads through every point, noise and all,
and the error is exactly 0.

The validation error tells another story: 0.743, 0.156, 0.118, 0.0308, 0.0354, 0.041, 0.0718, 0.134, 0.137 and 0.606.
It falls to degree 3 and then climbs, and degree 9's 0.606 is about 20 times degree 3's 0.0308. Drawn against the
degree, it makes a **U**. On the left the curves underfit: too stiff to follow the bends, they miss the training points
and the validation points alike. On the right they overfit: the extra weights are spent following the noise in the ten
training points, and the next points have noise of their own.

<KeyIdea>

**Training error measures memory; validation error measures what carries over.** The training error can only fall as
a model grows more flexible, so on its own it always votes for the most flexible model. Judge flexibility on points the
model never saw.

</KeyIdea>

Flexibility is neither good nor bad in itself. A model too stiff for the pattern underfits, and more flexibility helps
it; a model flexible enough to follow the noise overfits, and less helps. The validation U has its lowest point
somewhere in between, here at degree 3, and only points kept apart can find it.

</Misconception>

Degree 3 won the contest, with 0.0308 on the validation points. Is that the error to expect on new points? Here is a
test set, 40 more points kept back until now: degree 3 scores 0.0421 there, worse than its winning score. Part of the
gap is the luck of the draw. Measured against the true curve, which this lesson can do, degree 3's average error on new
points is 0.0439, and degrees 1 to 5 all score below their true errors on these 40 validation points: they happen to be
easy points. But even without such luck, a score used to pick a winner tends to flatter it. When ten degrees compete on
the same points, the winner is partly the one whose curve happened to suit them. The test set is the cure: it plays no
part in any choice, so its score is honest, and it is used once, at the very end. Look at it, change the model and look
again, and it starts to flatter too.

In [learning from examples](/learn/supervised-learning) a test set chose between the memoriser and the line. With one
choice between two models that set was really doing a validation set's job. Once you choose among many models, the job
splits in two: a validation set for choosing, and a test set for one final score.

choice. Choose on the validation set (or by cross-validation), then score the chosen model once on the test set.

<Section step="see" title="Bias and variance: twenty training sets">

Why does the validation error make a U? The ten training points were one roll of the noise. On another day, at the
same ten $x$ values, the noise would come out differently, and so would every fitted curve. So deal twenty training
sets from the true curve, fit a line and a degree-9 curve to each, and compare them with the truth, which in this
widget you can see.

<Explore slug="overfitting" settings={{ preset: 'bias-variance', seed: 7 }} caption="The true curve dashed; one more training set and its line; then twenty training sets' lines, their twenty degree-9 curves, and each degree's average; then bias² and variance for every degree." />

The twenty lines bunch together, but their average is a line too, and it misses the curve's bends just where each line
does. Measure that miss: at each $x$ from $-1$ to $1$, take the gap between the average fit and the true curve, square
it, and average over $x$. That is the **bias²** (bias squared), here 0.131 for the lines. Now measure how far the twenty
fits scatter around their own average: at each $x$, average the twenty fits' squared distances from their average, then
average that over $x$. That is the **variance**, 0.006 for the lines. A stiff model: big bias, small variance.

The degree-9 curves are the opposite. Each one threads its own ten points, noise and all, so they fan out wildly
between the points, widest near the ends: variance 0.178. Yet their misses point every which way, up for one training
set and down for the next, and averaged they nearly cancel: bias² 0.005. A flexible model: small bias, big variance.
Think of darts: bias is aiming at the wrong spot, variance is a shaky hand.

<KeyIdea>

**Expected error = bias² + variance + the noise's average square.** The expected error is the squared error on a new
point, averaged over every training set the model might have been fitted to and over every new point. The last term,
here 0.04, comes from the noise in the new point itself, and no model can remove it. Raising the degree trades bias for
variance, and the best degree balances the two.

</KeyIdea>

The last frames work bias² and variance out exactly, over every possible training set at these ten $x$ values rather
than twenty of them. Bias² falls as the degree rises, to near 0 from degree 3 on, and variance climbs. Their sum is
smallest at degree 3: bias² 0.0009 plus variance 0.0135 makes 0.0144. Add the noise's 0.04 and degree 3 should average about 0.0544 on new points,
while degree 9's variance alone is 0.246. The validation errors you saw, 0.0308 for degree 3 and 0.606 for degree
9, were one training set's view of these averages, scored on one set of 40 points.

needs more flexibility, not more data.

</Misconception>

<Section step="do" title="k-fold cross-validation, in the widget and in code">

The lesson so far had points to spare: 40 for validation and 40 for testing, each four times the training set. Real
data are rarely that generous. With only 20 points, every point held back to validate is one fewer to fit, and the score then
depends on which points happened to be held back. **k-fold cross-validation** uses every point for both jobs.

<Explore slug="overfitting" settings={{ preset: 'k-fold' }} caption="Twenty points cut into five folds of consecutive points, drawn as a timeline with a row per model; one model fitted and scored, then all five, then the cross-validated error for every degree, and the final fit." />

The 20 points, in the random order they were collected, are cut into 5 folds of 4 consecutive points. Model 1 is fitted
to the 16 points outside fold 1 and scored on fold 1's 4, which it never saw; at degree 3 its MSE there is 0.0807.
Models 2 to 5 do the same with their own folds. In the timeline every column has exactly one marked cell: each point is
scored once, by the one model that never trained on it, and helps train the other four. Pool all 20 squared errors and
average them, and you have the **cross-validated error**, 0.035 for degree 3.

Doing that for every degree from 0 to 9 takes 50 fitted curves, 5 for each degree. The cross-validated errors make the
same U as before, lowest at degree 3. Then the folds have done their job: the final model is degree 3 fitted to all 20
points, so no point is wasted.

With only 20 points, though, the cross-validated error is itself noisy. Type k-fold 10 into the widget's input and the
lowest score is degree 8's, an isolated dip between much worse neighbours; k-fold 2 picks degree 4. A pick that moves
with k is a warning: prefer the simplest degree that scores nearly as well, or get more data.

Cross-validation fits $k$ models for every setting it scores, and they are only for scoring. The model you keep is a
fresh one: the chosen setting fitted to all the data.

</Misconception>

The cost is $k$ fits for every setting tried, instead of 1. Five or ten folds are common. With
$k = n$, one point per fold, it is called leave-one-out. Consecutive folds are fair only when the points come in a random
order. If they were sorted, by $x$ or by date, each fold would be one stretch of the data, so shuffle them first.

Now cut the folds yourself. Fold sizes differ by at most one: each fold holds $\lfloor n/k \rfloor$ points (the whole
part of $n \div k$), and the first $n \bmod k$ folds (the remainder) hold one more. With 10 points in 4 folds that is
sizes 3, 3, 2 and 2: $[0, 1, 2]$, $[3, 4, 5]$, $[6, 7]$ and $[8, 9]$. Code counts folds from 0, so the widget's fold 1
is fold 0 there.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A polynomial of degree $d$ is a weighted sum of the powers $1, x, \dots, x^d$: $d + 1$ weights, fitted by least squares like a line.
- Training error never rises as a model grows more flexible, and reaches 0 when it has a weight for every point. On its own it always votes for the most flexible model.
- Error on points kept apart falls and then rises: a U, with underfitting on the stiff side and overfitting on the flexible side. Choose where it is lowest.
- Bias is a stiff model missing the pattern the same way every time; variance is a flexible model changing with every roll of the noise. Expected error = bias² + variance + noise.
- Choose on a validation set or by cross-validation; score the final choice once on a test set kept out of every choice.
- k-fold cross-validation fits $k$ models per setting, scores every point once, and then refits the chosen setting on all the data.

</Section>
