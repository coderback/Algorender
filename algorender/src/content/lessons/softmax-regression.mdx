<Section step="hook" title="Cat, dog, fox or owl?">

[Logistic regression](/learn/logistic-regression) answers yes-or-no questions: one score $z$, and the sigmoid
$\sigma(z)$ turns it into the chance of "yes". Most questions have more than two answers. Is this photo a cat, a dog, a
fox or an owl? Which of 50,000 words comes next in this sentence? A model for these gives every possible answer, every
**class**, its own score, and needs a way to turn a list of scores into probabilities: each between 0 and 1, all adding
up to 1, the biggest score getting the biggest share.

That way is **softmax**, the last step of almost every classifier and of every language model. This lesson builds it
from three small steps, turns its temperature knob, trains it on three classes of points, and then breaks it: scores
of 1,000 make a computer's numbers overflow, unless the code takes one small step first.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="class">One of the possible answers, such as "cat". There are $K$ of them, numbered $1$ to $K$.</Term>
  <Term name="score, or logit">A number the model gives each class: $z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_K$. Any size, any sign; bigger means "more likely".</Term>
  <Term name="e to a power">$e^x$, with $e \approx 2.718$, as in [exponents and logarithms](/learn/exponents-logs). It is positive for every $x$, grows as $x$ grows, and turns sums into products: $e^{a+b} = e^a \times e^b$. Code writes it `exp(x)`.</Term>
  <Term name="softmax">Turns scores into probabilities: $e$ to the power of each score, divided by the total of those powers.</Term>
  <Term name="temperature T">A positive number every score is divided by before softmax. $T = 1$ leaves the scores alone.</Term>
  <Term name="softmax regression">Logistic regression for many classes: each class's score is a weighted sum of the inputs plus a bias, and training finds the weights.</Term>
  <Term name="cross-entropy">The loss: $-\ln p$ of the true class, averaged over the examples, as in [maximum likelihood](/learn/maximum-likelihood). $\ln$ is the natural log, the log to base $e$.</Term>
  <Term name="overflow, underflow">A result too big for a float becomes $\infty$ (infinity); one too close to 0 becomes 0, as in [floating point](/learn/floating-point).</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Scores into probabilities, in three steps">

A classifier looks at a photo and scores four classes: cat 2, dog 1.5, fox 1 and owl $-0.5$. Scores can be negative, and
their total, 4 here, means nothing. Softmax turns them into probabilities in three steps: divide each score by the
temperature $T$ (here $T = 1$, which changes nothing), take $e$ to the power of each, and divide each power by the
total of the powers. Watch the steps, then drag the scores and the temperature:

<Explore slug="softmax" settings={{ preset: 'bars', seed: 1 }} caption="Four classes' scores, then e to the power of each, then each divided by their total. Then every score plus 10, the temperature halved, a huge temperature, and two classes on their own." />

Step by step: $e^2 = 7.389$, $e^{1.5} = 4.482$, $e^1 = 2.718$ and $e^{-0.5} = 0.607$. Every power is positive, even
owl's, and the order of the scores is kept. Their total is $s = 15.196$. Dividing each power by $s$ gives cat 0.486, dog
0.295, fox 0.179 and owl 0.04, which add up to 1, because each is its part of the same total. In symbols, with
$\Sigma$ (capital sigma) for "add up over every class $j$", as in [sums](/learn/sums-notation):

$$
p_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}
$$

Notice that cat has the biggest score, yet less than half the probability: its three rivals take the rest between them.

Why $e$ and not the scores themselves? Dividing the scores by their total would give cat $2 \div 4 = 0.5$, a different
answer, and owl a negative "probability". Worse, scores that add up to 0 can't be divided by their total at all.
Powers of $e$ are always positive, and they turn the gaps between scores into ratios: $e^a / e^b = e^{a - b}$. Cat is
$e^{0.5} = 1.649$ times as likely as dog because its score is 0.5 higher, whatever the scores are.

>

Softmax divides **powers of $e$**, never the scores themselves. That keeps every probability positive, works for
negative scores and for scores that add up to 0, and makes a class's share depend on the gaps between scores.

</Misconception>

The gaps are all that matter. Add 10 to every score and every power grows by the same factor, $e^{10} = 22026$, and so
does their total: the factor cancels in each division, and the probabilities are exactly what they were. That one fact
will rescue softmax from overflowing later on.

<KeyIdea>

**Softmax only sees the gaps between scores.** $p_k = e^{z_k} / \sum_j e^{z_j}$ is unchanged when the same number is
added to every score, and the ratio of two classes' probabilities is $e$ to the gap between their scores.

</KeyIdea>

The temperature divides every score by $T$ first. Halving it, to $T = 0.5$, doubles every gap, so every ratio is
squared: cat against dog goes from 1.649 to $e^1 = 2.718$, and cat's share rises from 0.486 to 0.662. A small $T$
sharpens the shares towards the top class; a huge one, $T = 100$, shrinks every gap to almost nothing and the shares
flatten towards a quarter each: 0.253, 0.251, 0.25 and 0.246. Dividing by a positive $T$ never changes which score is
biggest, so it never changes the top class. When a chatbot offers a "temperature" setting, this is it: its next word is
drawn from a softmax, a low $T$ makes it pick the likeliest words, a high $T$ lets surprising ones through.

$T$ divides every score by the same positive number, which keeps their order, so the top class stays on top at any
temperature; only its share changes.

</Misconception>

Last, two classes. Divide the top and bottom of $e^a / (e^a + e^b)$ by $e^a$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{e^a}{e^a + e^b} &= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(a - b)}} \\
&= \sigma(a - b)
\end{aligned}
$$

That is the sigmoid of logistic regression, at the gap $a - b$. Cat 2 against dog 1.5, alone, gives cat
$\sigma(0.5) = 0.622$. Logistic regression is softmax with two classes, the second class's score fixed at 0. Pinning it loses nothing, because
only the gap matters, and that gap is logistic regression's one score, the log-odds.

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: three classes in the plane">

**Softmax regression** gives each class its own score at a point $(x, y)$:
$z_k = w_{k1} x + w_{k2} y + b_k$, two weights and a bias per class. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and the
prediction is the class with the biggest. Training is gradient descent on the mean cross-entropy, starting with every
weight and bias at 0. Here are 30 points in three classes, A, B and C, and a query point you can drag. Predict the loss
at the start, the shape of the border between two classes' regions, the shares where all three regions meet, and what
doubling every weight and bias does.

<PredictChallenge slug="softmax" settings={{ preset: 'regions', seed: 7 }} goal={4} />

With every weight and bias at 0, every class scores 0 everywhere, each gets $1/3$, and every point's cross-entropy is
$-\ln(1/3) = \ln 3 = 1.099$. As in maximum likelihood, the mean cross-entropy is $-\ln L$ divided by the number of
points. The gradient is logistic regression's: each point pushes its own class's score up and every other class's
score down. After 80 steps the loss is 0.034, and all 30 points sit in their own class's region.

Two regions meet where their scores tie, $z_A = z_B$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(w_{A1} - w_{B1})\,x + (w_{A2} - w_{B2})\,y \\
&\quad + (b_A - b_B) = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

That is the equation of a straight line, so every border is straight, as with logistic regression. The three borders
meet at one point, here $(0.18, 0)$, where all three scores tie and each class gets $e^z / (3 e^z) = 1/3$. The query
point $(0.75, 0.5)$ gets A 0.8, B 0.155 and C 0.045.

Doubling every weight and bias doubles every score, which is softmax at $T = 1/2$. No border moves, because doubling
keeps every point's scores in the same order, but the query's A rises from 0.8 to 0.961, with no new data and no better
model.

"right 9 times in 10" (whether it is **calibrated**) has to be measured on data it was not trained on.

</Misconception>

<Section step="see" title="When the scores are huge">

Scores can be big: a confident model, or one early in a training run that has gone wrong, can produce scores in the
thousands. Mathematically, softmax of $[1000, 999, 998]$ is softmax of $[2, 1, 0]$, since only the gaps count:
0.665, 0.245 and 0.09. But a computer stores numbers as floats, and models are often trained in float32, whose largest
number is about $3.4 \times 10^{38}$. See what the three steps do with these scores:

<Explore slug="softmax" settings={{ preset: 'overflow', seed: 1 }} caption="Scores of 1,000, 999 and 998 in float32: e to the power of each, then the division; the largest score subtracted first; then scores far below 0, and the log of a probability." />

$3.4 \times 10^{38}$ is $e^{88.72}$, and $e^{1000}$ is about $1.97 \times 10^{434}$, so it rounds to infinity, the bit
pattern with every exponent bit 1, from the floating-point lesson. So do $e^{999}$, $e^{998}$ and their total, and
$\infty \div \infty$ has no sensible value: it is NaN, "not a number", which is also what $0 \div 0$ and
$\infty - \infty$ give. Every probability is NaN, and NaN spreads: the
loss is NaN, the gradient is NaN, and one step of training makes every weight NaN.

The fix is the fact from before. Subtract the largest score, $m = 1000$, from every score: $[0, -1, -2]$. The
probabilities are exactly the same, and now the biggest power is $e^0 = 1$, so nothing can overflow. The powers are
1, 0.368 and 0.135, their total is 1.503, and the shares come out as 0.665, 0.245 and 0.09.

      answer: 0,
      trap: 1,
      why: 'Each e^(z − m) is e^z divided by the same e^m, and so is their total; dividing one by the other cancels it exactly.',
    },
  ]}
>

Subtracting the same number from every score divides every power, and their total, by the same $e^m$, which cancels.
The probabilities are exactly those of the original scores; only the sizes of the numbers along the way change, and
that is the point: the biggest power becomes $e^0 = 1$.

</Misconception>

Very negative scores fail the other way. $e^{-1000}$ is about $5.08 \times 10^{-435}$, far below the smallest positive
float32, a subnormal, $2^{-149}$, about $1.4 \times 10^{-45}$, so it **underflows** to 0. So do the others, their total is 0, and $0 \div 0$ is NaN again.
Subtracting the largest score, $-1000$, gives $[0, -1, -2]$ once more. The same subtraction handles both ends.

Training needs one more thing: the cross-entropy, $-\ln p$ of the true class. Say the scores are
$[1000, 999, 880]$ and the true class is the third, 120 below the top. Even the safe softmax gives it
$p = e^{-120} / s$, which is below the smallest float32 and rounds to 0, and $-\ln 0 = \infty$: an infinite loss.
The way out is never to compute $p$ first:

$$
-\ln p_y = -\ln \frac{e^{z_y - m}}{s} = \ln s + m - z_y
$$

where $s$ is the safe total of $e^{z - m}$. This is **log-sum-exp**. Here $s = 1.368$, $\ln s = 0.313$, and the loss is
$0.313 + 120 = 120.313$: big, as it should be for a class the model all but ruled out, and finite, so training can learn
from it. Machine-learning libraries compute the cross-entropy from the scores this way.

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Softmax turns scores into probabilities, $p_k = e^{z_k} / \sum_j e^{z_j}$, positive and adding up to 1. Only the gaps matter: two classes' probabilities are in the ratio $e^{z_a - z_b}$, and adding the same number to every score changes nothing.
- A temperature divides every score by $T$: small $T$ sharpens, large $T$ flattens towards equal shares, and the order never changes.
- With two classes, softmax is the sigmoid of the gap: logistic regression is softmax with the second score fixed at 0.
- Softmax regression gives each class a linear score; the borders between classes are straight lines, and training is gradient descent on the cross-entropy.
- Softmax's numbers are not measured confidence: scaling every weight and bias changes them without changing any prediction.
- In code, subtract the largest score before exp, so nothing overflows, and compute the loss as $\ln s + m - z_y$ (log-sum-exp), never as the log of a probability that may have rounded to 0.

</Section>
