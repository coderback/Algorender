<Section step="hook" title="A network that knows where to look">

A 28 × 28 photo of a digit has 784 pixels. A fully connected layer with 100 neurons needs 78,400 weights just to look at
it once, and it has to learn separately that a stroke in the top-left corner and the same stroke in the bottom-right are
the same thing.

Convolutional networks fix both problems with one idea: a tiny pattern detector of 9 weights, slid over every position
of the image. This lesson shows what that detector computes, why the same 9 weights work everywhere, and what happens
to its output next.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="kernel">A small grid of weights, here 3 × 3. It is the pattern the layer looks for.</Term>
  <Term name="feature map">The output: one number per window position, large where the image looks like the kernel's pattern.</Term>
  <Term name="stride">How far the window jumps between positions. Stride 2 skips every other position and roughly halves the map.</Term>
  <Term name="ReLU">The activation $\max(x, 0)$: keep positive responses, turn the rest into 0.</Term>
  <Term name="max-pooling">Shrink the map by keeping only the largest value in each 2 × 2 block.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Slide, multiply, add">

<Explore slug="convolution" settings={{ preset: 'vertical-edges' }} caption="The vertical-edge kernel weighs the right column +1, +2, +1 and the left column −1, −2, −1. Each output is the sum of the nine products under the window." />

At every position the window computes the same thing, a dot product of the patch with the kernel:

$$
\text{out}[i][j] = \sum_{a=0}^{2}\sum_{b=0}^{2} \text{image}[i + a][j + b] \cdot \text{kernel}[a][b]
$$

Where the image is darker on the left and brighter on the right, the positive weights win and the output is large. Over
a flat patch, bright or dark, the left and right columns cancel and the output is 0. So the feature map is a picture of
where vertical edges are, and of which way they go.

<KeyIdea>

**One kernel, every position.** The same 9 weights are reused across the whole image (weight sharing). The layer needs 9
parameters instead of tens of thousands, and an edge is detected the same way wherever it appears.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Other kernels, other patterns">

Rotate the weights a quarter turn and the kernel finds horizontal edges instead: the top of the 7 lights up, its
slanted stroke only a little.

<Explore slug="convolution" settings={{ preset: 'horizontal-edges' }} caption="The horizontal-edge kernel: positive where the picture gets brighter going down, negative where it gets darker." />

A kernel of nine equal weights of $\tfrac19$ is an average, so it blurs; a big centre weight minus its neighbours
sharpens. Classic image editors used exactly these hand-made kernels. A convolutional network starts from random ones and
learns its own by backpropagation, usually ending up with edge and colour detectors in its first layer.

</Section>

<Misconception title="Pooling just throws information away">

It does throw away *where exactly* a feature was inside each block, and that is the point: a network that only needs to
know "there is an edge around here" becomes tolerant to small shifts of the input, and every later layer works on a
smaller map. What survives is the strongest response, not an average that a weak neighbour could dilute.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A convolution slides a small kernel over the image; each output is the dot product of a patch with the kernel.
- The feature map is large where the image looks like the kernel's pattern; edge kernels respond to changes, not
  brightness.
- The same weights at every position (weight sharing) means few parameters and a pattern found wherever it is.
- Output size: $\lfloor (n - k)/s \rfloor + 1$. ReLU keeps the positive responses; max-pooling keeps the strongest per
  block and shrinks the map.
- Stacked layers detect patterns of patterns: edges, then corners and curves, then whole shapes.

</Section>
