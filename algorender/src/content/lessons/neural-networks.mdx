<Section step="hook" title="Why one neuron isn't enough">

A single artificial neuron draws a straight line through its inputs and says which side a point is on. That is enough to
separate two clusters, but not to tell the inside of a ring from the outside, or to compute XOR (one or the other, but
not both). In 1969 this limitation stalled the field for a decade.

The fix is to stack neurons in layers. Each hidden neuron draws its own soft straight edge, and the next layer combines
those edges into curves. This lesson shows the failure, then the fix.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="neuron">A weighted sum of inputs plus a bias, passed through a squashing function: $\tanh(w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b)$ or a sigmoid.</Term>
  <Term name="perceptron">The oldest learning rule: when a point is misclassified, add it to (or subtract it from) the weights.</Term>
  <Term name="hidden layer">Neurons between the inputs and the output. Each one becomes a new feature the output can use.</Term>
  <Term name="decision boundary">Where the network's output crosses ½: the line, or curve, between the two classes.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

On data like this the perceptron always finds a separating line in a finite number of mistakes. On XOR it never does:
no straight line puts the two orange corners on one side and the two blue corners on the other.

<Explore slug="perceptron" settings={{ preset: 'xor' }} caption="XOR: the line keeps moving forever, because no line can be right about all four corners." />

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Hidden units bend the boundary">

<Explore slug="mlp" settings={{ preset: 'xor', n: '4' }} caption="Four hidden tanh units, trained by gradient descent with backpropagation. Edge width is the weight's size; colour is its sign." />

Each hidden unit learns a soft straight edge; the output weighs them, so the boundary becomes a combination of edges
that can bend around the corners. The training is the same recipe as linear regression: forward, loss, backward, step.

<KeyIdea>

**Hidden layers turn straight edges into curves.** A network with one hidden layer of enough units can approximate any
reasonable boundary; deeper networks build curves out of curves, using far fewer units.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Break it and fix it">

Use the slider to change the number of hidden units, and the buttons to change the data:

- **XOR, no hidden layer:** the network is back to a single neuron, and accuracy stalls.
- **Circles with 1 or 2 hidden units:** too few edges to surround the inner ring. How many do you need?
- **Step too big:** the loss jumps around instead of falling smoothly.

<Misconception title="More layers always means a better model">

More units and layers can fit more complicated boundaries, but they also fit noise, train more slowly, and with too big a
step they diverge. The right size depends on the data; that trade-off is what validation sets are for.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A neuron draws one straight boundary; the perceptron rule finds one whenever it exists.
- XOR and rings need curved boundaries, which a single neuron can't draw.
- Hidden layers create new features that combine into curves; backpropagation trains every weight at once.
- Next: the same building blocks, arranged differently, become convolutional networks and transformers.

</Section>
