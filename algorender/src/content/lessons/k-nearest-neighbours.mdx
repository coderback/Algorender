<Section step="hook" title="A model that only remembers">

Will you like a song you have never heard? One way to guess: think of the songs you know that are most like it, and
go with what most of them were to you. No rule, just remembered examples.

That is the whole of **k-nearest neighbours**, k-NN for short: no line to fit, no weights to learn. It keeps the
labelled training examples as they are, and to label a new one it finds the $k$ examples most like it and lets them
vote. Two choices remain, how many neighbours vote and which ruler measures "most like it", and one question: what
happens with hundreds of features instead of two? This lesson settles each by experiment.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

As in [learning from examples](/learn/supervised-learning), each example has **features** (what we know) and a
**label** (the answer). Here the features are two numbers, so each example is a point $(x, y)$, and the label is a
**class**: A, B or C. In code the classes are numbered from 0, so A is 0 and B is 1.

<Terms>
  <Term name="training points">The labelled examples k-NN keeps: each a point and its class.</Term>
  <Term name="query point q">A new point whose class we want to predict.</Term>
  <Term name="distance">Unless we say otherwise, the straight line: the L2 distance $\sqrt{d_x^2 + d_y^2}$ from [lengths and distances](/learn/norms-distances), where $d_x$ and $d_y$ are the gaps in $x$ and in $y$.</Term>
  <Term name="the k nearest">The $k$ training points closest to $q$, for a whole number $k$ we choose.</Term>
  <Term name="vote">Each of the $k$ nearest gives one vote to its own class, and the most votes wins. If classes tie, the one whose point is nearest to $q$ wins.</Term>
  <Term name="map of predictions">Every spot of the plane coloured by the class a query there would get. Where the colour changes is the **boundary**.</Term>
  <Term name="training and test mistakes">How many training points the model labels wrongly, and how many test points, kept aside and never seen.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="One query, one vote">

Here are 40 training points in three blobs, 14 of class A and 13 each of B and C, and a query point $q$ at $(0, 0)$,
right between the blobs, where the answer is not obvious. We use $k = 5$. Step through, predicting as you go.

<Explore slug="k-nearest-neighbours" settings={{ preset: 'query', seed: 3 }} caption="40 training points of three classes and a query q at (0, 0) with k = 5: the distances sorted nearest first, the vote, a second k, every point voting, and the map of what k = 5 predicts." />

Plain k-NN can't know which 5 points are nearest without measuring them all: it works out the distance from $q$ to
all 40 training points and sorts them. The chart shows the nearest 15 as bars, each labelled with its point's class.
The 5 nearest lie inside a circle of radius 1.18 around $q$, and they vote 3 for C and 2 for A, so k-NN predicts C.

With $k = 1$ only the nearest point votes, an A point 1.01 away, so $k = 1$ predicts A: same data, same query, a
different answer. With $k = 40$ every point votes wherever $q$ is, always 14 A, 13 B and 13 C, so the prediction is A
everywhere and the map is a single colour.

<KeyIdea>

**k-NN does its work at prediction time.** Training is just storing the points. Plain k-NN measures the distance to
every training point for every prediction: with a million points, a million distances each time. (Cleverer versions
sort the points into regions first, to skip the far-away ones.)

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: k = 1 against k = 15">

Now a harder data set: 121 training points of two classes in four overlapping clouds, class A top right and bottom
left, class B in the other two corners, like a blurred chessboard. No straight line splits them. Another 600 points
from the same clouds are kept aside as a test set. Predict how $k = 1$ and $k = 15$ do on their own training points,
which boundary is more jagged, and which does better on the test points.

<PredictChallenge slug="k-nearest-neighbours" settings={{ preset: 'regions', seed: 3 }} goal={4} />

$k = 1$'s perfect training score is no achievement: each training point is its own nearest neighbour, at distance 0,
so it votes for its own label, as long as no two training points sit at the same spot with different labels. The
memoriser in learning from examples was exactly this, $k = 1$ on a single feature. On the 600 test points the order
flips, 146 wrong for $k = 1$ against 119 for $k = 15$: a new point that lands on a stray's island takes the stray's
class.

The last charts run $k$ through every odd value (with two classes an odd $k$ never ties). Test mistakes fall, with some
wobble, to 112 at $k = 19$, then climb past about $k = 61$. Each of the four clouds holds about 30 training points, so
beyond about 60 neighbours a query's own cloud can't fill its vote, and the two clouds beside it, of the other class,
outvote it: at $k = 97$ the model gets 455 of the 600 wrong, worse than guessing. At $k = 121$ the far cloud of the
same class joins in, every query gets the same vote, 61 A to 60 B, and the model predicts A everywhere: 300 wrong,
exactly half. That overshoot is the chessboard's doing; on most data a big $k$ simply drifts towards predicting the
bigger class everywhere.

<KeyIdea>

**$k$ is the knob between remembering and averaging.** Small $k$ follows every point, noise included: jagged, perfect
on the training points, worse on new ones. Big $k$ averages over wide neighbourhoods and ignores local detail, at the
extreme ignoring $q$ altogether. The best $k$ lies in between, and only points the model never saw can find it.

</KeyIdea>

But not the test set, or it has helped choose the model and no longer measures new points honestly: $k$ is chosen on
a third group of points, a **validation set**.

<Section step="do" title="k-NN in code">

The predictor needs no training step, only a function called for each query. Try it by hand first on six points, with
the query $q = (3, 3)$ and $k = 3$. In the order the code lists them:

1. $(1, 2)$, class 0
2. $(2, 4)$, class 0
3. $(4, 4)$, class 1
4. $(5, 2)$, class 1
5. $(3, 1)$, class 1
6. $(1, 5)$, class 0

Use the **squared** distance $d_x^2 + d_y^2$: a bigger distance always has a bigger square, so it puts the points in
the same order and skips the square root. For point 1 it is $(1 - 3)^2 + (2 - 3)^2 = 4 + 1 = 5$; for the six points in
turn, the squared distances are 5, 2, 2, 5, 4 and 8. The three nearest are points 2 and 3, both at 2, and point 5, at
4. They vote one for class 0 and two for class 1, so the prediction is class 1.

Two rules settle ties. Points equally far go in list order: points 2 and 3 tie at 2, so with $k = 1$ point 2 would
decide. If classes tie in the vote, return the class of the nearest point among them. Your code writes each squared distance into an array `dist` and
counts votes in an array `votes`, one cell per class.

<Section step="see" title="Which ruler? And in which units?">

"Nearest" depends on how distance is measured. In [lengths and distances](/learn/norms-distances) you met three
rulers for the gap $(d_x, d_y)$ between two points: L2, the straight line $\sqrt{d_x^2 + d_y^2}$; L1, city blocks,
$|d_x| + |d_y|$; and L∞, the biggest step, $\max(|d_x|, |d_y|)$. Under each ruler, the points within a given distance
of $q$ fill a different shape: a circle, a diamond, a square. Below are 16 training points of three classes and a
query at $(0, 0)$, with $k = 3$. The panel lists the coordinates of the points near $q$, so you can work out each
distance yourself. Predict the vote under each ruler before you see it.

<Explore slug="k-nearest-neighbours" settings={{ preset: 'metric', seed: 1 }} caption="16 points of three classes and a query q at (0, 0), k = 3: the three nearest under L2, L1 and L∞, each in its own neighbourhood shape, then under L2 with every y gap counted three times." />

Under L2, the 3 nearest are C at $(1, 0.45)$, 1.1 away, A at $(0.8, 0.9)$, 1.2 away, and C at $(-0.45, 1.25)$, 1.33
away: C wins; the next is 1.48 away. Under L1 the C point at $(1, 0.45)$ is 1.45 away, and the two B points on the
axes, $(1.5, 0)$ and $(0, -1.5)$, are 1.5: B wins. Under L∞, which counts only the bigger part of the gap, the A points
on the diagonals are nearest, $(0.8, 0.9)$ at 0.9 and $(-1.05, -1.05)$ at 1.05, with the C point at $(1, 0.45)$ at 1
between them: A wins. One query, one $k$, three rulers, three different predictions.

The units of the features matter just as much. Suppose $y$ were recorded in units 3 times smaller, so every $y$ value,
and every gap in $y$, is 3 times bigger, while $x$ stays as it was. The straight-line distance becomes
$\sqrt{d_x^2 + (3 d_y)^2}$, and the neighbourhood is an ellipse 3 times wider than it is tall: the 3 nearest are B at
1.5, C at 1.68 and B at $(-1.9, 0.1)$, 1.92 away, so B wins. Only the units of $y$ changed. The usual fix is to put
every feature on the same scale before k-NN: subtract its smallest value and divide by its range, largest minus
smallest, so every feature runs from 0 to 1.

<Section step="see" title="Distances in many dimensions">

Real examples often have many more than two features: an image of 28 × 28 pixels has 784. A point with $d$ features
has $d$ coordinates, and the straight-line distance is Pythagoras with $d$ parts, $\sqrt{g_1^2 + g_2^2 + \cdots + g_d^2}$,
where $g_1$ is the gap in the first coordinate, and so on. What happens to distances as $d$ rises? Scatter 200 random
points in a square, then in 10, 100 and 1000 dimensions, each coordinate a random number from 0 to 1, add a random
query $q$, and compare the nearest distance with the farthest.

<Explore slug="k-nearest-neighbours" settings={{ preset: 'dimensions', seed: 3 }} caption="200 random points and a query q, in 2, 10, 100 and 1000 dimensions: the distances from q as a histogram, each divided by the farthest, and nearest ÷ farthest for each dimension." />

In 2 dimensions the nearest point is 0.036 from $q$ and the farthest 0.898: nearest ÷ farthest is 0.04, and the
distances spread all the way from near 0 to the farthest. In 10 dimensions the nearest is 0.572 away and the farthest
1.798, a ratio of 0.32. In 100 dimensions it is 0.77, and in 1000 dimensions, with the nearest 12.41 away and the
farthest 13.68, it is 0.91: every one of the 200 points is about as far from $q$ as every other.

Why? To be very near $q$, a point must be near it in all $d$ coordinates at once, which almost never happens when $d$
is large; to be very far, it must be far in all of them, just as rare. This is the **curse of dimensionality**: when
every point is about equally far, "the $k$ nearest" are hardly nearer than the rest, and their vote says little.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- k-NN keeps the training points. To label a new point it measures the distance to every one, takes the $k$ nearest, and lets them vote; a tie goes to the class of the nearest.
- No training, but slow predictions: plain k-NN measures every training point for each prediction.
- $k = 1$ is perfect on its own training points, which measures memory, not prediction. Small $k$ gives a jagged boundary, big $k$ a smooth one, and $k = n$, every one of the $n$ training points voting, predicts the biggest class everywhere.
- The best $k$ lies in between. Choose it on a validation set, not the test set.
- The ruler (L2, L1, L∞) and the units of each feature decide who is nearest. Rescale features to the same range first.
- In many dimensions all distances bunch together and "nearest" stops meaning much. Fewer, better features beat more of them.

</Section>
