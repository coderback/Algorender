<Section step="hook" title="The same both ways">

Level, noon, racecar: a palindrome reads the same forwards and backwards. Checking whether a whole word is one is easy:
compare its two ends and step inwards, the [two pointers](/learn/two-pointers) pattern. The harder question is finding
the **longest palindrome inside** a string. In “bananas” it is “anana”.

The obvious way checks every substring. A string of n letters has n(n + 1)/2 of them, and checking one can take up to
n/2 comparisons, so the search is O(n³). On 1,000 letters that is 500,500 substrings, and if the letters are all a's,
every check runs to the middle: about 83 million comparisons.

This lesson cuts that twice. Growing each palindrome from its middle is O(n²). **Manacher's algorithm** is O(n): like
KMP in [string matching](/learn/string-matching), it never pays twice for what it has already proved, and here the
proof is a mirror.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="palindrome and substring">A substring is a run of consecutive letters; it is a palindrome when it reads the same both ways.</Term>
  <Term name="centre">The middle of a palindrome: a letter when its length is odd (the c of “abcba”), the gap between two letters when it is even (between the b's of “abba”). n letters have 2n − 1 centres.</Term>
  <Term name="radius">How far a palindrome reaches out from its centre. Manacher records P[i], the radius of the longest palindrome centred at cell i.</Term>
  <Term name="separators">The string T puts # between and around the letters, so “aba” becomes #a#b#a#: 2n + 1 cells, and every centre, letter or gap, is a cell of its own.</Term>
  <Term name="C, R and the mirror">C is the centre of the palindrome found so far that reaches furthest right, and R the cell where it ends. The mirror of cell i is 2C − i, its reflection in C.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Grow from the middle, then borrow from the mirror">

A palindrome is symmetric about its centre, so grow it from there: put two pointers on the centre and step them
outwards while the letters under them match. They stop just outside the longest palindrome with that centre. Try every
centre and keep the longest.

There are 2n − 1 centres, and one can grow for about n/2 steps, so this is O(n²). On ordinary text most centres stop
after a comparison or two, and it needs no extra memory: the usual interview answer. On aaaa…a nothing stops early:
every centre grows until it meets an end of the string, about 500,000 comparisons for 1,000 a's. A
[dynamic programming](/learn/dynamic-programming) table, where s[i..j] is a palindrome when its ends match and
s[i + 1..j − 1] is one, is O(n²) too, with O(n²) memory.

Manacher's algorithm makes two changes. First, it works on T, with # between and around the letters. A gap is now a
cell, so one loop over T's 2n + 1 cells tries every centre, odd and even. And the longest palindrome around any cell
starts and ends on a #, so a radius of p holds exactly p letters of s: P[i] is simply its length.

Second, it remembers the palindrome found so far that reaches furthest right: centred at C, ending at R. Take a centre
i after C but before R. Its mirror, 2C − i, lies the same distance left of C, and the big palindrome makes the letters
around i the reverse of those around the mirror. A palindrome reversed is the same palindrome, so the mirror's
palindrome is around i too, at least as far as the big one reaches. The mirror's radius is already known, so i starts
from min(R − i, P[2C − i]) instead of 0.

<Explore slug="manacher" settings={{ input: 'abacaba' }} caption="T is #a#b#a#c#a#b#a#, 15 cells. The centre on c, cell 7, grows to radius 7, the whole word, so C = 7 and R = 14. Cells 8 to 13 lie inside that palindrome and start from their mirrors: cell 11 copies radius 3 (“aba”) from cell 3 and compares nothing. The run makes 19 comparisons and borrows 6 radius steps from mirrors." />

<KeyIdea>

**Inside the palindrome that reaches furthest right, centred at C and ending at R, the right side mirrors the left. So
a centre i before R starts at radius min(R − i, P[2C − i]), what its mirror already proved, instead of 0. Every
comparison that matches pushes R further right, R never moves back, and each centre has at most one mismatch: fewer
than 2|T| comparisons for the whole string, O(n).**

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Expanding in code, and why the mirror pays">

Here is expanding around all 2n − 1 centres in one loop. An even `centre` is the letter at index centre / 2, so lo
and hi both start on it; an odd one is the gap after the letter at (centre − 1) / 2, so they start on the letters
either side. When the while loop stops, they sit one place outside the palindrome, so its length is hi − lo − 1. The
program finds the longest palindrome in “see”.

<TraceCode
  snippet="expand-around-centre"

  choices={['lo = 1, hi = 2', 'lo = 0, hi = 3', 'lo = -1, hi = 4', 'lo = 0, hi = 2']}
  answer={1}
  why="lo = 1 and hi = 2 start on the two e's, which match, so both step out: lo = 0, hi = 3. hi is past the end of “see”, so the loop stops without reading s[3]. The palindrome runs from lo + 1 to hi − 1: “ee”, of length hi − lo − 1 = 2, and the program prints ee. A loop over the letters alone never tries this gap, and would print “s”."
/>

Forgetting the gaps is the classic bug here.

  title="Expanding around each letter finds every palindrome"

      trap: 0,
      why: 'Each of the 10 letters can centre an odd-length palindrome, and each of the 9 gaps an even-length one: 2n − 1 = 19.',
    },
  ]}
>

An even-length palindrome has no middle letter: its centre is the gap between its two middle letters. Expanding only
around letters finds “aba” but never “abba”, so try the n − 1 gaps as well, with lo and hi starting on the letters
either side. Manacher's separators do the same job: every gap gets a # cell of its own.

</Misconception>

Manacher's start, min(R − i, P[2C − i]), comes in three cases, and in two of them it is already the final radius:

- **The mirror's palindrome ends inside the big one**: i's is exactly as long, and its next comparison is bound to
  fail. In “abacaba”, cell 9 copied radius 1 from cell 5, and c against b failed.
- **It sticks out past the big one's left edge**: only R − i is mirrored, and that is exactly the radius, or the
  palindrome around C would reach further too. In the challenge, cell 9's mirror, cell 5, had radius 5, but R − i was
  3, so P[9] = 3.
- **It ends exactly at the edge**: nothing past R has been compared, so comparing carries on. In the challenge, cell 7
  borrowed 3 from cell 3 and grew to 5.

The visualiser still makes the comparison that is bound to fail, and says so; a tuned version skips it.

<Misconception
  id="copy-mirror-radius"
  title="A centre inside the palindrome copies its mirror's radius"

    },
    {
      q: 'Centre i’s mirror has radius 3, and R − i is also 3. What does Manacher do next at i?',
      choices: ['Sets P[i] = 3 and moves to the next centre', 'Starts from radius 3 and compares the letters just past R', 'Starts from 0, since the mirror reaches the edge'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Radius 3 takes i exactly to R, and nothing past R has been compared, so the palindrome around i may go further.',
    },
  ]}
>

The mirror only speaks for what lies inside the rightmost palindrome, because only there do the letters around i
reflect those around 2C − i. So i starts from min(R − i, P[2C − i]). That copy is final when the mirror's palindrome
ends inside the big one, or sticks out past its left edge, where i's stops exactly at R. Only when it ends exactly at
the edge is the copy a starting point, and comparing carries on past R.

</Misconception>

So a comparison can only match past R, and each match pushes R right for good. One repeated letter, the slowest input
for expanding from zero, shows it best.

The inner loop can run long at one centre, but not at many. Each comparison that matches pushes R right, R never
moves back, and it stops at the end of T, so all the centres together make fewer than |T| matches. Each centre makes
at most one comparison that fails. That is under 2|T| = 4n + 2 comparisons: O(n), counted the way KMP's are.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A palindrome is symmetric about its centre: a letter for odd length, a gap for even. n letters have 2n − 1 centres;
  skip the gaps and every even-length palindrome is missed.
- Checking every substring is O(n³). Expanding from each centre is O(n²) at worst (aaaa…a), fast on ordinary text and
  O(1) in memory: the usual interview answer.
- Manacher puts # between and around the letters, so every centre is a cell and P[i] is the palindrome's length in s.
- It keeps C and R, the palindrome reaching furthest right. A centre i before R starts from min(R − i, P[2C − i]),
  which is final unless the mirror's palindrome ends exactly at the edge.
- Every match pushes R right for good, and each centre has at most one mismatch: under 2|T| comparisons, O(n).
- Next: practise with [Longest Palindromic Substring](/practice/longest-palindromic-substring), where expanding around
  centres is plenty, and [Palindrome, Ignoring Punctuation](/practice/valid-palindrome), two pointers moving inwards.
  Or try your own words on [Manacher's visualiser](/visualisers/manacher).

</Section>
