<Section step="hook" title="A number too small for a computer">

Suppose a language model, the kind of program that writes text one word at a time, reads a 120-word sentence. Say it
gives each word a chance of about one in a thousand. The chance of the whole sentence is all 120 of those chances
multiplied together: about $2^{-1200}$. Written as a decimal, that is a point followed by roughly 360 zeros before the
first digit.

Ask Python or JavaScript to multiply those 120 numbers and the answer is exactly 0. Both store decimals as 64-bit
floats, and the smallest positive number a float can hold is about $2^{-1074}$. The product sank below it, so it was
rounded to 0. That is why programs that work with probabilities do something else: they take **logarithms**, which
turn the product into a sum. Here the sum is $-1200$, an everyday number. By the end of this lesson you will know why
that works, and you will have fixed code that gets it wrong.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="exponent (power)">How many copies of a number to multiply together: $2^{10} = 2 \times 2 \times \cdots \times 2 = 1024$, ten 2s.</Term>
  <Term name="logarithm">The exponent, worked out backwards: $\log_2 1024 = 10$, because ten doublings take 1 to 1024. Read it as "log base 2 of 1024 is 10".</Term>
  <Term name="base">The number being multiplied: 2 for $\log_2$, 10 for $\log_{10}$, and $e$ for $\ln$.</Term>
  <Term name="e">A number, about 2.718, that compound interest levels off at (you will see how below). Logs to base $e$ are called natural logs and written $\ln$.</Term>
  <Term name="log scale">An axis where each step up multiplies by the same amount (1, 2, 4, 8, …) instead of adding.</Term>
</Terms>

Three facts about powers explain every rule in this lesson:

- Multiplying adds the exponents: $2^3 \times 2^2 = 2^5$ (three doublings, then two more).
- Anything to the power 0 is 1: no doublings at all.
- A negative power divides: $2^{-3} = \frac{1}{8}$, three halvings.

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Counting doublings">

<Explore slug="logarithms" settings={{ preset: 'doubling' }} caption="Double from 1 until you reach 1000, halve 1000 back down, then see the same bars on a log scale." />

Starting from 1, ten doublings pass 1000: $2^9 = 512$ falls short and $2^{10} = 1024$ gets there. So $\log_2 1000$ is a
little under 10. More precisely, it is $9.97$, because $2^{9.97}$ is about 1000.

Logarithms don't have to be whole numbers. A fraction of a doubling makes sense: $2^{0.5}$ is about 1.414, and
multiplying by 1.414 twice is one doubling ($0.5 + 0.5 = 1$). So 1000, which sits between $2^9$ and $2^{10}$ and much
nearer the top, has a log between 9 and 10, much nearer 10.

Halving runs the same steps backwards: halve 1000 ten times and you are below 1. That is why
[binary search](/learn/binary-search) needs at most 10 looks to find an item among 1000 sorted ones. It checks the
middle item and throws away the half the item can't be in. Each look halves what is left, so the number of looks grows
like $\log_2 n$: for a million items, only 20.

On a log scale the bars that shot up become a straight staircase, one equal step per doubling. That is why charts of
things that keep multiplying, like savings or the cases in an epidemic, are often drawn on a log scale: steady growth
becomes a straight line.

<KeyIdea>

**A logarithm is an exponent.** $\log_2 N$ is the power you raise 2 to in order to get $N$: the number of doublings from
1 to $N$. It grows very slowly. Doubling $N$ adds just 1 to it.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Products become sums">

Multiply $8 \times 4$. In doublings, 8 is three doublings and 4 is two. So $8 \times 4$ is three doublings followed by
two more, five in all, and $2^5 = 32$. That is the rule logarithms are loved for:

$$
\log(a \times b) = \log a + \log b
$$

It works in every base, and for numbers that aren't whole powers of 2:

<Explore slug="logarithms" settings={{ preset: 'product-uneven' }} input caption="log₂ 10 = 3.32 and log₂ 100 = 6.64 stack up to log₂ 1000 = 9.97. Type product a b to try your own." />

If you add the rounded 3.32 and 6.64 you get 9.96, not 9.97. That gap is only rounding: the logs really are 3.3219…
and 6.6438…, which add to 9.9657….

This is what saves the 120-word sentence. Its probability is a product of 120 small numbers, so its log is a sum of 120
logs. In the exercise below, the words' chances are $2^{-9}$, $2^{-10}$ and $2^{-11}$ in turn (1 in 512, 1 in 1024 and
1 in 2048, all near one in a thousand). Their logs are $-9$, $-10$ and $-11$, and 120 of them add up to a comfortable
$-1200$.

Notice the minus signs. A number below 1 is reached by halving 1, so its log is negative. Every probability is at most
1, so every log-probability is negative, or 0 for a sure thing.

Logs turn **multiplying** into adding, never adding into anything simpler. $\log_2(8 + 8) = \log_2 16 = 4$, but
$\log_2 8 + \log_2 8 = 6$, which is $\log_2 64$. There is no rule for the log of a sum: work out the sum first.

</Misconception>

A negative power is a halving: $2^{-1} = 0.5$ and $2^{-3} = 0.125$. So every positive number has a logarithm.
$\log 1 = 0$, numbers above 1 have positive logs, and numbers between 0 and 1 have negative ones. Only 0 and negative
numbers have no log, and 0 is exactly where the underflowing product ends up.

</Misconception>

<Section step="do" title="Where e comes from">

The number $e$ turns up whenever growth compounds. A bank pays 100% interest once a year: £1 becomes £2. Now pay it as
two halves of 50%. After six months you have £1.50, and the second payment earns interest on that, so you end with
£2.25. Paid in $n$ equal parts, each payment multiplies the pot by $1 + \frac{1}{n}$, so £1 becomes
$(1 + \frac{1}{n})^n$. Split it finer and finer:

<Explore slug="logarithms" settings={{ preset: 'compound' }} caption="Paid yearly, half-yearly, quarterly, monthly, weekly, daily, hourly and every minute." />

Monthly gives £2.613, daily £2.7146, every minute £2.71828, and it never gets past $e = 2.71828\ldots$. Each extra
split adds less than the one before. (Every minute is really £2.718279, a whisker under $e = 2.718281\ldots$.)

Money that grows all the time, at a rate in proportion to what is already there, follows $e^x$: at 100% a year, £1
grows to $e^x$ pounds after $x$ years. The natural log runs that backwards: $\ln x$ is how many years it takes £1 to
grow to $x$ pounds. Swapping inputs and outputs like this reflects a graph in the line $y = x$:

<Explore slug="logarithms" settings={{ preset: 'mirror' }} input caption="Drag P along eˣ and watch Q trace ln x on the other side of the line y = x." />

So $\ln$ undoes $e^x$: $\ln(e^x) = x$. Every base gives the same shape of log, just scaled. One doubling is about 0.693
of a step in base $e$, because $e^{0.693}$ is about 2. So $\ln x = \log_2 x \times \ln 2$, and
$\ln 1000 = 9.97 \times 0.693 = 6.91$.

Machine learning papers usually write $\ln$ as plain $\log$. That is safe, because changing the base multiplies every
log by the same number. It never changes which of two things has the bigger log.

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- $\log_2 N$ is the number of doublings from 1 to $N$: an exponent, worked backwards. Halving $N$ down to 1 takes as many steps.
- $\log(a \times b) = \log a + \log b$ in any base. There is no rule for $\log(a + b)$.
- Numbers below 1 have negative logs. Models add log-probabilities, so a tiny product never has to be stored and never underflows to 0.
- $e \approx 2.718$ is where compound growth levels off, and $\ln$ undoes $e^x$. Changing the base only scales a log.
- A float has a smallest size because it stores a number with a power of 2; the [floating-point lesson](/learn/floating-point) shows how.

</Section>
