<Section step="hook" title="A curve that learned its points by heart">

In [overfitting, validation and bias–variance](/learn/overfitting-bias-variance), a curve with too much freedom
memorised its training points and guessed badly everywhere else. Here is that failure again, on ten new points. A
curve of degree 9, $\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x + w_2 x^2 + \cdots + w_9 x^9$, has 10 weights, and least squares fits it to
10 noisy points. Ten weights for ten points: the curve threads every point, so its training error is 0. Between the
points it swings wildly, and on 9 validation points, kept out of the fit, its mean squared error is 4.446.

Look at its weights. The biggest, $w_7$, is $-1338$. The labels stay between about $-1.5$ and $1.5$, yet the curve is
built from terms in the hundreds that almost cancel: the signature of a model bending itself around noise. Rather than
take that freedom away, **charge the model for big weights**, so it uses them only when the data pay for it. That
charge is **regularisation**, in two flavours, ridge and lasso, which behave very differently.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="weights w">The numbers a model learns: $w_1, w_2$ for a model with two features, or $w_0, \ldots, w_9$ for the curve. Together, a vector $w$.</Term>
  <Term name="training error S(w)">The total of the squared errors $(\hat{y} - y)^2$ over the training points. Least squares picks the $w$ with the smallest $S$, written $\hat{w}$. (A total, not a mean, in this lesson; a mean would only rescale $\lambda$.)</Term>
  <Term name="charge (penalty)">An extra cost, added to $S(w)$, that grows with the size of the weights. The model minimises $S(w)$ plus the charge.</Term>
  <Term name="λ (lambda)">How much the charge counts: a number $\lambda \ge 0$ you choose. $\lambda = 0$ is plain least squares.</Term>
  <Term name="ridge">Charges $\lambda \lVert w \rVert_2^2 = \lambda(w_1^2 + w_2^2 + \cdots)$, the squared L2 length from [lengths and distances](/learn/norms-distances). In neural networks it is called weight decay.</Term>
  <Term name="lasso">Charges $\lambda \lVert w \rVert_1 = \lambda(|w_1| + |w_2| + \cdots)$, the L1 length: the sizes added up.</Term>
  <Term name="ŵ(λ) and its path">The weights that minimise $S$ plus the charge at a given $\lambda$, and the path they trace as $\lambda$ grows.</Term>
  <Term name="intercept">A weight that only lifts the whole prediction up or down, like $w_0$. It is left uncharged.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Charging the curve for its weights">

Ridge fits the curve by minimising

$$
\begin{aligned}
&S(w) \\
&\quad + \lambda\,(w_1^2 + w_2^2 + \cdots + w_9^2)
\end{aligned}
$$

with $S(w)$ the total squared error over the 10 training points. $w_0$ is not charged: it only sets the curve's
height, and there is no reason to prefer curves that sit near $y = 0$. The fit minimises that total, but the chart and
the numbers below report means, the training MSE over 10 points and the validation MSE over 9, so the two compare
fairly. Step through $\lambda$ from 0 to 100, predicting before each step, and watch the curve, the sizes of its
weights (on a log scale, each gridline a hundred times the one below) and the two errors.

<Explore slug="regularisation" settings={{ preset: 'curve' }} caption="Left: the training points (larger dots), the validation points (smaller dots) and the fitted curve. Middle: the size of each weight, on a log scale. Right: the training and validation error at each λ tried so far." />

The squares of the weights at $\lambda = 0$ add up to about 3 million, so even $\lambda = 0.000001$ makes a charge the
fit notices: the biggest weight drops from 1338 to 191, and the validation MSE falls from 4.446 to 0.288, while the
training MSE rises from 0 to 0.036. At $\lambda = 0.01$ the curve is a gentle wave, with training MSE 0.067 and
validation MSE 0.089, the lowest of any $\lambda$ here. Then the charge outweighs the fit: at $\lambda = 1$ the
validation MSE is back up to 0.299, and at $\lambda = 100$ every charged weight is squeezed almost to 0, leaving a flat
line at $-0.186$, the mean of the training labels, with validation MSE 0.605: underfitting. Over the sweep the training
MSE only ever rises, from 0 to 0.469, while the validation MSE makes a U, like the one against the degree of the curve.

<KeyIdea>

**Regularisation trades training error for smaller weights.** A good $\lambda$ lowers the error on data the model
never saw, so choose it by the validation error (or cross-validation), never by the training error, which always
prefers $\lambda = 0$.

</KeyIdea>

<Section step="see" title="Two weights: a bowl and a circle">

Why does a charge shrink weights the way it does? With two weights you can see it. Take a model
$\hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2$ fitted to 8 points whose two features rise and fall together. Every column of the data
averages 0 (it is centred), so no intercept is needed. The training error $S(w)$ is a bowl over the plane of weights
$(w_1, w_2)$, drawn with contours as in [contour maps](/learn/contour-maps). Because the features move together, the
bowl is a tilted valley. Its lowest point, the least-squares weights, is $\hat{w} = (1.64, 1.21)$, where $S = 1.92$.

Ridge minimises $S(w) + \lambda(w_1^2 + w_2^2)$. Weights of the same L2 length pay the same charge, so they make a circle
round the origin. Before each step, predict where on its circle ridge's answer sits, and which way it heads as
$\lambda$ grows.

<Explore slug="regularisation" settings={{ preset: 'ridge' }} caption="Left: the training error S over the weights (w₁, w₂), the least-squares weights ŵ, and ridge's weights as λ grows. Right: w₁ and w₂ against λ, which doubles at each tick from 1." />

At $\lambda = 1$ ridge's weights are exactly $(1.6, 1.2)$: $S$ there is 2, a little above 1.92, and the charge is
$1 \times (1.6^2 + 1.2^2) = 4$, the same all round the circle of radius 2. So ridge picked the point of that circle
with the lowest $S$: where a contour of $S$ just **touches** the circle (a contour that crossed it would leave lower
ground on the circle).

At $\lambda = 16$ the weights are $(1.2, 0.97)$, on a circle of radius 1.54 touched by the contour $S = 14.15$. They
are not on the straight line from $\hat{w}$ to the origin: $w_2/w_1$ is 0.81 against 0.74 at $\hat{w}$, and the
circle's point on that line, $(1.24, 0.92)$, has $S = 14.26$. In a tilted valley, nearest is not lowest, and ridge
leans towards sharing the weight between features that move together. As $\lambda$ doubles up to 256 both weights
shrink, to $(0.26, 0.22)$, but neither reaches 0.

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: lasso">

Lasso keeps the bowl but changes the charge to $\lambda(|w_1| + |w_2|)$, the L1 length instead of the squared L2
length. Same 8 points, same tilted valley. Predict which weight falls further at first, what happens to $w_2$ as
$\lambda$ grows, where the contour of $S$ meets the outline of equal charge, and what happens to $w_1$.

<PredictChallenge slug="regularisation" settings={{ preset: 'lasso' }} goal={4} />

Weights of equal L1 length make a diamond, the L1 unit ball scaled up. By $\lambda = 16$, $w_2$ has fallen from 1.21
to 0.99 while $w_1$ only went from 1.64 to 1.54: lasso's weights are $(1.54, 0.99)$, and the contour $S = 4.54$
touches the diamond on a flat side. The two features carry much the same information, so the fit loses little when
$w_1$ stands in for $w_2$.

At $\lambda = 86.18$, $w_2$ becomes **exactly** 0 and stays there: at $\lambda = 128$ the weights are $(0.44, 0)$, and
the contour touches the diamond at its corner on the $w_1$ axis. Then $w_1$ falls steadily, by the same amount for
each unit of $\lambda$, and becomes exactly 0 too, at $\lambda = 156$; beyond that both weights are 0.

Why exact zeros? Move $w_2$ off 0 by a small amount $h$. The charge goes up by $\lambda |h|$: a fixed price per unit,
however small $h$ is. The fit improves by at most the slope of $S$ along $w_2$ times $h$. Once $\lambda$ is bigger
than that slope, every move off 0 costs more than it gains, so $w_2$ stays at 0. Ridge's charge $\lambda w_2^2$ costs
$\lambda h^2$ for a tiny $h$, next to nothing, so some move off 0 always pays.

<KeyIdea>

**Ridge shrinks; lasso selects.** A circle has no corners, but a diamond's corners sit on the axes, so lasso sets
weights to exactly 0 one by one as $\lambda$ grows, dropping those features from the model.

</KeyIdea>

<Section step="do" title="Ridge in code">

Ridge has a formula, found where the slope is 0 in every direction. In [linear regression](/learn/linear-regression)
the chain rule gave the slope of the mean squared error along $w$ as $-\frac{2}{n}\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)\,x_i$. For
the total $S$ drop the $\frac{1}{n}$: with the error $e = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 - y$ at each point, the slope of $S$ along
$w_1$ is $2\sum e\,x_1$, twice column 1 of the data dotted with the errors. The slope of $\lambda(w_1^2 + w_2^2)$ along
$w_1$ is $2\lambda w_1$. Set their sum to 0 and halve it; writing $x_1$ and $x_2$ for the feature columns and using the
[dot product](/learn/dot-product),

$$
\begin{aligned}
&x_1 \cdot (w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 - y) + \lambda w_1 = 0 \\
&w_1 (x_1 \cdot x_1) + w_2 (x_1 \cdot x_2) \\
&\qquad - x_1 \cdot y + \lambda w_1 = 0 \\
&(x_1 \cdot x_1 + \lambda)\,w_1 + (x_1 \cdot x_2)\,w_2 \\
&\qquad = x_1 \cdot y
\end{aligned}
$$

The slope along $w_2$ gives a second equation. With the data as a table $X$ (one row per point, one column per
feature), together they are

$$
(X^\top X + \lambda I)\,w = X^\top y
$$

$X^\top$, "X transposed", is $X$ with rows and columns swapped, so entry $[i][j]$ of $X^\top X$ is column $i$ dotted
with column $j$, and $X^\top y$ is each column dotted with $y$. $I$ is the identity, 1s on the diagonal and 0s
elsewhere, so $\lambda I$ adds $\lambda$ to each diagonal entry. For the 8 points, $X^\top X$ has rows $(32, 21)$ and
$(21, 26)$, and $X^\top y = (78, 66)$. With $\lambda = 16$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
48\,w_1 + 21\,w_2 &= 78 \\
21\,w_1 + 42\,w_2 &= 66
\end{aligned}
$$

whose answer is $(1.2, 0.97)$, the weights the widget showed. Solving such equations is Gaussian elimination, the
`solve` of the least-squares project, if you built it ([solve Ax = b](/code/solve-system)); the exercise hands it to
you, with `transpose`, `matmul` and `matvec`. Your part is ridge itself: build $X^\top X$, add $\lambda$ to the
diagonal, solve. The run draws $X^\top X + \lambda I$ as a table.

<CodeExercise exercise="ridge-regression" />

One test has two identical columns: $X^\top X$ has rows $(10, 10)$ and $(10, 10)$, and elimination meets a zero pivot.
With $\lambda = 1$ the diagonal becomes 11, and ridge splits the weight equally, $13/21 \approx 0.62$ each.

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Penalties as beliefs">

There is a second way to read the charge. Before seeing any data you might believe that each weight is probably
small. Write that belief, a **prior**, as a bell curve over each weight, centred at 0. A bell curve falls away in a
particular way: every extra unit of $w^2$ multiplies the believability by the same fraction $r$, below 1, so it is
proportional to $r^{w^2}$. The chance of two things together is the product of their
chances, so beliefs about separate weights multiply: $r^{w_1^2} \times r^{w_2^2} = r^{w_1^2 + w_2^2}$. If the noise in
the labels is bell-shaped too, the believability of the data given the weights is likewise proportional to
$q^{S(w)}$, with $q$ below 1 because a bigger miss is less believable. The most believable weights, data and prior
together, make $q^{S(w)} \times r^{w_1^2 + w_2^2}$ as big as possible. Write $r$ as a power of $q$, $r = q^\lambda$,
and multiplying powers adds exponents:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&q^{S(w)} \times q^{\lambda (w_1^2 + w_2^2)} \\
&\quad = q^{S(w) + \lambda (w_1^2 + w_2^2)}
\end{aligned}
$$

As $q$ is below 1, that is biggest when the exponent is smallest: exactly ridge. So **ridge finds the most believable
weights under a bell-curve prior**, an estimate called maximum a posteriori, or MAP ("posterior": after seeing the
data). A narrower bell, a stronger belief that the weights are small, falls away faster, so $\lambda$ is bigger. A
prior with a sharp point at 0, falling away with $|w|$ instead of $w^2$ (a Laplace prior), gives lasso.

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Regularisation minimises the training error plus a charge for big weights: $\lambda \lVert w \rVert_2^2$ for ridge, $\lambda \lVert w \rVert_1$ for lasso. $\lambda = 0$ is plain least squares.
- The training error only rises with $\lambda$; the validation error makes a U. Choose $\lambda$ by validation, never by training error.
- The answer sits where a contour of the training error just touches the outline of equal charge: a circle for ridge, a diamond for lasso.
- Ridge shrinks every weight and almost never reaches exactly 0. Lasso sets weights exactly to 0 one by one, so it also picks features.
- Ridge solves $(X^\top X + \lambda I)w = X^\top y$, which has one answer for any $\lambda > 0$, even when features copy each other. Lasso needs a short search, such as coordinate descent.
- Leave the intercept uncharged, and put the features on similar scales first.
- With bell-shaped noise, ridge is the most believable weights under a bell-curve prior on each weight (MAP), and lasso under a Laplace prior; a stronger belief means a bigger $\lambda$.

</Section>
