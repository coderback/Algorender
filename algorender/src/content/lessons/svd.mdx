<Section step="hook" title="How few numbers does a picture need?">

A 24 × 24 grayscale picture is 576 numbers. But most pictures aren't random: neighbouring rows look alike, and whole
regions share the same pattern. The **singular value decomposition** (SVD) finds that shared structure and lists it
from most to least important, so you can keep the important part and throw the rest away.

The same idea compresses images, removes noise, powers recommender systems and is the engine inside PCA.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="rank-1 piece">A column pattern $u$ times a row pattern $v^\top$: every row of the piece is the same row pattern, scaled. It takes $m + n$ numbers instead of $m \cdot n$.</Term>
  <Term name="singular value σ">How strongly a piece counts. The SVD sorts them: $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$.</Term>
  <Term name="rank">How many pieces have $\sigma > 0$: the number of pieces that rebuild the matrix exactly.</Term>
  <Term name="energy">The share of $\sum \sigma_i^2$ that the pieces kept so far account for.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Rebuild a face one piece at a time">

<Explore slug="svd" settings={{ preset: 'smiley' }} caption="Each step adds the next piece σ·u·vᵀ. Blue in the piece adds brightness, orange takes it away." />

Every matrix $A$ can be written as

$$
A = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^\top + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^\top + \dots
$$

with the $u$'s perpendicular to each other, the $v$'s too, and the $\sigma$'s in decreasing order. The first piece alone
already has about 80% of the smiley's energy; by the fifth the face is clearly there. Each piece costs $24 + 24 + 1 = 49$
numbers, so five pieces are 245 numbers instead of 576.

Some pictures are simple enough to have only a couple of pieces at all:

<Explore slug="svd" settings={{ preset: 'checker' }} caption="A checkerboard is an even gray plus one ± pattern: rank 2, however many squares it has." />

<KeyIdea>

**The first $k$ pieces are the best possible picture you can build from $k$ pieces** (the Eckart–Young theorem). Nothing
with rank $k$ comes closer to $A$, and the squared error left over is exactly the sum of the squared $\sigma$'s you dropped.
That is why sorting by $\sigma$ is the whole trick: when the $\sigma$'s fall quickly, a few pieces carry almost everything.

</KeyIdea>

There is also a geometric reading, the one the eigenvectors lesson prepares you for. Written as $A = U \Sigma V^\top$,
any matrix acts in three moves: $V^\top$ rotates (or reflects) the space, $\Sigma$ stretches it along the axes by the
singular values, and $U$ rotates it again. A circle always lands on an ellipse whose half-axes are the $\sigma$'s.

</Section>

It keeps no pixels at all. Each piece is a pattern spread over the whole picture, and every pixel changes a little as
each piece is added. What it keeps is the most important *patterns*, which is why a compressed picture looks blurry
everywhere rather than missing some spots.

</Misconception>

<Misconception
  id="rank-is-size"
  title="A bigger or busier picture always has a higher rank"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'A 100 × 100 checkerboard of 1-pixel black and white squares. What is its rank?',
      choices: ['100', '2', '1', '50'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Every row is one of two patterns, the second being all ones minus the first. Two independent row patterns: rank 2, whatever the size.',
    },
    {
      q: 'Picture A is 24 × 24 with three horizontal stripes of different grays. Picture B is 24 × 24 random noise. Which has the higher rank?',
      choices: ['A, because it has more distinct regions', 'B: every row of A is a multiple of one all-ones row, but noise shares no row pattern', 'Neither: both are 24 × 24, so both have rank 24'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 2,
      why: 'Each row of A is one gray all the way across, a multiple of (1, 1, …, 1), so A has rank 1. Noise has rank 24, the most a 24 × 24 picture can have.',
    },
  ]}
>

Rank counts independent *row patterns*, not pixels, regions or edges. A checkerboard of any size is rank 2, a single
stripe of any width is rank 1, and a small patch of random noise can have the highest rank its size allows. Size only
sets the upper limit.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Any matrix is a sum of rank-1 pieces $\sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$, sorted by singular value.
- The first $k$ pieces are the best rank-$k$ approximation; the error is the dropped $\sigma$'s.
- Fast-falling $\sigma$'s mean structure you can compress; flat ones mean noise.
- Geometrically, every matrix rotates, stretches along axes, and rotates again.

</Section>
