<Section step="hook" title="Same arrow, different ruler">

Ask for directions in a city laid out on a grid and you might hear "3 blocks east, then 3 blocks north". In a city
whose streets run on a slant you would hear different numbers for the very same walk, because the streets you count
along point other ways. The walk hasn't changed; only the ruler has.

Vectors work the same way. The numbers $(3, 3)$ aren't the arrow itself: they are how many steps the arrow takes along
$\hat{\imath}$ and $\hat{\jmath}$. Choose two other arrows to count along and the same vector gets new numbers. Picking
good arrows to count along is one of the most useful moves in machine learning: later in the path, principal component
analysis finds the best axes for a cloud of data, and here you will see a matrix that looks complicated on the usual
grid become two simple stretches on its own.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="basis">Two arrows $b_1, b_2$ that you measure every vector against. They must not lie on one line, or some points can't be reached. The standard basis is $\hat{\imath} = (1, 0)$ and $\hat{\jmath} = (0, 1)$.</Term>
  <Term name="coordinates c">The numbers $c = (c_1, c_2)$ with $v = c_1 b_1 + c_2 b_2$: how many steps along $b_1$, then along $b_2$. Change the basis and they change.</Term>
  <Term name="P = [b₁ b₂]">The matrix whose columns are $b_1$ and $b_2$. It turns coordinates in the new basis into ordinary ones: $v = P c$.</Term>
  <Term name="P⁻¹">The inverse of $P$, from the lesson on inverses. It goes the other way: $c = P^{-1} v$.</Term>
  <Term name="eigenvector">A direction a matrix $A$ only stretches, without turning it: $A b = \lambda b$, where the number $\lambda$ (“lambda”) is the stretch.</Term>
  <Term name="eigenbasis">A basis made of a matrix's eigenvectors.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="One arrow, two sets of coordinates">

<Explore slug="change-of-basis" settings={{ preset: 'coords' }} caption="v = (3, 3) on the standard grid, then on the grid of b₁ = (2, 1) and b₂ = (−1, 1). The arrow stays put; the grid behind it changes." />

On the ordinary grid $v = (3, 3)$: 3 steps along $\hat{\imath}$ and 3 along $\hat{\jmath}$. Now take the basis
$b_1 = (2, 1)$ and $b_2 = (-1, 1)$. The grid drawn behind $v$ is theirs: its lines are whole steps along $b_1$ and
along $b_2$. It looks like the moved grids of “Matrices move space”, but there every arrow moved with the grid. Here
$v$ stays exactly where it was, and only the grid behind it is redrawn. Reading off that grid, $v$ is 2 steps along
$b_1$ and 1 along $b_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= 2\,b_1 + 1\,b_2 \\
  &= (4, 2) + (-1, 1) = (3, 3)
\end{aligned}
$$

That is the recipe $(a, b)$ from the last lesson, where every point in the span of two vectors was $a$ of the first plus
$b$ of the second. Those recipe numbers are the point's **coordinates** in the basis. So in this basis $v$ has
coordinates $c = (2, 1)$. The order of the basis counts: list $b_2$ first and the coordinates become $(1, 2)$.

Stack $b_1$ and $b_2$ side by side as the columns of a matrix:

$$
P = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

From the lesson on matrices, $P c$ is $c_1$ times the first column plus $c_2$ times the second, which is exactly
$c_1 b_1 + c_2 b_2$. So $P c = v$: **$P$ turns new coordinates into standard ones.** To go the other way, from $v$ to
$c$, undo $P$ with the inverse formula from the lesson on inverses:

$$
P^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det P &= 2 \cdot 1 - (-1) \cdot 1 = 3 \\
c = P^{-1} v &= \tfrac{1}{3}(3 + 3,\ -3 + 6) = (2, 1)
\end{aligned}
$$

That only works because $\det P \neq 0$: if $b_1$ and $b_2$ lay on one line, their grid would collapse and most points
would have no coordinates at all.

<KeyIdea>

**A basis is a choice of ruler.** With $b_1, b_2$ as the columns of $P$: $v = P c$ turns new coordinates into standard
ones, and $c = P^{-1} v$ turns standard ones into new. The arrow never moves; only its numbers do.

</KeyIdea>

<Misconception
  id="p-not-inverse"
  title="To get the new coordinates, multiply v by P"
  probes={[

      choices: ['(11, 3)', '(−1, 3)', '(5, 3)'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: '−1·(1, 0) + 3·(2, 1) = (5, 3), so c = (−1, 3). (11, 3) is P·v: that treats (5, 3) as new coordinates and converts them the wrong way.',
    },
    {
      q: 'P has the new basis vectors as its columns. Which gives a vector’s new coordinates c from its standard ones v?',
      choices: ['c = P·v', 'c = P⁻¹·v', 'c = v, since the vector is the same'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'P·c = v turns new coordinates into standard ones, so going from v to c needs P’s inverse: c = P⁻¹·v.',
    },
  ]}
>

$P$ is built from the new basis, so it feels like the matrix that takes you *into* the new basis. It does the opposite.
$P c$ means "$c_1$ of $b_1$ plus $c_2$ of $b_2$", which is the arrow written in ordinary numbers. Going from ordinary
numbers to new coordinates means undoing that: $c = P^{-1} v$. A quick check settles it: $P$ times the coordinates of
$b_1$ in its own basis, $(1, 0)$, must give $b_1$ itself, and it does, because $(1, 0)$ picks out $P$'s first column.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: back to ordinary coordinates">

Now the other direction. On the grid of $b_1 = (1, 1)$ and $b_2 = (-1, 1)$, a vector has coordinates $c = (3, 1)$.
Before each step, predict where it is on an ordinary graph, which matrix gets it there, and where one more step along
$b_2$ takes it.

<PredictChallenge slug="change-of-basis" settings={{ preset: 'back' }} goal={3} />

Three steps along $b_1$ reach $(3, 3)$, and one along $b_2$ adds $(-1, 1)$, so the tip is at $(2, 4)$. That is $P c$,
and each of its parts is a row of $P$ dotted with $c$, as in the lesson on multiplying matrices:
$1 \times 3 + (-1) \times 1 = 2$ and $1 \times 3 + 1 \times 1 = 4$. One more step along $b_2$ adds a whole $b_2$ arrow,
moving the tip to $(1, 5)$, not one square up. When the standard grid comes back, the arrow doesn't move. Only the grid
behind it does.

<Misconception
  id="vector-moves"
  title="Changing the basis moves the vector"
  probes={[
    {
      q: 'v = (−2, 5) in standard coordinates. In a new basis its coordinates come out as (1, 4). Where is v’s tip on an ordinary graph now?',
      choices: ['(1, 4)', '(−2, 5)', '(−1, 9)'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Choosing a basis only changes how v is described. The arrow is the same, so on an ordinary graph its tip is still at (−2, 5).',
    },
    {
      q: 'You switch from the standard basis to b₁ = (4, 0) and b₂ = (0, 4). What happens to the arrow v and to its coordinates?',
      choices: ['The arrow grows 4 times longer; its coordinates stay the same', 'The arrow stays the same; its coordinates become a quarter as big', 'Both shrink to a quarter'],
      answer: 1,
      trap: 0,
      why: 'Each new step is 4 times as long, so a quarter as many of them cover the same arrow. The arrow itself doesn’t change.',
    },
  ]}
>

When the numbers describing something change, it is natural to think the thing itself changed. Here it didn't: a
matrix *as a transformation* moves arrows, but a change of basis only re-reads the same arrow on a different grid. The
same $P$ can play either role, so it helps to ask "did the arrow move, or did the ruler?" Stretching a basis vector $k$
times makes its coordinate $k$ times smaller, and leaves the arrow alone.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="The ruler a matrix likes best">

Some bases make a matrix simple. Take $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. On the standard grid it
stretches and slants every square, and its four numbers don't say simply what it does.

<Explore slug="change-of-basis" settings={{ preset: 'eigenbasis' }} caption="A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]] on the standard grid, then its two eigen directions, then the grid of those directions before and after A." />

Two directions are special: $A$ only stretches them, without turning them. $b_1 = (1, 1)$ goes to
$A b_1 = (3, 3)$, which is $3 b_1$, and $b_2 = (1, -1)$ goes to $A b_2 = (1, -1)$, which is $1 b_2$. Arrows like these,
with $A b = \lambda b$, are **eigenvectors** of $A$, and the stretches are $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$. (A later
lesson on eigenvectors shows how to find them; here they are given.) Use them as the basis. A point with coordinates
$(1, 1)$ in this basis lands at $(3, 1)$ in the same basis: $A$ just multiplies the first coordinate by 3 and the
second by 1.

“$A$ written in the eigenbasis” is the matrix that does this: it takes a point's eigen coordinates to the eigen
coordinates of where $A$ sends it. You can build it from three moves, read right to left in $P^{-1} A P$: $P$ turns
eigen coordinates into an ordinary arrow, $A$ moves the arrow, and $P^{-1}$ reads the moved arrow back in eigen
coordinates. Here that gives

$$
D = P^{-1} A P = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

a **diagonal** matrix: the stretches on the diagonal, in the order of the basis (the first basis vector's stretch
first), and zeros everywhere else. Turned around, $A = P D P^{-1}$: change into eigen coordinates ($P^{-1}$), stretch
each one ($D$), change back ($P$). This is why the eigenbasis is so handy. Applying $A$ ten times is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{10} &= (P D P^{-1})(P D P^{-1}) \cdots (P D P^{-1}) \\
       &= P D^{10} P^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

because each $P^{-1} P$ in the middle is the identity $I$, which changes nothing. And $D^{10}$ just has $3^{10}$ and
$1^{10}$ on its diagonal.

Now write the conversion yourself: given a basis and a vector in ordinary coordinates, find its coordinates in the
basis. You need $c_1$ and $c_2$ with $c_1 b_1 + c_2 b_2 = v$, two equations in two unknowns, which is what $P^{-1} v$
solves.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A **basis** $b_1, b_2$ is a ruler; a vector's **coordinates** $c$ in it satisfy $v = c_1 b_1 + c_2 b_2$.
- With $P = [b_1\ b_2]$: $v = P c$ (new to standard) and $c = P^{-1} v$ (standard to new), as long as $\det P \neq 0$.
- Changing the basis never moves the arrow, only its numbers. Stretch a basis vector $k$ times and its coordinate
  shrinks $k$ times.
- An **eigenvector** is a direction $A$ only stretches, $A b = \lambda b$. In a basis of them $A$ is diagonal,
  $D = P^{-1} A P$, so $A = P D P^{-1}$ and powers of $A$ become powers of its stretches.

</Section>
