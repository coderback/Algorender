<Section step="hook" title="Five copies, one truth">

A bank keeps your balance on five servers, so that losing one machine loses nothing. Now two requests arrive at
once: "withdraw 80" and "withdraw 50", with 100 in the account. If two servers each accept a different one first, the
copies disagree, and when one of them crashes, which balance was the real one?

Keeping copies is easy. Keeping them **in agreement** while machines crash, restart and lose touch with each other is
the hard part, and it has a name: **consensus**. The usual trick is a **replicated state machine**. Every server holds
the same log of commands and applies them in the same order, so they all compute the same state. Consensus is
agreeing on that log, one entry at a time.

**Raft** (Ongaro and Ousterhout, 2014) is the consensus algorithm behind etcd, Consul, CockroachDB and many others. It
was designed to be understood: one leader takes all the writes, and a simple majority rule makes sure nothing it has
promised is ever lost.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="leader, follower, candidate">The three roles. One leader takes client writes and sends them to the followers; a follower that stops hearing from a leader becomes a candidate and asks for votes.</Term>
  <Term name="term">A numbered period with at most one leader. Every message carries the sender's term, and a server that sees a higher term moves up to it.</Term>
  <Term name="election timeout">How long a follower waits without hearing from a leader before it starts an election. Each server picks its own at random, typically 150 to 300 ms.</Term>
  <Term name="log entry">One command, stored with the term in which a leader received it. Shown here as term:value, so 2:x is x, written in term 2.</Term>
  <Term name="majority">More than half of all the servers: 3 of 5, 2 of 3. Raft needs one to elect a leader and one to commit an entry.</Term>
  <Term name="committed">Safe for ever: stored on a majority under the rules below, so every future leader will have it. Only then is it applied and answered.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Electing a leader">

Raft divides time into **terms**, numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on. Each term starts with an election, and at most one
leader can win it. Terms work like the Lamport clocks from the logical-clocks lesson: every request and reply carries
the sender's term, and a server that sees a larger one adopts it. A leader that learns of a newer term knows it is out
of date and steps down at once.

A leader sends AppendEntries messages, empty ones as heartbeats, often enough that no follower's election timer runs
out. When one does, that follower becomes a **candidate**: it starts a new term, votes for itself and sends RequestVote
to every other server. A server grants at most **one vote per term**, so two candidates can never both reach a majority
in the same term. Because each server draws its timeout at random, one usually times out well before the rest and wins
before anyone else starts.

<Explore slug="raft" settings={{ start: 'fresh-5' }} caption="Run until a new leader is elected. S3's timer runs out first, at 154 ms: it starts term 1, and the other four vote for it. S4's vote makes 3 of 5, a majority, and S3 sends heartbeats at once so that no one else starts an election." />

<KeyIdea>

**A majority is the whole trick.** A leader needs votes from a majority, and an entry is committed once a majority
stores it. Any two majorities of the same cluster share at least one server, so every new leader's voters include
someone who holds every committed entry, and the up-to-date check below makes sure the winner holds them too.

</KeyIdea>

That overlap is also why the cluster survives failures. With $2f + 1$ servers, any $f$ can crash and the other $f + 1$
are still a majority, able to elect a leader and commit. Three servers survive one failure, five survive two.

<Explore slug="raft" settings={{ start: 'steady' }} caption="S3 leads term 1, and a, b and c are committed. Crash S3, then run until a new leader is elected: S4 times out first, wins term 2 once two of S1, S2 and S5 vote for it, and its log still holds a, b and c. Then undo back to the start and crash two followers, S1 and S2, instead: a write to S3 still commits, since three of five are up. Crash S4 as well, and the next write never commits." />

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Replicating the log, and who may win">

Once elected, the leader takes every client write. It appends the command to its own log with its current term, then
sends it to every follower in an AppendEntries message. Each message also names the entry just before the new ones, by
**index and term**. A follower accepts only if its own log has that same entry there. That one check keeps the logs in
step: if two logs hold an entry with the same index and term, they are identical up to that point.

When the check fails, the follower refuses, and the leader backs up: it moves its **nextIndex** for that follower one
entry earlier and tries again, until it finds the last entry they agree on. From there it sends everything the follower
is missing, and the follower deletes any of its own entries that conflict.

Votes have a second rule, and it is what keeps committed entries safe. A server refuses a candidate whose log is **less
up to date** than its own: compare the terms of the last entries, and if those are equal, the longer log wins. A
candidate missing a committed entry can't collect a majority, because a majority holds that entry.

<Explore slug="raft" settings={{ start: 'lagging' }} caption="The leader has crashed, and S1 missed writes b and c while it was down. Press Next event: S1 times out first and asks for votes in term 2, and S2, S4 and S5 all refuse, since its log ends at index 1 and theirs at 3. Keep going: S5 and S4 both start term 3, S5 wins, and running until every log catches up shows S5 backing its nextIndex for S1 down from 4 to 2 before sending b and c." />

A free vote is not enough: a server also checks the candidate's log. It compares the **terms** of the two logs' last
entries, and only if they are equal does it compare their lengths. A candidate whose log is behind gets refused, even by
a server that has voted for no one this term. That rule is why a new leader always holds every committed entry, and why
Raft never has to copy entries from followers back to a leader.

</Misconception>

Now break the network. A **partition** splits the servers into groups that can't reach each other. Raft promises
nothing to the side without a majority; it only promises that that side can't do damage.

<Explore slug="raft" settings={{ start: 'split' }} caption="The old leader S3 and S1 are cut off from the other three, which have elected S2 for term 2. Write to S3: it can reach only one other server, so the entry never commits and the client waits. Write to S2: three of five store it, and it commits. Then heal the network and run until every log catches up: S3 sees term 2 and steps down, and S1 and S3 drop their uncommitted entries for S2’s." />

>

It happens, briefly, and it is harmless. Each term has at most one leader, so two leaders always lead **different
terms**. The older one is just out of date: the servers that elected the newer leader are a majority, and they reject
anything from an older term, so the old leader can't commit a single entry. When it next hears from the new term, it
steps down, and its uncommitted entries are overwritten where they conflict with the new leader's log.

</Misconception>

There is one more subtlety, and it is the one most explanations get wrong. A leader counts how many servers store each
entry and marks it committed once a majority do, but only for entries from **its own current term**. An entry left by an
earlier leader is not committed just because it reaches a majority. The paper's Figure 8 shows why. A leader of term 2
gets an entry onto two of five servers and crashes. Another server is elected in term 3 and takes a different entry at
that index, then crashes in turn. The first server comes back, wins term 4 and copies its term 2 entry onto a third
server, a majority. If it counted that as committed and then crashed, the term 3 server could still win an election,
because its last entry, from term 3, is newer than theirs, and it would overwrite the "committed" entry. So older
entries commit only **indirectly**: once an entry from the leader's current term is on a majority, everything before it
in the log is committed too. Real implementations append an empty no-op entry at the start of each term to get there
quickly.

Being on a majority is necessary, not sufficient. A leader commits by counting copies only for entries from its
**current term**. An entry from an older term can sit on a majority and still be overwritten, because a server whose
last entry has a newer term can win an election without it. It becomes safe only once an entry from the current term,
later in the log, reaches a majority: then everything before it is committed too.

</Misconception>

Raft has limits worth knowing. It assumes servers crash or fall silent but never lie: a server that sends wrong
messages on purpose, a **Byzantine** fault, needs heavier protocols. And when no majority can talk to each other, Raft
stops taking writes rather than risk two answers. That is the consistency side of the CAP trade-off: during a partition
it gives up availability on the minority side to stay correct.

Fault tolerance follows the majority, not the head count. A cluster of $n$ servers needs $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor + 1$ to

writes faster: every write still needs a majority to store it.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Consensus means agreeing on one log; replicated state machines apply it in the same order on every server.
- Raft elects one leader per term with randomised timeouts and one vote per term; higher terms always win, so stale leaders step down.
- The leader replicates its log with AppendEntries, which checks the previous entry's index and term; on a mismatch it backs up nextIndex until the logs agree.
- Voters refuse candidates with less up-to-date logs, so every leader already holds every committed entry.
- An entry commits once the leader has it on a majority and it is from the leader's own term; older entries commit along with it.
- With $2f + 1$ servers Raft survives $f$ failures; a side without a majority stops rather than disagree.

</Section>
