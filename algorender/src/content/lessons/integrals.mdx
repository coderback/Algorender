<Section step="hook" title="How far did the car go?">

A car drives at a steady 20 metres per second for 5 seconds. How far does it go? Speed times time: 100 metres. On a graph
of speed against time, that is the area of a rectangle under a flat line, 5 wide and 20 tall.

Pulling away from the lights, though, the speed climbs every moment, so there is no single speed to multiply by. The
distance is still the area under the speed graph, but now its top is a curve. The trick is to cheat a little, many
times: cut the time into thin strips, pretend the speed is steady in each, and add up the rectangles. Thinner strips,
smaller cheat. The number the sums settle on is an **integral**, and machine learning is full of them: the next lesson,
**Densities and the normal distribution**, measures probabilities as areas under a curve. Along the way you will meet
the surprise at the heart of calculus: building up area is the derivative run backwards.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="strip width Δx">Cut the stretch from $a$ to $b$ into $n$ equal strips. Each is $\Delta x = (b - a)/n$ wide (Δ, "delta", means "a change in"). For time, it is written $\Delta t$.</Term>
  <Term name="Riemann sum">The rectangles added up: $\sum f(x_i)\,\Delta x$, one term per strip, where $x_i$ is the point in strip $i$ whose height you use. A **left-end** sum uses each strip's left edge, a **right-end** sum its right edge, and a **middle** sum its middle.</Term>
  <Term name="trapezoid rule">Give each strip a slanted top joining the curve's heights at its two edges, making a trapezoid (in British schools, a trapezium).</Term>
  <Term name="integral ∫">The number the sums settle on as the strips get thinner: the area under $f$ from $a$ to $b$, written $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. The ∫ is a stretched S, for sum, and $dx$ stands for the thin width.</Term>
  <Term name="signed area">Area above the x-axis counts plus, area below it counts minus. An integral adds the two up.</Term>
  <Term name="accumulated area A(x)">The signed area under $f$ from a fixed start up to $x$. As $x$ slides, $A(x)$ changes, so it is a function of its own.</Term>
  <Term name="antiderivative">A function $F$ whose derivative is $f$: $F'(x) = f(x)$. Finding one runs a derivative backwards.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Rectangles under a speed curve">

A car pulls away from the lights, and $t$ seconds later it is doing $t^2$ metres per second. After 3 seconds it is
doing 9 m/s. How far has it gone?

<Explore slug="integral" settings={{ preset: 'sums' }} caption="The speed t² from 0 to 3 seconds, cut into 2, 4, 8 and 16 strips: rectangles as tall as the speed at each strip's left end, then its right end. A second chart follows the sums. Drag b to stop the clock elsewhere." />

With 2 strips, each is $\Delta t = 3/2 = 1.5$ seconds wide. Make each rectangle as tall as the speed at its left end:
0 at the start and $1.5^2 = 2.25$ halfway. The area is $(0 + 2.25) \times 1.5 = 3.375$ metres. Too little: the
car speeds up during every strip, but each rectangle only knows the speed at its start. With 4 strips
of 0.75 seconds the left-end sum grows to 5.906, still too little but closer.

Take each height from the strip's right end instead and every rectangle is as fast as the car gets in it: 16.875 with 2
strips, 12.656 with 4. Too much. So the distance is trapped between the two sums, and the trap narrows. The two sums
use the same heights except at the ends: the right sum drops the very first height, 0, and adds the very last one, 9.
So they always differ by $(9 - 0) \times \Delta t$: 13.5, then 6.75, then 3.375, then 1.6875 as the strips go from 2
to 16. Each doubling halves the gap.

With 8 strips the sums are 7.383 and 10.758; with 16, 8.174 and 9.861. Both are closing in on **9 metres** (why exactly 9
comes later). The left-end sum misses by 5.625, 3.094, 1.617 and then 0.826: each time the
strips halve, the miss roughly halves. A miss that halves when $n$ doubles shrinks like $1/n$, and that is slow. Each
left-end rectangle misses a sliver shaped roughly like a triangle, $\tfrac{1}{2} \times \Delta t \times$ the speed's
rise across the strip, and the rises add up to $9 - 0$, so the miss is about $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 9 \times \Delta t$. With 16 strips
that is about 0.84, against the real 0.826. Doubling on, it takes 2,048 strips to miss by less than 0.01 metres.

Written as a sum, a left-end sum with $n$ strips, counting the strips $i = 0, 1, \ldots, n - 1$ like list positions, is

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} f(a + i\,\Delta x)\,\Delta x
$$

the loop from [the Σ lesson](/learn/sums-notation): a running total of heights, times the width.

<KeyIdea>

**The integral is the number the sums settle on as the strips get thinner: the area under the curve.**

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx
$$

reads "the integral of $f$ from $a$ to $b$". Under a speed graph it is the distance travelled: here
$\int_0^3 t^2\,dt = 9$ metres.

</KeyIdea>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Better heights: middles and trapezoids">

Left and right ends are the slowest and the fastest moments of each strip. Try the middle.

<Explore slug="integral" settings={{ preset: 'rules' }} caption="The same car, 0 to 3 seconds: rectangles as tall as each strip's middle, and trapezoids with slanted tops, for 2, 4, 8 and 16 strips. Drag b to change the end." />

With just 2 strips the middles are at 0.75 and 2.25 seconds, so the middle sum is
$(0.5625 + 5.0625) \times 1.5 = 8.4375$, missing 9 by only 0.5625. That already beats 16 left-end strips, which missed
by 0.826. Each middle rectangle pokes above the curve on its left half and falls below it on its right half, and the
two bits nearly cancel.

A trapezoid's area is its width times the average of its two edge heights. Every inner edge counts half in each of
the two trapezoids that share it, so in full, while the two end edges count half:
$(0/2 + 2.25 + 9/2) \times 1.5 = 10.125$. That is 1.125 too much: the curve bends up, so each straight top runs above
it.

Now double the strips, and double again:

- middles: miss 0.5625, then 0.1406, then 0.0352, then 0.0088
- trapezoids: miss 1.125, then 0.2813, then 0.0703, then 0.0176

Each doubling makes both misses 4 times smaller, where a left-end sum's only halves: these misses shrink like $1/n^2$.
Doubling until the miss is under 0.0001, the middles and the trapezoids both stop at 256 strips; the
left ends need 262,144.

On this curve the trapezoids overshoot by exactly twice what the middles fall short, so
$(2 \times \text{middles} + \text{trapezoids})/3$ cancels the two misses: with just 2 strips,
$(2 \times 8.4375 + 10.125)/3 = 9$ on the dot. That mixture is **Simpson's rule**. On most curves the two misses are
only nearly in that ratio, so it is not exact, but it misses by far less than either rule alone: it is one of the ways
computers find areas that no formula gives.

</Section>

shrinks 4 times with each doubling. In the car's case the middles went from 0.5625 to 0.1406: four times smaller, not two.

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: area that builds up">

So far the end of the area was fixed. Now let it slide. Take $f(x) = x^2 - 1$, which is below the x-axis from 0 to 1
and above it after that, and let $A(x)$ be the signed area under it from 0 up to $x$. Think of $f$ as a speed: below zero the car is reversing, which takes away from how far
forward it is. $A(0) = 0$, since there is no area yet.

Slide $x$ from 0 to 2 and watch $A(x)$ drawn on its own graph. Predict which way it goes and how fast, whether it
ends above or below zero, and then what the slope of the $A(x)$ graph has to do with $f$.

<PredictChallenge slug="integral" settings={{ preset: 'accumulate' }} goal={5} />

At first the shaded area is under the axis, so $A$ falls: $A(0.5) = -0.458$, then $A(1) = -0.667$. But the second
half-step only took away 0.208, against 0.458 for the first, because near 1 the curve is close to the axis and each bit
of width adds only a thin sliver. Past 1 the curve is above the axis, the new area counts plus, and $A$ climbs:
$A(1.5) = -0.375$, still negative but rising, and $A(2) = 0.667$. The 1.333 above the axis outweighs the 0.667 below it.
For the reversing car that means it ends up 0.667 metres ahead of where it started, after driving 2 metres in all.

So $A$ was lowest at $x = 1$, exactly where $f$ crosses the axis, and it fell fastest where $f$ was furthest below the
axis. How fast $A$ changes looks like how tall $f$ is. Test it at $x = 1.5$, where $f(1.5) = 1.5^2 - 1 = 1.25$. Moving
from 1.5 to 1.6 adds one thin strip to $A$, almost a rectangle 1.25 tall and 0.1 wide: the strip's area is 0.1403,
against the rectangle's $1.25 \times 0.1 = 0.125$. The extra is a sliver on top, where the curve rises across the
strip. Divide by the width to get how fast $A$ grew: 1.403. A strip ten times thinner, from 1.5 to 1.51, has area
0.01265, and $0.01265 / 0.01 = 1.265$. The width shrank 10 times but the sliver about 100 times, so the rate closes in
on 1.25. In general, moving from $x$ to $x + h$ adds a strip that is almost a rectangle $f(x)$ tall and $h$ wide, so

$$
\frac{A(x + h) - A(x)}{h} \approx f(x)
$$

and the left side is the secant slope from [the derivatives lesson](/learn/derivatives-gradients). As $h$ shrinks to 0
it becomes the slope of $A$, the sliver's share vanishes, and the "almost" disappears: at 1.5 the $A(x)$ graph's
slope is exactly 1.25.

<KeyIdea>

**The fundamental theorem of calculus: the slope of the accumulated area is the curve's height.**

$$
A'(x) = f(x)
$$

Accumulating area undoes the derivative. So to find an area, find a function whose derivative is $f$.

</KeyIdea>

Which function has slope $x^2 - 1$ and is 0 at 0? Run the power rule backwards. The derivative of $x^3$ is $3x^2$, three
times too much, so $x^3/3$ has slope $x^2$, and $x$ has slope 1. So $A(x) = x^3/3 - x$, the cubic whose slope the
[derivatives lesson](/learn/derivatives-gradients) worked out. Its graph runs through every area the strips measured:
$2^3/3 - 2 = 0.667$.

>

An integral adds up $f(x)\,\Delta x$ strip by strip, and where $f$ is negative those terms are negative. So area below
the axis counts minus. That is what you want for a speed: a car that reverses really does end up less far forward. If
you want the total size of the shaded region instead, add up the pieces above and below the axis as positive numbers
yourself.

</Misconception>

      answer: 2,

>

$A'(x)$ is how fast area piles up, and a thin strip at $x$ adds about $f(x)$ times its width. So the slope of $A$ is the
**height** of $f$, not its slope. Where $f$ is tallest, $A$ climbs fastest; where $f$ crosses zero, $A$ is level and has
a peak or a valley. At $x = 1.5$ above, the slope of $A$ was $f(1.5) = 1.25$, not $f'(1.5) = 2 \times 1.5 = 3$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="The power rule run backwards">

Is there a formula for $A(b)$, the area under $x^2$ from 0 to $b$? Its slope must be $b^2$, and
$b^3/3$ has slope $3b^2/3 = b^2$ and is 0 at 0. So the area under $x^2$ from 0 to $b$ is $b^3/3$, and for the car,
$3^3/3 = 9$ metres: the number every sum was closing in on.

<Explore slug="integral" settings={{ preset: 'power' }} caption="The area under x² from 0 to b = 2, checked against strips; then the end doubled, the area between 2 and 4, and the area under x³. Drag b to change the end." />

With $b = 2$ the formula gives $2^3/3 = 2.667$, and 16 middle strips give 2.6641. Double the end to 4 and the area is
$4^3/3 = 21.333$, 8 times as much: the region is twice as wide, and at every matching point the curve is 4 times as
tall, since $(2x)^2 = 4x^2$. Areas under $x^2$ grow like the cube of the end.

The area between 2 and 4 is everything up to 4 take away everything up to 2: $21.333 - 2.667 = 18.667$. But $x^3/3$
is not the only function with slope $x^2$: $x^3/3 + 5$ has the same slope everywhere, since adding 5 lifts the whole
graph without tilting it. And if two functions have the same slope everywhere, their difference has slope 0
everywhere, so it is flat: a constant. So any two antiderivatives differ by a constant, which cancels in a difference:
$(4^3/3 + 5) - (2^3/3 + 5)$ is still 18.667. It is like a car's odometer: whatever it read at the start, the distance
between two moments is the later reading take away the earlier one. So for any $F$ with $F' = f$,

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)
$$

And the power rule runs backwards for any whole-number power: raise the power by one and divide by the new power.
$x^4/4$ has slope $x^3$, so the area under $x^3$ from 0 to 2 is $2^4/4 = 4$. In general the area under $x^n$ from 0 to $b$ is
$b^{n+1}/(n + 1)$, for $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$

</Section>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- The area under a curve from $a$ to $b$ is what thin strips add up to: $\sum f(x_i)\,\Delta x$ with $\Delta x = (b - a)/n$, settling on the integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ as $n$ grows. Under a speed graph it is the distance.
- Left- and right-end sums trap the area between them and miss by an amount that halves when $n$ doubles. Middle and trapezoid sums miss 4 times less with each doubling, the trapezoids by about twice as much as the middles, on the other side.
- Area below the axis counts negative: an integral is a signed area.
- The accumulated area $A(x)$ climbs as fast as the curve is tall, $A'(x) = f(x)$: accumulating undoes the derivative. So $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$ for any $F$ with $F' = f$; under $x^n$ from 0 to $b$ that gives $b^{n+1}/(n + 1)$.

</Section>
