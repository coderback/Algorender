<Section step="hook" title="Every model is a function with knobs">

An estate agent guesses a flat's price from its size: about £3,000 a square metre, plus £20,000 on top. That guess is
a rule. Put a size in, get one price out:

$$
\text{price} = 3000 \times \text{size} + 20000
$$

A 50 square metre flat: $3000 \times 50 + 20000 = 170000$, so about £170,000.

The two numbers, 3000 and 20000, are knobs. Turn them and the rule gives different prices. Machine learning, at its
heart, is choosing the knobs from data: look at lots of real sales, then pick the numbers that make the rule's guesses
closest to the real prices. Before any of that, you need to be at ease with two things. One is rules like this, called
**functions**. The other is their pictures, called **graphs**, which show you what turning a knob does.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="function">A rule that turns each input into exactly one output. We often call it $f$ and write the output for input $x$ as $f(x)$, read "f of x".</Term>
  <Term name="table of values">A few inputs written next to their outputs: $x = 2$ gives $f(2) = 4$ for $f(x) = x^2$.</Term>
  <Term name="graph">Every point $(x, f(x))$ drawn on a grid: $x$ is how far along, $f(x)$ is how high. It is the whole table at once.</Term>
  <Term name="knob (parameter)">A number in the rule that you choose rather than put in, like the 3000 above. Changing it changes the whole graph.</Term>
  <Term name="slope">How much the output rises for each step of 1 to the right. For a straight line it is the same everywhere.</Term>
</Terms>

In this lesson every graph starts from a plain rule $f$, such as $x^2$ or $|x|$. (The bars mean the size of $x$,
ignoring its sign: $|-3| = 3$.) Three knobs, $a$, $h$ and $k$, then move it:

$$
y = a \cdot f(x - h) + k
$$

Here $y$ is the output, the height of each point, and the dot means "times". Read the rule from the inside out: take
$x$, subtract $h$, apply the plain rule $f$, multiply by $a$, then add $k$. With $f(x) = x^2$, $a = 1$, $h = 2$ and
$k = -1$ it is $y = (x - 2)^2 - 1$, the first example below. Don't worry if the formula looks dense: the visualiser
builds it one knob at a time.

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="From a rule to a picture, then three knobs">

<Explore slug="functions" settings={{ preset: 'parabola' }} caption="Fill the table, join the dots, then watch a, h and k move the graph. Each dot glides to its new place, so you can follow it." />

Step through it one frame at a time. The first frames are just arithmetic: put $x = -2$ into $x^2$ and you get $4$,
which becomes the point $(-2, 4)$. Five rows of the table give five dots. But every input in between has an output
too, so the dots join into one unbroken curve. That curve is the graph. It holds every row of the table at once,
infinitely many of them.

A function gives **one** output per input, so a vertical line can never meet its graph twice. A circle is not the graph
of a function: above most $x$ it has a point at the top and another at the bottom.

Then come the knobs, one at a time:

- **$a$ multiplies every output.** Heights double when $a = 2$ and halve when $a = 0.5$. A negative $a$ flips the
  graph upside down.
- **$h$ moves the graph sideways.** $f(x - 2)$ sits 2 to the **right** of $f(x)$, which surprises almost everyone.
- **$k$ is added to every output**, so the whole graph moves up by $k$.

<KeyIdea>

**Every point $(x, f(x))$ on the plain graph lands at $(x + h,\ a \cdot f(x) + k)$.** Outputs are stretched by $a$ and
lifted by $k$; inputs are moved by $h$. In particular the point $(0, f(0))$, which is $(0, 0)$ for all the rules here,
lands on the anchor $(h, k)$, so you can read $h$ and $k$ straight off the picture.

</KeyIdea>

Try it on the example, $y = (x - 2)^2 - 1$. The plain parabola's lowest point $(0, 0)$ lands at $(2, -1)$, and its
point $(1, 1)$ lands at $(3, 0)$. Check: $(3 - 2)^2 - 1 = 0$.

Why does $x - h$ move the graph to the right? Because the graph needs a bigger $x$ to produce the same output. With
$y = (x - 2)^2$, the output $0$, which $x^2$ gave at $x = 0$, now needs $x - 2 = 0$, that is $x = 2$. Reading from
left to right, every output arrives 2 later, so the whole curve sits 2 further right.

</Section>

<Section step="do" title="Match the curve">

Now drive the knobs yourself. This graph opens finished, with two handles on it:

- The **anchor** is where $f$'s point $(0, 0)$ landed: $(h, k)$. Moving it changes $h$ and $k$.
- The **unit point** is where $f$'s point $(1, 1)$ landed: $(h + 1, k + a)$. It stays one step right of the anchor
  and only moves up and down. Its height above the anchor is $a$.

Drag them, or focus one and use the arrow keys. The Knobs panel and the formula in the narration change as you go.

<Explore slug="functions" settings={{ preset: 'parabola', step: '9' }} input caption="Try to make y = −(x − 1)² + 4: the anchor goes to (1, 4), and the unit point one step right and one step down from it." />

Some targets to match:

- $y = -(x - 1)^2 + 4$: an upside-down parabola, its peak at $(1, 4)$. Unit point: $(2, 3)$.
- $y = 2x + 1$: dragging can't turn a parabola into a line, so change the rule itself. Type `line a=2 h=0 k=1` into
  the input box below the graph, press Run, then step to the end. Every step right climbs 2: the slope is $a$.
- $y = 0.5|x + 3|$: a wide V with its corner at $(-3, 0)$. Type `abs`, press Run and step to the end. Then place the
  anchor, and ask how far above it the unit point should sit.

Typing a new rule starts the visualiser again from the first frame. The handles appear on the last two frames.

For a straight line, $a$ is the slope: the rise for each step of 1 to the right, the same everywhere. That is exactly
the "3000 per square metre" knob of the price rule. For a curve such as $x^2$, the rise for one step depends on where
you start. Measuring how steep a curve is at each single point is what [derivatives](/learn/derivatives-gradients) do.
You will meet them later on the path.

A change **inside** the brackets moves the graph the opposite way to its sign: $f(x - 2)$ is 2 to the right and
$f(x + 2)$ is 2 to the left. Find where the inside is 0. That is where the plain graph's $x = 0$ point has gone.

</Misconception>

Outside, a number changes the **outputs**: $f(x) + 3$ is the whole graph moved up 3. Inside, it changes the **inputs**:
$f(x + 3)$ is the graph moved sideways, 3 to the left. Outside moves go the way the sign says; inside moves go the
opposite way.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A function is a rule with one output per input; its graph is every point $(x, f(x))$, the whole table at once.
- $a \cdot f(x - h) + k$: $a$ stretches (and flips when negative), $h$ moves right, $k$ moves up.
- Inside the brackets moves go the opposite way to the sign; outside moves go the way it says.
- For a straight line, $a$ is the slope, like the 3000 in the price rule.
- Choosing the knobs so a graph fits real data is what machine learning does.

</Section>
