<Section step="hook" title="Do flashcards make a difference?">

Sixteen students revise for the same test. A coin toss decides which eight use flashcards; the other eight reread
their notes. The flashcard group averages 76.5 out of 100 and the rereaders 66.5: a gap of 10 points. Time to tell everyone to use
flashcards?

Not yet. Put any sixteen people into two groups of eight and one group will come out ahead, just from who happened to
land where. A gap of 10 might be the flashcards, or it might be that luck. A **hypothesis test** asks one sharp
question: if the method made no difference at all, how often would a gap this big turn up anyway? This lesson answers
it by brute force, shuffling the group labels a thousand times, and then looks at two traps that catch scientists
every year: what the answer, p, does and doesn't mean, and what happens when you test twenty things at once.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="null hypothesis">The claim that nothing is going on: here, that the revision method makes no difference to anyone's score. A test assumes it and asks whether the data look odd under it.</Term>
  <Term name="test statistic">The single number that measures the difference you care about. Here it is $\bar{a} - \bar{b}$, the mean of group A minus the mean of group B.</Term>
  <Term name="shuffle">Pool all the scores, put them in a random order, and deal the first 8 to a new group A′ (“A prime”) and the rest to B′. It is a random permutation of the pool, as in [counting](/learn/counting-combinatorics).</Term>
  <Term name="null distribution">The pile of differences the shuffles give: what the test statistic looks like when the labels mean nothing.</Term>
  <Term name="two-sided">Counting a shuffle as extreme when its difference is far from 0 on either side, because the question is whether the groups differ at all, in either direction.</Term>
  <Term name="p-value">The share of shuffles whose difference is at least as far from 0 as the real one, on either side, ties included. It is the chance of data at least this extreme if the null hypothesis is true.</Term>
  <Term name="threshold">A cut-off chosen in advance, usually 0.05. A p at or below it is called **statistically significant**.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="Shuffle the labels">

Suppose the null hypothesis is true. Then every student would have scored exactly the same whichever group they were
in, so the labels A and B carry no information: they are stickers, and the coin toss handed them out at random. The
real split is just one of all the ways to choose which 8 of the 16 scores wear the A sticker. That is a choice of 8 from 16, and as in
[counting](/learn/counting-combinatorics) there are

$$
\binom{16}{8} = 12{,}870
$$

of them. The null hypothesis and the random dealing together make every one of them as likely as the real split. So
the test is: deal the stickers out again at random, many times, and see how often a split looks as lopsided as the
real one. The coin toss matters for what a small p means, too: because chance alone decided who used flashcards,
keen or able students are spread across both groups, so a gap that chance can't explain points to the method. Had the
students chosen their own method, the keen ones might all have picked flashcards, and no shuffle could tell.

<Explore slug="permutation-test" settings={{ preset: 'shuffle', seed: 7 }} caption="Left: the 16 scores, coloured by group, with each group's mean; on a shuffle every score glides to its new group. Right: the difference of each shuffle piling up, the real difference as a solid line, and the extreme shuffles shaded." />

In this run the first shuffle happens to give two groups with the same mean, 71.5 each, so a difference of 0. The
first ten shuffles give 0, +4.5, +2, +0.5, +2, +8.25, +2, +2.5, −5.5 and −9.25. None of them is the real +10, and
they wander on both sides of 0. That is chance alone at work on these very scores.

After 1,000 shuffles the pile is a hump centred on −0.09. With the labels dealt at random, A′ is as likely to come
out ahead as behind, so the differences balance around 0, and 694 of the 1,000 lie between −5 and +5. The pile's
standard deviation is 4.88: it plays the role of a [standard error](/learn/estimation-confidence) for the difference
when nothing is going on, found by shuffling instead of by formula.

As a rough check, the real difference of 10 sits about 10 ÷ 4.88 ≈ 2.05 spreads from the middle. The pile is close to
a bell, as [the central limit theorem](/learn/central-limit-theorem) leads you to expect of a difference of averages,
and a bell keeps only about 5% of its area more than 2 spreads out, by the 95% rule of
[densities](/learn/continuous-distributions). So expect only a few shuffles in 100 to be as extreme as the real split.

Which shuffles count as at least as extreme as +10? The question, fixed before looking at the scores, was whether the
methods make a difference in either direction, so both tails count. Choosing one side after seeing which way the gap
went would count only one tail, roughly halving p and making the gap look more surprising than it is. Here 22 shuffles reached +10 or more and 20 reached −10 or less. Ties count too: a shuffle exactly 10
from 0 is at least as extreme. That makes 42 of the 1,000, so

$$
p = \frac{42}{1{,}000} = 0.042
$$

If the method made no difference, a split as lopsided as the real one would turn up about 4 times in 100. That is at
or below the usual threshold of 0.05, so the gap is called statistically significant: hard to put down to chance
alone.

Shuffles are random, so p from 1,000 of them wobbles, like any average in
[the law of large numbers](/learn/law-of-large-numbers): by about $\sqrt{p(1 - p)/N} \approx 0.0063$ here. Four times
the shuffles halve the wobble. But there are only 12,870 splits, so a computer can simply count them all: 488 are at
least as extreme, and the exact p is $488 / 12{,}870 \approx 0.0379$. The shuffles' 0.042 was close.

<KeyIdea>

**A permutation test:** assume the groups make no difference (the null hypothesis), so the labels are arbitrary.
Shuffle them many times and record the difference each shuffle gives. Then

$$
p = \frac{\text{shuffles at least as extreme}}{\text{shuffles}}
$$

where “at least as extreme” means at least as far from 0 as the real difference, on either side. A small p says chance
alone rarely produces a gap this big.

</KeyIdea>

Read the definition again: every shuffle was made **assuming** no difference. So p is a statement about the data under
that assumption, not about the assumption itself.

<Misconception
  id="p-is-chance-null-true"

    {
      q: 'You toss a coin many times to test whether it is fair. Which of these is p?',
      choices: ['The chance of results at least this lopsided, if the coin is fair', 'The chance the coin is fair, given these results'],
      answer: 0,
      trap: 1,

>

p answers “if nothing were going on, how often would data at least this extreme turn up?” It does **not** answer
“how likely is it that nothing is going on?” Those are the two directions of a conditional probability, and as in
[Bayes' rule](/learn/bayes), $P(\text{this extreme or more} \mid \text{no effect})$ can be small while
$P(\text{no effect} \mid \text{data})$ is not, for example when real effects are rare to begin with. The flashcards' p = 0.042 is not a 4.2% chance that they
don't work.

</Misconception>

<Section step="predict" title="When nothing is going on">

What does a permutation test do when the null hypothesis is really true? Here both groups are drawn from one
population, a bell curve of scores with mean 65 and spread 10, and the labels are handed out at random, so any gap is
luck by construction. Predict how often p comes out at or below 0.05 in experiments like this, what shape the p-values of 200 such
experiments pile into, what share of them a threshold of 0.05 calls significant, which is more likely for one more
experiment, and what changes with a stricter threshold.

<PredictChallenge slug="permutation-test" settings={{ preset: 'no-effect', seed: 9 }} goal={5} />

In this run the first experiment gives p = 0.779: nothing to see, as it should be. Across all 200 experiments the
p-values spread out roughly evenly from 0 to 1, about 10 in every bar of width 0.05. Of the 200, 11 came out at or below 0.05,
close to 1 in 20, and 94 came out above 0.5. With a threshold of 0.01, 2 of the 200 still came out at or below it, 1 in
100.

Why flat? When the null hypothesis is true, the real split is just one more random split, so its difference is no
more extreme than a typical shuffle's. Where it ranks among the shuffles is roughly equally likely to be anywhere
(whole-number scores and ties make it only roughly so), and p is that rank as a share. So a threshold of 0.05 lets
through about 1 in 20 of the experiments where nothing is going on. That is exactly what the threshold means: of the
experiments where nothing is going on, the share you agree to call significant. It is **not** the share of significant
results that are false alarms; that depends on how many real effects there are among the things tested, as in
[Bayes' rule](/learn/bayes).

The same flat pile says something about big p-values. A large p is what no effect usually looks like, but it is also
what a small real effect often looks like. In the first explorer, step to the last frame and drag group A's mean down
5 points (one point per arrow press), so the gap is 5 instead of 10: the exact p becomes 0.271. Then imagine
flashcards really are worth 5 points. In 400 simulated experiments, each drawing 16 scores from the bell curve above
(mean 65, spread 10) and adding 5 points to every A score, with 8 students a group, p came out above 0.05 in 329 of
them. With groups this
small, the test usually misses a real effect of that size.

      answer: 0,

>

A p above the threshold means the data don't rule out chance alone. It does not mean chance alone is the answer.
Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence: with 8 students a group, a real 5-point advantage gave p above 0.05 in
329 of 400 experiments. To show that a difference is small, report how big it could be, with an interval as in
[standard errors and confidence intervals](/learn/estimation-confidence), not a large p.

</Misconception>

<Section step="do" title="A permutation test in code">

The exercise builds the whole test. Its shuffles come from the Park–Miller generator of
[the law of large numbers](/learn/law-of-large-numbers): each step multiplies the state by 16,807 and keeps the
remainder after dividing by 2,147,483,647, and the state divided by 2,147,483,647 is a number $u$ between 0 and 1.

The shuffle is the **Fisher–Yates** shuffle. Walk $i$ from the last place of the pool down to 1. At each place, pick
$j = \lfloor u \cdot (i + 1) \rfloor$ ($u \cdot (i + 1)$ rounded down), a place from 0 to $i$, each equally likely, and swap places $i$ and $j$. Place
$i$ is then settled and never touched again. The $+1$ matters: it lets $j$ equal $i$, so a score can stay where it is.
With $j = \lfloor u \cdot i \rfloor$ instead, no score could ever stay put, and some orders could never come up.

After the swaps, the first 8 of the pool are A′ and the rest B′. Record $\bar{a}' - \bar{b}'$, count it if its size,
ignoring the sign, is at least the real difference's size, and keep shuffling the same pool. Compare means, not totals: when the groups
differ in size, the bigger group's total is bigger just for having more scores.

These are exactly the visualiser's shuffles. Your code on the flashcard scores with 1,000 shuffles from seed 7 makes
the same 1,000 differences and returns the same p = 0.042 as the run above.

<Section step="see" title="Twenty tests at once">

When nothing is going on, a threshold of 0.05 calls about 1 test in 20 significant. Now test twenty things at once. Here
twenty study tricks are each tested with 8 students against 8, and none of them works: every score comes from the same
population. The p-values are on a log scale, as in [exponents and logs](/learn/exponents-logs), so the small ones are
easy to tell apart.

<Explore slug="permutation-test" settings={{ preset: 'many', seed: 4 }} caption="Twenty no-effect tests, each a permutation test with 1,000 shuffles, their p-values on a log scale; a test is marked at p ≤ 0.05, then at a stricter, corrected threshold." />

In this round, test 7 lights up, with p = 0.012. Reported on its own, that looks like a discovery: a study trick that
works, significant at the 0.05 level. But nothing works here. Expect about 1 of 20 no-effect tests to light up, since
20 × 0.05 = 1.

How likely is it that at least one lights up? Each test stays dark with chance 0.95, and the tests are independent, so
all twenty stay dark with chance $0.95^{20} \approx 0.36$, as in
[chance and independence](/learn/probability-basics). At least one lights up with chance

$$
1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 0.64
$$

In 100 more rounds of twenty, 64 lit at least one test. Run enough tests and something usually turns up.

The fix is to hold each test to a stricter threshold. Dividing by the number of tests, $0.05 / 20 = 0.0025$
(Bonferroni's correction), brings the chance of any false alarm in the round back to $1 - 0.9975^{20} \approx 0.049$,
about 5%. In this round, no test reaches 0.0025: test 7's 0.012 is not enough once twenty tests have been run. The price
is that a real but modest effect now needs a much smaller p to be noticed.

Every test of nothing has a 1 in 20 chance of a false alarm at 0.05, so twenty tests of nothing light up about one, and at least
one with chance 0.64. A single lit test among many is what chance alone looks like. Count how many things were tested,
and either divide the threshold by that number or test the one that lit up again on fresh data.

</Misconception>

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- A hypothesis test asks whether chance alone could explain a difference: assume nothing is going on (the null hypothesis) and see how surprising the data are.
- A permutation test makes chance alone concrete: shuffle the group labels many times, and the pile of shuffled differences is the null distribution.
- p is the share of shuffles at least as extreme as the real difference, on both sides, ties included. A small p means chance alone rarely gives a gap this big.
- p is the chance of data at least this extreme if nothing is going on, never the chance that nothing is going on; and a large p doesn't prove there is no difference.
- With nothing going on, p is roughly equally likely to land anywhere from 0 to 1, so 0.05 calls about 1 in 20 of those experiments significant. Twenty tests usually light one up; divide the threshold by the number of tests.

</Section>
