<Section step="hook" title="Zoom in until it's a matrix">

A layer of a neural network takes in a list of numbers and gives back another list. If the layer were just a matrix, it
would be easy to reason about. From the lesson on matrices, you know a matrix moves the whole plane in one go, and
straight lines stay straight. Real layers bend things, with tanh, squares and products. Yet backpropagation passes
gradients back through them using nothing but multiplications, as if every layer were a matrix.

It can do that because of a fact you already know for curves. Close up, a curve looks like its tangent line:
$f(a + h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,h$. In the same way, a **smooth** map (one with no corners, like $|x|$ had) with two
inputs and two outputs looks like a **matrix** when you zoom in close. That matrix is its **Jacobian**. In this lesson
you zoom in until you can see that matrix, read its columns and its determinant, and then see what backprop really does
with it.

</Section>

<Section step="parts" title="The pieces you will see">

<Terms>
  <Term name="a map of the plane F">A rule that sends each point $(x, y)$ to a new point $F(x, y) = (F_1, F_2)$. It has two inputs, and two outputs $F_1$ and $F_2$, each a function of both inputs.</Term>
  <Term name="Jacobian J">The grid of all of $F$'s partial derivatives at a point $p$. Call the inputs $x_1 = x$ and $x_2 = y$. Row $i$ belongs to output $F_i$ and column $j$ to input $x_j$, and the entry where they meet is $\partial F_i / \partial x_j$.</Term>
  <Term name="local linear map">$F(p + d) \approx F(p) + J\,d$ for a small step $d$ from $p$. It is the best matrix guess near $p$, just as the tangent line is the best straight-line guess near a point of a curve.</Term>
  <Term name="det J">The Jacobian's determinant: how much $F$ scales tiny areas near $p$.</Term>
  <Term name="row vector">A vector written lying down in square brackets, like $[1, 2]$. Gradients are written this way here, so they can multiply a matrix from the left.</Term>
  <Term name="vector–Jacobian product">A row $g$ times a Jacobian, written $g\,J$. Entry $j$ is $g$ dotted with column $j$ of $J$. This is one step of backprop.</Term>
</Terms>

</Section>

<Section step="see" title="A bent plane, close up">

Here is a map that bends the plane:

$$
F(x, y) = \left(x + \frac{y^2}{4},\ y - \frac{x^2}{4}\right)
$$

The first output is $x$, pushed along by a quarter of $y^2$. The second is $y$, pulled down by a quarter of $x^2$.
Take the point $p = (1, 1)$. Then $F(p) = (1 + \tfrac14,\ 1 - \tfrac14) = (1.25, 0.75)$. Is there one matrix that does
what $F$ does everywhere? Step through to find out, then zoom in on a small square with one corner at $p$, and compare
what $F$ does to it with what a matrix would do.

<Explore slug="jacobian" settings={{ preset: 'zoom' }} input caption="The grid and where F sends it. Then a square at p, shrinking from side 0.8, with its curved image and, magnified beside it, a dashed parallelogram drawn over the image. Drag p." />

A matrix keeps lines straight and evenly spaced, and $F$ bends them, so no single matrix fits everywhere. Near $p$,
though, one matrix does fit. Its entries are $F$'s partial derivatives, each found with the other input held fixed:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial x} &= 1, & \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y} &= \frac{y}{2}, \\[4pt]
\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x} &= -\frac{x}{2}, & \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial y} &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Put them in a grid with one row for each output and one column for each input. Then fill in $p = (1, 1)$:

$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac{y}{2} \\[4pt] -\frac{x}{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ -0.5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The dashed parallelogram shows what $J$ does to the square: $F(p) + J\,d$ for every step $d$ from $p$ to a point of the
square. The filled patch shows what $F$ really does. For the square of side 0.8, the two outlines are up to 0.226 apart.
Halve the square to side 0.4 and the biggest gap drops to 0.057, about a quarter of what it was. At side 0.2 it is
0.014. Compared with the side of the square, the gap goes from 0.283 to 0.141, then 0.071, then 0.035, halving each
time. When the picture is magnified to the same size on screen, the patch and the parallelogram become one shape.

Why a quarter? Write the step from $p = (x, y)$ as $(a, b)$ and multiply out the squares. Every term lands in one of
three places:

$$
F(p + (a, b)) = F(p) + J \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} b^2/4 \\ -a^2/4 \end{bmatrix}
$$

This holds exactly, for any step. The gap between $F$ and the matrix guess is the last part. Halve the step, and $a$
and $b$ halve, but $a^2/4$ and $b^2/4$ drop to a quarter.

<KeyIdea>

**Zoom in on a smooth map and it looks like a matrix: its Jacobian $J$.** Near $p$,
$F(p + d) \approx F(p) + J\,d$, the tangent line in two dimensions. Row $i$ of $J$ belongs to output $i$, column $j$
to input $j$, and the entry where they meet is $\partial F_i / \partial x_j$.

</KeyIdea>

In the lesson on matrices, column 1 of a matrix was where $\hat\imath$ lands. For $J$, column 1 is where a tiny step
along $x$ goes, for each unit of step. Watch the columns being measured, then see what $J$ does to area.

<Explore slug="jacobian" settings={{ preset: 'columns' }} input caption="Through p, a curve with y held fixed and a curve with x held fixed. Then a step along x, J measured by nudging, a little square for det J, and a second point, q. Drag p." />

Column 1 of $J$ is $(1, -0.5)$, the partial derivatives of both outputs with respect to $x$. Step from $p$ along $x$ by
a tiny $h$, and $F(p)$ moves by about $h \times (1, -0.5)$, along the curve where $y$ is held fixed. A real step of
$h = 0.5$ gives $(1, -0.625)$ for each unit of step. That is close to column 1, but by then the curve has bent away from
its tangent. Step both ways and divide by $2h$, the central difference from the derivatives lesson. With $h = 0.01$ it
gives column 1 as $(1, -0.5)$ and column 2, from a step along $y$, as $(0.5, 1)$, exactly: for a map built from
squares, the central difference has no error.

The determinant is $1 \times 1 - 0.5 \times (-0.5) = 1.25$. A matrix's determinant is how much it scales area, so near
$p$, $F$ scales areas by about 1.25. A square of side 0.25 at $p$ comes out with 1.316 times its area, and smaller
squares come out closer and closer to 1.25 times. At any point,

$$
\det J = 1 \cdot 1 - \frac{y}{2} \cdot \left(-\frac{x}{2}\right) = 1 + \frac{xy}{4}
$$

so it changes as $p$ moves. At the origin, $J$ is the identity matrix and $\det J = 1$. At $q = (2, -2)$,
$\det J = 0$:

$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Its columns $(1, -1)$ and $(-1, 1)$ lie on one line, so tiny squares near $q$ are squashed flat.

It is easy to stack the partial derivatives the other way round, with one row for each input. That gives a different
matrix. At $p = (1, 1)$ it would be $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, which sends a step along $x$
towards $(1, 0.5)$ instead of $(1, -0.5)$. The rule comes from what $J$ is for: $J$ times a step must give the change in
the outputs. Each row, dotted with the step, gives one output's change, so a map with $n$ inputs and $m$ outputs has
$m$ rows and $n$ columns.

</Misconception>

>

A matrix fits $F$ only near one point. $J$ is a grid of formulas, and it only becomes a grid of numbers once you pick
the point. For the bent plane, $\det J = 1 + xy/4$: it is 1 at the origin, 1.25 at $p = (1, 1)$ and 0 at $q = (2, -2)$.
A linear map (or one plus a fixed shift) is the exception. Its Jacobian is its own matrix at every point, as the last
example in the exercise below shows.

</Misconception>

<Section step="predict" title="Your turn: backprop through a chain of maps">

Now put maps in a row, as a network does. Start at a point $x$ of the plane. Here $x$ names a whole point
$(x_1, x_2)$, as networks name their input. The bend gives $u = F(x)$. A linear layer with matrix

$$
W = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

gives $v = W u$. Last, a loss measures how far $v$ is from a target $t$:

$$
L = \tfrac12\left((v_1 - t_1)^2 + (v_2 - t_2)^2\right)
$$

Backprop needs $\partial L/\partial x$: how fast $L$ changes as each input moves. In a real network, $x$ is the output
of earlier layers, so this row is what gets passed further back, towards their weights. To get it, follow a tiny step
$d$ from $x$ along the chain. First $u$ moves by about $J_F\,d$. $W$ is a matrix, so its Jacobian is $W$ itself
everywhere, and $v$ moves by $W\,(J_F\,d)$. $L$ has two inputs and one output, so its Jacobian is a single row,
$\left[\partial L/\partial v_1,\ \partial L/\partial v_2\right]$. Only the first square holds $v_1$, and by the
derivative rules the slope of $\tfrac12(v_1 - t_1)^2$ in $v_1$ is $v_1 - t_1$. So that row is
$[v_1 - t_1,\ v_2 - t_2]$, and $L$ changes by about $(\partial L/\partial v)\,W\,J_F\,d$. The row that multiplies the
step $d$ is $\partial L/\partial x$:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial v}\, W\, J_F
$$

This is the chain rule from the backpropagation lesson, with each local derivative now a matrix. The Jacobian of a
chain of maps is the product of their Jacobians, with the loss's on the left and the first map's on the right. In a
product of matrices the brackets can go anywhere, $(AB)C = A(BC)$, and the answer comes out the same. The amount of
work does not. In the challenge you work out the backward pass, then pick the cheaper order, then check the answer by
nudging $x$.

<PredictChallenge slug="jacobian" settings={{ preset: 'chain' }} goal={4} />

At $x = (2, 2)$, the forward pass gives $u = (3, 1)$ and $v = (3, 4)$, and with $t = (2, 2)$ the loss is 2.5. Going
backwards, $\partial L/\partial v = [1, 2]$. Times $W$, one column at a time, that becomes $[3, 2]$. Times
$J_F(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, it gives $\partial L/\partial x = [1, 5]$. To check, nudge each
coordinate of $x$ by ±0.001 and work out $L$ again: you get the same $[1, 5]$.

Each step was a row times a matrix: a **vector–Jacobian product**. With two numbers in each layer, a row times a
$2 \times 2$ matrix takes $2^2 = 4$ multiplications, so the two steps take 8. Multiplying $W J_F$ first takes
$2^3 = 8$, and then the row times the result takes another 4, so 12 in all. The gap grows fast. For layers 1000 numbers
wide, a row at a time takes 2,000,000 multiplications, and building the product first takes 1,001,000,000, about 500
times as many. That is why backprop starts at the loss and carries one row backwards, and never builds a product of
Jacobians.

The chain rule in matrix form looks like an instruction to multiply the Jacobians, and the answer would be right. But a
matrix times a matrix costs $n^3$ multiplications, and a row times a matrix only $n^2$. Backprop starts from the one row
it has, $\partial L/\partial(\text{output})$, and multiplies it by one Jacobian at a time, so it never needs a product
of Jacobians. Each box in the backpropagation lesson's graph did exactly this, with numbers in place of matrices.

</Misconception>

<Section step="do" title="Another map, then measure a Jacobian in code">

One more map: **polar coordinates**. To find a point, go out a distance $r$ from the origin, turned $\theta$ radians
anticlockwise from the $x$-axis. On the [unit circle](/learn/trigonometry), turning by $\theta$ landed at
$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, so $r$ times further out the point is

$$
F(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)
$$

The left plane holds the pairs $(r, \theta)$. Holding $r$ fixed draws a circle, and holding $\theta$ fixed draws a ray
out from the origin. The point $p = (2, 0.5)$ means 2 out, turned 0.5 radians (about 29°). The same steps as before run
on this map. Drag $p$ inwards, towards smaller $r$.

<Explore slug="jacobian" settings={{ preset: 'polar' }} input caption="Polar coordinates at p = (2, 0.5): a curve with θ held fixed, a curve with r held fixed, J's columns and det J. Drag p." />

Holding $\theta$ fixed, both outputs are $r$ times a number, so column 1 is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. For column 2
you need the slopes of cos and sin. Turning a point a little round the unit circle moves it at right angles to its
radius, so the slope of $\cos\theta$ is $-\sin\theta$ and the slope of $\sin\theta$ is $\cos\theta$. Check either with
a central difference: $(\sin 0.51 - \sin 0.49) / 0.02 \approx 0.878$, and $\cos 0.5 \approx 0.878$ too. So

$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
$$

Top-left times bottom-right, minus top-right times bottom-left:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det J &= r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta \\
&= r\,(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = r
\end{aligned}
$$

At $p = (2, 0.5)$ that is 2, so tiny squares there come out with twice their area. At $r = 0$, every angle lands on the
origin, and $\det J = 0$.

Now measure a Jacobian yourself. Write `jacobian(name, x, y, h)`. To get column 0, nudge $x$ alone by $h$ each way and
take central differences of both outputs. Do the same with $y$ for column 1. Write the four numbers into a $2 \times 2$
table, with row $i$ for output $i$. In code, rows and columns count from 0.

<Section step="recap" title="What to remember">

- Zoom in on a smooth map and it looks like a matrix, its Jacobian: $F(p + d) \approx F(p) + J\,d$ for a small step $d$.
- $J$ has a row for each output and a column for each input, and its entries are $\partial F_i / \partial x_j$. Column $j$ is where a tiny step along input $j$ goes.
- $J$ depends on the point, and so does $\det J$, which is how much $F$ scales tiny areas there. Where $\det J = 0$, tiny squares are squashed flat.
- For a chain of maps the Jacobians multiply: the chain rule in matrix form.
- Backprop carries one row backwards, a vector–Jacobian product at each step, at $n^2$ multiplications a step instead of $n^3$.

</Section>
